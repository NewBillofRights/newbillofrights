# Pardon Reform Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

This document supports **Target Category 8 (Limited Presidential Pardon Reform)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md) and is a companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). The framing decision made at the category level governs everything below: this is **transparency-and-public-benefit-led reform**, not a self-pardon-ban-led amendment. A self-pardon ban polls as an anti-Trump measure and collapses inside the incumbent's party; a "no president of either party may use mercy as personal property" frame does not. The abuse record here is deliberately balanced — Clinton, Trump (both terms), and Biden — because the amendment's only viable pitch is that it binds **all future presidents**, including ones the reader likes.

**The headline findings:**

1. **The durable bipartisan core is narrow but real: family/self-benefit bans plus transparency.** Banning pardons that benefit the president's family polls at 77% (64R/88D) and pardons of staff/associates at 69% (50R/84D) in [Protect Democracy's 2024 polling](https://protectdemocracy.org/) *(advocacy source — pro-reform organization; numbers should be independently replicated before use in materials)*, while a generic "limit the pardon power" amendment gets only 56% (34R/76D, Dec 2025) because it reads as aimed at the sitting president. See [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md).
2. **Both parties' recent pardons were unpopular with the public — including each party's own voters.** The Hunter Biden pardon drew ~32% overall approval; Trump's associate pardons ~29%; pardons of violent January 6 offenders drew 83% disapproval ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). The public is already where the amendment needs it to be; elites are the lagging indicator.
3. **A public-justification requirement has never been directly polled.** This is the single most important polling gap for the foundation to fill — it is the least "aimed at anyone" provision in the package and plausibly the highest-consensus one.
4. **The US is the comparative outlier.** No peer democracy gives one person unreviewable, uncounseled, unexplained clemency power. Germany and Italy require ministerial countersignature; India and South Africa subject clemency to judicial review for bad faith; the UK routes mercy through a cabinet minister answerable to Parliament; France abolished collective pardons in 2008. State governors are even more constrained: Georgia's governor has **no** pardon power at all.
5. **The strongest evidence base is the states.** Roughly half of US states condition or remove the governor's clemency power — board-exclusive (Georgia, Idaho, Utah, Nebraska), board-recommendation-gated (Pennsylvania, Texas, Delaware, Florida), or transparency-mandated (New York, California require every act reported to the legislature with reasons). The board-gated states are conspicuously absent from the pardon-scandal record; the scandal states (Tennessee 1979, Kentucky 2019) are governor-alone states.
6. **Two traps can kill this amendment: incumbent-coding and mercy-destruction.** Any draft that reads as targeting the current president loses ~40 points of his party (34R on the generic amendment). And any draft that proceduralizes clemency into paralysis worsens the existing clemency *drought* for ordinary applicants — only 11% of first-term Trump grants went through the Pardon Attorney, but the deeper scandal is the [thousands of ordinary petitions that sit unanswered for years](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-statistics).

## Amendment Framework

### Core provisions

The research below supports a package built in this order (most consensual first):

1. **Transparency / public justification.** Every act of clemency must be published — recipient, offense, and the president's written justification — and transmitted to Congress. Modeled directly on [California Const. art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V) and [New York Const. art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution), which have required exactly this of governors for over a century without impairing mercy.
2. **Categorical self-benefit exclusions.** No pardon of the president by himself or herself; no pardon of the president's spouse or close relatives; no pardon granted in exchange for anything of value (with express confirmation that the bribery laws reach the pardon power — codifying what [DOJ already treated as prosecutable in its 2020 pardon-bribery investigation](https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/01/politics/pardon-investigation-doj/index.html)).
3. **Individual-naming requirement.** Clemency must name the person and specify the offenses covered — no blanket categorical pardons of unnamed persons, and no open-ended preemptive pardons for "any offenses" over a decade. This targets the Hunter Biden 2014–2024 blanket, the Biden preemptive pardons, and the January 6 mass grant symmetrically.
4. **(Harder tier) Coordination exclusion.** No pardon for offenses committed at the direction of, or in coordination with, the president — the Cohen-amendment core ([H.J.Res.13, 119th Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13)).
5. **(Optional, historically grounded) Congressional disapproval.** Mondale's 1974 mechanism: a supermajority of both houses may void a pardon within a fixed window ([S.J.Res.241, 93rd Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241)).

### Challenges

- **The doctrine is maximalist.** [*Ex parte Garland* (1866)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/71/333/) calls the power "unlimited," reaching "every offence known to the law," and holds it "cannot be fettered by any legislative restrictions"; [*Schick v. Reed* (1974)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/419/256/) confirms it "cannot be modified, abridged, or diminished by the Congress." Statutory reform of the power's *scope* is therefore constitutionally dead on arrival — which is precisely the argument for an amendment, but also means there is no incremental statutory proof-of-concept for the exclusions (transparency and bribery statutes are the exceptions; see **Inherently statutory**).
- **Every federal proposal since 1974 has died without a floor vote** (Mondale's included), and the one bill that did pass a chamber — the [Protecting Our Democracy Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/5314) — passed the House 220-208 with a single Republican, proving how easily pardon reform gets absorbed into a partisan omnibus.
- **Line-drawing on "family" and "associates"** is genuinely hard (degrees of kinship; who counts as a campaign employee), and the "coordination" and "corrupt purpose" provisions raise justiciability problems (who decides a pardon was corrupt, and when?).
- **The mercy function is already broken in the other direction.** Clemency for ordinary applicants is in a decades-long drought; reform that adds friction without fixing supply will be attacked — fairly — by criminal-justice reformers on the left and right (the [Barkow/Osler critique](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2479595)).

### Strategic read

Lead with **transparency and the public-benefit principle**, framed as *"the pardon belongs to the public, not the president's family and donors."* Every messaging artifact should pair one Democratic and one Republican abuse in the same sentence (Marc Rich + Charles Kushner; Hunter Biden + January 6). The self-pardon ban rides *inside* the self-benefit provision rather than leading the package — it is the same principle (no one may be the judge and the beneficiary of his own mercy, per the [1974 OLC memo](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president)) but framed as anti-corruption rather than anti-Trump. The state models are the persuasion centerpiece for Republican audiences: red-state constitutions (Georgia, Texas, Utah, Idaho, Nebraska) are the *most* restrictive in the country, so conditioning clemency is a conservative governance tradition, not a progressive innovation. Commission the missing poll on public-justification first.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

### Constitutional doctrine — what the power is, and what it already is not

Article II, §2, cl. 1: the President "shall have Power to grant Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment."

- **Scope near-plenary.** [*Ex parte Garland* (1866)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/71/333/): the power "extends to every offence known to the law, and may be exercised at any time after its commission, either before legal proceedings are taken or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment" and "is not subject to legislative control." [*United States v. Klein* (1871)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/80/128/): Congress cannot direct courts to treat pardons as inoperative or as proof of guilt.
- **Conditions are permitted.** [*Schick v. Reed* (1974)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/419/256/): the president may attach conditions (there, commuting a death sentence to life without parole); the power "flows from the Constitution alone." Conditional clemency is thus already constitutional practice — relevant precedent for an amendment that itself conditions the power.
- **Acceptance may be refused; "acceptance implies guilt" is dictum.** [*Burdick v. United States* (1915)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/236/79/): a pardon may be refused, and its dictum that acceptance "carries an imputation of guilt; acceptance a confession of it" has rhetorical but not legal force (*Lorance v. Commandant*, 10th Cir. 2021: acceptance "does not have the legal effect of a confession of guilt"; *United States v. Schaffer*, D.C. Cir. en banc 2001: acceptance only "may imply" a confession). Do not build public messaging on "acceptance = confession." [corrected 2026-08-18; was: presented as doctrine; "Useful framing: preemptive pardons brand their recipients"]
- **Existing hard limits.** Federal offenses only — pardons cannot reach **state crimes** (Bannon and Manafort both faced New York prosecution after federal pardons) — and cannot be used "in Cases of Impeachment."
- **The open self-pardon question.** Never tested. The Office of Legal Counsel's [August 5, 1974 memorandum](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president) (Mary Lawton, days before Nixon's resignation): "Under the fundamental rule that no one may be a judge in his own case, the President cannot pardon himself." An OLC memo binds no court; an amendment would settle it.
- **Bribery-for-pardon is already prosecutable.** DOJ has treated selling a pardon as bribery of an official act — a [2020 unsealed court filing revealed an active DOJ investigation into a pardon-for-money scheme](https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/01/politics/pardon-investigation-doj/index.html), and the Clinton-era Rich pardon drew a federal criminal investigation. The amendment's quid-pro-quo provision constitutionalizes and clarifies this rather than inventing it — important for answering "isn't this already illegal?" After *Trump v. United States* (2024), a prosecution of the President for a bribed pardon may use the public record and evidence of the quid but not the President's private records or advisers' testimony probing the official act itself; a prosecution of the *recipient* or *broker* is unaffected. [corrected 2026-08-18; was: no mention of the 2024 evidentiary limits]

### The Abuse Record (both parties — this balance is the argument)

**Clinton (2001) — the paradigmatic sale-adjacent pardon.** On his final morning, Clinton pardoned fugitive financier **Marc Rich**, whose ex-wife Denise Rich had given [over $1 million to Democratic causes and $450,000 to the Clinton presidential library](https://www.propublica.org/article/questions-for-potential-ag-holder-on-clinton-pardon-1202). Clinton took the extraordinary step of defending himself in a [New York Times op-ed, "My Reasons for the Pardons" (Feb. 18, 2001)](https://abcnews.go.com/Politics/story?id=121773&page=1), insisting "there was no quid pro quo"; federal prosecutors in New York nonetheless [opened a criminal investigation](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/clinton-pardon-under-fire/) (closed without charges). The Rich pardon bypassed the Justice Department entirely and remains the template for why *process transparency* — not just outcome bans — matters.

**Trump, first term (2017–21) — associates and family-adjacent grants.** Commutation and then pardon of **Roger Stone** (convicted of lying to Congress in the Russia investigation), pardons of **Paul Manafort** and **Michael Flynn** (both convicted in matters touching the president's own campaign and administration), **Steve Bannon** (pardoned mid-prosecution for defrauding Trump's own donors in "We Build the Wall"), and **Charles Kushner** (his son-in-law's father, later appointed ambassador to France). Per [Lawfare's analysis of DOJ records](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/trump-and-pardon-attorney), only **25 of 238 first-term grants (11%) came through the Pardon Attorney** — a historic low that turned the end-run into the norm.

**Biden (2024–25) — the family precedent crosses party lines.** On December 1, 2024, after repeated public pledges not to, Biden granted **Hunter Biden** a "full and unconditional" pardon covering *any* federal offenses committed **2014–2024** — a decade-wide blanket, approved by only ~32% of the public ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). In his final hours (January 2025), Biden issued **preemptive pardons** to five family members (his brothers, sister, and their spouses) plus Anthony Fauci, Gen. Mark Milley, and January 6 committee members — pardons for people charged with nothing, criticized across the spectrum, including by Democratic senators, as normalizing exactly the tool a future president could abuse. These grants are what make a family-pardon ban a *both-parties* provision rather than an anti-Trump one.

**Trump, second term (2025–) — mass, transactional, and personal-benefit-adjacent.** On January 20, 2025, blanket clemency for **~1,500 January 6 defendants**, including those convicted of assaulting police — the 83%-disapproval grant ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). October 23, 2025: pardon of Binance founder **Changpeng Zhao ("CZ")**, whose exchange [closely supported the Trump family's World Liberty Financial crypto venture](https://www.cnn.com/2025/10/23/politics/binance-founder-trump-pardon); Trump said he acted "at the request of a lot of very good people," and Senate Banking Democrats [demanded a DOJ/Treasury accounting](https://www.banking.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/letter_to_doj_treasury_re_binance_pardon.pdf). December 1, 2025: pardon of former Honduran president **Juan Orlando Hernández**, 18 months into a 45-year US cocaine-trafficking sentence ([CRS analysis](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12621)), while the administration waged a military campaign against Latin American traffickers. December 3, 2025: pardon of sitting Democratic Rep. **Henry Cuellar** and his wife on the eve of their bribery trial — followed by Trump's public complaint of Cuellar's ["lack of LOYALTY"](https://www.cnn.com/2025/12/03/politics/henry-cuellar-trump-pardon) when Cuellar filed for reelection as a Democrat, as candid a statement of the transactional theory of clemency as exists in the record. See also the [Christian Science Monitor's synthesis of how 2025 clemency "broke the mold"](https://www.csmonitor.com/USA/Politics/2025/1209/trump-pardons-cuellar-hernandez).

The record's shape across four presidencies: **the abuse is structural, not partisan.** Every messaging document should use it in balanced pairs.

### Prior Amendment Proposals (with history)

- **Post-Watergate (93rd Congress, 1974).** The Ford–Nixon pardon triggered the first serious wave. **Sen. Walter Mondale's [S.J.Res.241](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241)** would have voided any pardon disapproved by **two-thirds of both Houses within 180 days** of issuance — clemency subject to a legislative check, mirroring the veto-override structure. It received hearings but never reached a floor vote; the Senate's only recorded action in the episode was a nonbinding sense-of-the-Senate resolution opposing further Watergate pardons before trial. A parallel House proposal, [H.J.Res.1145](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/house-joint-resolution/1145), would have limited pardons to post-conviction absent congressional concurrence. Lesson: even at peak public fury over a pardon, an amendment perceived as aimed at one act stalled.
- **Rep. Steve Cohen's serial amendments (2017–present)** — the modern standard text. Introduced in every Congress since October 2017: [H.J.Res.120 (115th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/house-joint-resolution/120), [H.J.Res.8 (116th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/house-joint-resolution/8/text), [H.J.Res.4 (117th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-joint-resolution/4/text), [H.J.Res.77 (118th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-joint-resolution/77), [H.J.Res.13 (119th, Jan. 9, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13). The text prohibits self-pardons and pardons of the president's **family members, administration officials, and paid campaign employees**; bars pardons for offenses motivated by a "direct and significant personal interest" of the president or those close to him, or committed **at the direction of or in coordination with** the president; and voids pardons issued for a **corrupt purpose** ([Cohen's summary](https://cohen.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/congressman-cohen-will-reintroduce-constitutional-amendment-limit)). History: never a hearing, never a markup, no Republican cosponsors of note — a Democratic-caucus artifact, which is exactly the packaging failure the foundation exists to avoid.
- **[Protecting Our Democracy Act, H.R. 5314 (117th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/5314)** — the statutory high-water mark. Passed the House **220-208 on Dec. 9, 2021** with one Republican (Kinzinger); died in the Senate. Pardon provisions: required DOJ and the White House to **submit pardon-related materials to Congress** for self-, family-, and obstruction-adjacent pardons; declared self-pardons invalid; and clarified that the **federal bribery statute covers pardons and commutations as "official acts."** Why it read partisan despite popular components: it was an omnibus of Trump-era grievances (emoluments, Hatch Act, subpoena enforcement, IG protections) passed near-party-line eleven months after Trump left office — a case study in how good provisions inherit the coding of their vehicle. Sen. Blumenthal's standalone [Pardon Transparency and Accountability Act](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/video-blumenthal-introduces-pardon-transparency-and-accountability-act) carries the disclosure piece forward but faces the *Garland* problem for anything beyond disclosure.
- **The process-reform track (statutory, complementary).** The [Office of the Pardon Attorney](https://www.justice.gov/pardon) — the regularized application pipeline inside DOJ — has been systematically bypassed: 11% usage in Trump's first term ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/trump-and-pardon-attorney)), with recent grants overwhelmingly originating from White House and political channels. Meanwhile [thousands of ordinary petitions languish](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-statistics). The leading academic reformer is **NYU's Rachel Barkow** (with Mark Osler): their ["Restructuring Clemency"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2479595) proposes an **independent, bipartisan clemency board outside DOJ** reporting directly to the president — removing the institutional conflict of prosecutors reviewing mercy for their own convictions; Barkow's [2022 House testimony](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/JU/JU08/20220519/114782/HHRG-117-JU08-Wstate-BarkowR-20220519.pdf) documented that 63% of pending FY2020 petitions were stalled at the Deputy AG or White House Counsel. This is the answer to the "mercy drought" objection — but it is statutory/executive-order territory, not amendment text.

## State Models

State constitutions are the richest source of tested designs. Taxonomy, from most to least constrained:

**Model 1 — Board-exclusive (governor has little or no clemency power).** Six board-exclusive states (Alabama, Connecticut, Georgia, Idaho, South Carolina, Utah) plus four (Nebraska, Nevada, Minnesota, Florida) in which the governor is one vote on a board [corrected 2026-08-18; was: "About nine states"]. **Georgia** is the pure case: [Const. art. IV, §II](https://law.justia.com/constitution/georgia/conart4.html) vests the **State Board of Pardons and Paroles** — not the governor — with the full clemency power ("the powers to grant reprieves, pardons, and paroles; to commute penalties; [and] to remove disabilities imposed by law"). Georgia's governor cannot pardon anyone, even in death cases. The design dates to a **1943 constitutional amendment passed after a governor's pardon-selling scandal** — ratified by the voters of a deep-red-tradition state ([Georgia Board history](https://pap.georgia.gov/about)). **Idaho** (Commission of Pardons and Parole), **Utah** (Board of Pardons and Parole, Const. art. VII, §12), and **Nebraska** ([Const. art. IV, §13](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/articles.php?article=IV-13): clemency vested in a **Board of Pardons** consisting of the Governor, Attorney General, and Secretary of State, acting by majority — the governor holds one vote of three) follow the same logic. Connecticut and South Carolina likewise vest pardons in boards.

**Model 2 — Board-recommendation-gated (governor acts only on a board's affirmative recommendation).** The exact constitutional texts are the foundation's best drafting precedents:

- **Pennsylvania**, [Const. art. IV, §9](https://codes.findlaw.com/pa/constitution-of-the-commonwealth-of-pennsylvania/pa-const-art-4-sect-9.html): *"No pardon shall be granted, nor sentence commuted, except on the recommendation in writing of a majority of the Board of Pardons, and in the case of a sentence of death or life imprisonment, on the unanimous recommendation in writing of the Board of Pardons, after full hearing in open session, upon due public notice. The recommendation, with the reasons therefor at length, shall be delivered to the Governor and a copy thereof shall be kept on file in the office of the Lieutenant Governor in a docket kept for that purpose."* — gating, open hearings, public notice, and written reasons in one clause.
- **Texas**, [Const. art. IV, §11](https://law.justia.com/constitution/texas/sections/cn000400-001100.html): *"In all criminal cases, except treason and impeachment, the Governor shall have power, after conviction, on the written signed recommendation and advice of the Board of Pardons and Paroles, or a majority thereof, to grant reprieves and commutations of punishment and pardons…"* The governor of **Texas** — the state most rhetorically committed to executive strength — cannot pardon without the board, and only **after conviction** (no preemptive pardons at all).
- **Delaware**, [Const. art. VII](https://delcode.delaware.gov/constitution/constitution-08.shtml): pardons only "upon the recommendation in writing of a majority of the Board of Pardons," with the recommendation *"with the reasons therefor at length"* filed with the Secretary of State, and the governor required to *"fully set forth in writing the grounds of all reprieves, pardons and remissions, to be entered in a register... and laid before the General Assembly."*
- **Florida** requires the governor plus two cabinet members; **Oklahoma** and **Louisiana** also gate on board recommendation.

**Model 3 — Transparency/reporting-mandated (governor free to act, but must publicly account).** The direct precedent for the amendment's lead provision:

- **California**, [Const. art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V): *"The Governor shall report to the Legislature each reprieve, pardon, and commutation granted, stating the pertinent facts and the reasons for granting it."* Section 8(b) adds a striking check: no pardon of a twice-convicted felon *"except on recommendation of the Supreme Court, 4 judges concurring."*
- **New York**, [Const. art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution): the governor *"shall annually communicate to the legislature each case of reprieve, commutation or pardon granted, stating the name of the convict, the crime of which he or she was convicted, the sentence and its date, and the date of the commutation, pardon or reprieve."*
- New Jersey and other states impose similar annual reporting with reasons; see the [Collateral Consequences Resource Center's 50-state comparison](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/50-state-comparisoncharacteristics-of-pardon-authorities-2/) for the full map.

**Model 4 — Unconditioned governor-alone.** A minority of states — and the home of essentially every modern state pardon scandal: Tennessee's Gov. Ray Blanton **cash-for-clemency scandal (1979)**, which forced his successor's early swearing-in; Kentucky's Gov. Matt Bevin **lame-duck pardon spree (2019)**, including the brother of a fundraiser (recipient later convicted federally of the same murder), which prompted Kentucky legislative proposals to bar election-to-inauguration pardons. **The correlation is the argument: board-gated and reporting states are conspicuously absent from the scandal record; the scandals cluster where one person decides alone, in secret, on the way out the door.** The federal pardon power is a Model 4 power — the least constrained clemency design in American law, state or federal.

## International Models

### Germany — countersignature and delegation

[Basic Law art. 60(2)](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/englisch_gg/englisch_gg.html): the Federal President "shall exercise the power to pardon offenders on behalf of the Federation **in individual cases**." Under the dominant reading of art. 58 — *"Orders and directions of the Federal President shall require for their validity the countersignature of the Federal Chancellor or of the competent Federal Minister"* — pardons, not being among art. 58's enumerated exceptions, require **ministerial countersignature**; in practice the President has delegated most federal clemency to the Federal Ministry of Justice, and most crimes fall under *Land* (state) clemency anyway. German presidential pardons are individual, counseled, rare, and have never functioned as political self-protection.

### Italy — the countersignature conflict, resolved

[Const. arts. 87 and 89](https://www.senato.it/documenti/repository/istituzione/costituzione_inglese.pdf): the President "may grant pardons and commute punishments," but no presidential act is valid unless countersigned by the proposing minister. When a justice minister refused to process a pardon, the Constitutional Court's [judgment no. 200/2006](https://www.cortecostituzionale.it/) resolved the standoff: the pardon is a **substantially presidential act** with an exclusively **humanitarian purpose** — the minister cannot block it on political grounds and is obliged to countersign, but the process still runs through the ministry's file, ensuring a documented, counseled decision. Two institutions must touch every pardon; neither can use it politically.

### India — clemency on advice, reviewable for bad faith

[Const. art. 72](https://www.india.gov.in/my-government/constitution-india) vests pardon power in the President, exercised — like all presidential powers under art. 74 — **on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**. The Supreme Court in [*Epuru Sudhakar v. Govt. of Andhra Pradesh* (2006)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/758562/) held clemency **judicially reviewable** — not on the merits, but where the order shows *"non-application of mind,"* is *"mala fide,"* rests on *"extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations,"* or is arbitrary — and quashed a remission granted on political/party grounds. A pardon granted as a political favor is, in the world's largest democracy, simply void.

### United Kingdom — mercy as a ministerial, accountable act

The royal prerogative of mercy is exercised **on the advice of the Justice Secretary** (in practice, decided by the minister, answerable to Parliament) — the sovereign personally decides nothing. After the Birmingham Six and related miscarriages, the Criminal Appeal Act 1995 created the [Criminal Cases Review Commission](https://ccrc.gov.uk/) (1997), an independent body that refers doubtful convictions back to the courts — institutionalizing the pardon's core legitimate function (correcting injustice) and shrinking free pardons to near-extinction. The UK's lesson: build the *machinery* of mercy and the discretionary act withers honestly.

### France — the 2008 narrowing

[Const. art. 17](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr/en/constitution-of-4-october-1958), as amended by the [constitutional law of 23 July 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_constitutional_law_of_23_July_2008): the President "has the right to grant **individual** pardons" — the reform **abolished collective pardons** (the traditional Bastille Day mass grants). Under art. 19 the act is countersigned by the Prime Minister and Justice Minister. A modern peer democracy looked at *mass, categorical clemency* and constitutionally banned it — direct precedent for an individual-naming requirement.

### South Africa — pardons under, not above, the constitution

Pardon power sits in [Const. §84(2)(j)](https://www.justice.gov.za/constitution/SAConstitution-web-eng.pdf). In [*President v. Hugo* (1997)](http://www.saflii.org/za/cases/ZACC/1997/4.html), the Constitutional Court held the pardon power is **subject to the Constitution and judicially reviewable**; in *Albutt v. Centre for the Study of Violence and Reconciliation* (2010) it went further — a special pardon process for political offenders was invalid because **victims had a right to be heard** first. Clemency as a public, procedurally fair act rather than a private favor.

**The comparative norm:** across every peer democracy surveyed, clemency is at least one of — counseled (ministerial advice/countersignature), individual, explained, or reviewable for bad faith. The United States federal pardon is **none of the four**. The US is not preserving an ancient norm; it is the outlier that dropped every safeguard the tradition evolved.

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Two-key systems don't kill mercy; they kill sale.** Germany and Italy have had countersignature for 75 years with no clemency paralysis and no pardon-selling scandal. The second signature's function is evidentiary and deliberative, not obstructive (Italy's Court even *ordered* the minister to sign).
2. **"Individual cases only" is a tested constitutional phrase.** France 2008 and Germany art. 60(2) both use it. It surgically removes blanket/categorical clemency while leaving every act of individual mercy intact.
3. **Review for bad faith is separable from review on the merits.** India and South Africa police only *mala fides*, non-application of mind, and irrelevant considerations — courts never reweigh whether mercy was deserved. That line has held for decades and answers the "courts will run clemency" objection.
4. **Institutionalized mercy outperforms discretionary mercy at mercy's own job.** The UK's CCRC corrects more miscarriages than free pardons ever did; Georgia's board grants far more pardons per capita than recent presidents. Constraining the *political* pardon and building *administrative* clemency are complements, not trade-offs.
5. **Humanitarian-purpose language does real work.** Italy's Court confined the pardon to humanitarian ends, which is what stripped the minister's political veto *and* the president's political use in one move. Purpose clauses are enforceable.
6. **Advice requirements convert clemency from a personal act into a governmental act.** UK/India-style "on advice" models make some minister own every pardon on the record — accountability without any ban at all. The weakest intervention with a track record.
7. **Victims and the public are recognized stakeholders in clemency elsewhere.** South Africa's *Albutt* (victims must be heard) and Pennsylvania's "full hearing in open session, upon due public notice" converge from opposite hemispheres on the same principle: mercy done in secret is presumptively suspect.
8. **No democracy trusts the pardon to protect the pardoner.** Self-protective clemency (self, family, co-conspirators) is either structurally impossible (board systems, ministerial systems) or voidable (mala fides review) everywhere surveyed. The US debate over whether a president may pardon his own co-conspirators would be unintelligible in any peer system.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. The four below clear it — public justification (never directly polled, but the least incumbent-coded provision, backed by a century of California/New York constitutional practice — commission the poll), the family ban (77%, 64R/88D), the sold-pardon ban (codifies existing bribery law; corruption is a "big problem" for 92%, 90R/93D), and individual naming (both parties' blanket pardons drew majority disapproval from both parties' voters). Three candidates are held back at the end.*

1. **Public justification — no secret mercy.**
   Proposed formulation: *"Every reprieve or pardon granted shall be published, together with the name of the recipient, the offenses covered, and a statement of the reasons for the grant, and shall be reported to Congress. No reprieve or pardon shall take effect until so published."*
   - Direct lineage: [California art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V) ("stating the pertinent facts and the reasons for granting it") and [New York art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution); over a century of state practice with zero evidence of chilling legitimate clemency. Also the never-polled provision — commission the poll.
   - ⚠️ **Mercy-destruction trap:** the requirement must be a *disclosure* condition, not an *approval* condition — no official may sit in judgment of the reasons' adequacy, or the clause becomes a heckler's veto and deepens the clemency drought. Draft so a boilerplate reason satisfies the clause; its power is publicity, not review.

2. **No family clemency.**
   Proposed formulation: *"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to his or her spouse, or to any relative of the President or the President's spouse within the third degree of kinship, or to the spouse of any such relative."*
   - The strongest-polling provision in the package — 77% (64R/88D) — with fresh precedents from **both** parties (Hunter Biden and the January 2025 Biden family pardons; Charles Kushner). Degree-of-kinship language is standard in state ethics and judicial-recusal law.
   - ⚠️ **Line-drawing trap:** every boundary excludes a sympathetic hypothetical (a president's child railroaded by a hostile prosecutor). Answer in the record: the family member retains every other remedy — courts, the clemency board, and a *successor* president; the ban is only on the conflicted grantor, not on mercy itself.

3. **No sold or self-benefiting pardons (the public-benefit principle).**
   Proposed formulation: *"A reprieve or pardon granted in exchange for anything of value, or for the purpose of shielding the President from legal liability or advancing the President's private financial interest, is void; and the acceptance of anything of value in return for the grant of a reprieve or pardon may be punished as bribery notwithstanding any grant."*
   - Codifies what DOJ already treats as chargeable (the [2020 pardon-bribery investigation](https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/01/politics/pardon-investigation-doj/index.html); PODA's bribery clarification) and reaches the Rich and CZ fact patterns; the "void" language means a corrupt pardon fails even if the bribery prosecution never comes.
   - ⚠️ **Review-standard trap:** any motive test invites case-by-case judicial excavation of presidential intent — exactly what *Garland*-minded critics will call a destruction of the power. Mitigate by drafting the *bribery* half as self-executing criminal law (a transaction test, not a motive test) and treating the "purpose" half as the fallback severable clause; the categorical exclusions (family, naming) should carry the load precisely because they require no inquiry into motive.

4. **Individual, specified clemency only.**
   Proposed formulation: *"Every reprieve or pardon shall name the individual to whom it is granted and specify the offenses to which it applies; no reprieve or pardon shall extend to offenses not yet committed at the time of the grant."*
   - France's 2008 "individual basis" reform and Germany's "individual cases" are the tested models; this provision symmetrically addresses the January 6 blanket (~1,500 unnamed-by-deliberation grants), the Hunter Biden 2014–2024 decade blanket, and open-ended preemptive pardons — while leaving genuinely individual preemptive mercy (Ford–Nixon-style, for *specified past* conduct) available.
   - ⚠️ **Mass-mercy trap:** a strict naming rule would have barred Carter's 1977 draft-evasion amnesty and Washington's Whiskey Rebellion pardons — categorical clemency has legitimate uses. Consider a safety valve: categorical grants permitted only for classes defined by offense (not by relationship to the president) with each beneficiary individually certified and published. If the safety valve gets complicated, drop the categorical ban and rely on naming-plus-publication alone.

**Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar:**

- **No self-directed clemency** (*"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to himself or herself."*) — consistent with the rejected standalone category in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md): Republican support is regime-dependent (59% on a norm statement in Protect Democracy's 2024 battleground polling; 43% on the ban itself in YouGov's Aug 15–19, 2024 national poll — both mid-2024 fieldwork, so the gap is wording/sample, not movement across an administration change). [corrected 2026-08-18; was: "43% by Dec 2025", misattributed to Economist/YouGov Dec 2025] The principle partially survives inside the sold/self-benefiting clause ("shielding the President from legal liability"); the explicit ban returns if polling stabilizes across an administration change. Pedigree when it does: the [1974 OLC memo](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president) ("no one may be a judge in his own case").
- **No clemency for the president's own conspirators** (*"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon for an offense committed at the direction of, or in knowing coordination with, the President, or an offense of which the President was an intended beneficiary."*) — the associates ban sits at exactly 50% with Republicans, on advocacy-commissioned polling only ([H.J.Res.13](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13) lineage), and it carries the acute review-standard trap ("at the direction of" requires a factfinder). Path back: independent polling above the bar; until then it is the first provision to trade away.
- **Congressional disapproval — the Mondale check** (*"A pardon shall be void if, within ninety days of its publication, two-thirds of each House of Congress votes to disapprove it."*) — genuine bipartisan-era pedigree ([S.J.Res.241, 1974](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241)) but never polled and procedurally complex; if revived, draft the 90 days to run from *publication* so end-of-term pardons are reviewed by the next Congress, and do not substitute a lame-duck blackout (which would also bar legitimate end-of-term mercy).

**The package skeleton.** A single amendment article: **Section 1** — publication, naming, and reasons requirement (principles 1 + 4); **Section 2** — categorical exclusions: family and quid pro quo (principles 2 + 3); **Section 3** — savings clause: *"Nothing in this article shall be construed to diminish the power to grant reprieves, pardons, or commutations to any person not described herein"* — the drafted answer to the mercy-destruction objection, and the sentence every spokesperson should memorize. Held-back provisions rejoin as polling clears them. Lead all public materials with Sections 1–2; the pitch is *"every president, both parties, forever: mercy in public, never for sale, never for family."*

**Inherently statutory** (belongs in companion legislation, not the amendment):

- **An independent clemency board outside DOJ** (the [Barkow–Osler model](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2479595)) with a duty to process every petition within a fixed period — the supply-side fix for the clemency drought that makes the amendment's constraints defensible.
- **Pardon-lobbying disclosure** — registration and fee disclosure for paid pardon brokers.
- **Bribery-statute clarification** that clemency is an "official act" (the PODA provision) — worth enacting even before ratification.
- **Document-preservation and congressional-transmission rules** for clemency files (PODA's transparency title), including Burdick-notice and acceptance records.
- **A CCRC-style miscarriage-of-justice review body** for federal convictions — institutionalizing the pardon's legitimate core, on the UK model.
- **Machine-readable publication** of the clemency database (recipients, offenses, process channel, OPA recommendation status) so the bypass rate is permanently public.
- **Victim-notification requirements** before grants of clemency in crimes with identifiable victims (the *Albutt* principle, already partial DOJ practice).

### Extends: Constitutional doctrine — what the power is, and what it already is not

- **The Framers considered and rejected the two-key model — the strongest originalist counterargument.** At the Convention on **August 25, 1787**, Roger Sherman moved to give the President power "to grant reprieves until the ensuing session of the Senate, and pardons with consent of the Senate"; it failed **1–8** (only Connecticut aye) ([Madison's notes, Aug. 25](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_825.asp)). On **September 15**, Edmund Randolph moved to except treason — "The President may himself be guilty. The Traytors may be his own instruments" — Wilson answered that a complicit President could be impeached, King floated Senate concurrence, Madison preferred "an association of the Senate as a Council of advice," and Randolph's motion failed **2–8–1** ([Madison's notes, Sept. 15](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_915.asp)). Hamilton then defended the design in [*Federalist* No. 74](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed74.asp): "one man appears to be a more eligible dispenser of the mercy of government, than a body of men"; in rebellion "a well-timed offer of pardon to the insurgents or rebels may restore the tranquillity of the commonwealth" and "the loss of a week, a day, an hour, may sometimes be fatal"; and "the reflection that the fate of a fellow-creature depended on his sole fiat, would naturally inspire scrupulousness and caution." Every conservative objection to the Mondale check or a board model will cite this record. The memo's answer is already in the package: the lead provisions (publication, family, sale, naming) add no second decision-maker and no delay — they leave the "sole fiat" intact and constrain only its beneficiaries and its secrecy. Randolph's "traytors may be his own instruments" is also the founding-era pedigree for the held-back conspirators clause.
- ***Murphy v. Ford* (W.D. Mich. 1975) — the only judicial test of a preemptive pardon.** A Michigan lawyer sued to void the Nixon pardon on the ground that Nixon had never been indicted or convicted; the district court dismissed, holding the power "is unlimited, except in cases of impeachment, and may as properly be exercised before criminal proceedings begin as after conviction," and that the Framers designed it "to provide for just such instances" ([Encyclopedia.com summary](https://www.encyclopedia.com/politics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/murphy-v-ford-390-f-supp-1372-1975); [opinion, 390 F. Supp. 1372](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1966699/murphy-v-ford/)). Relevant to Principle 4: any limit on open-ended preemptive pardons must be constitutional text, because the pre-charge pardon is settled law at the only level it has been litigated.
- ***Biddle v. Perovich* (1927) — commutations cannot be refused, and clemency "is not a private act of grace."** Holmes, for the Court (May 31, 1927), upheld a death-to-life commutation over the prisoner's objection: a pardon "is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power. It is a part of the Constitutional scheme," and "the public welfare, not his consent determines what shall be done" ([LII text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/274/480)). Two uses: (i) drafters should not assume *Burdick*'s acceptance rule applies to commutations; (ii) Holmes's "part of the Constitutional scheme … public welfare" language is the best Supreme Court pedigree for the memo's public-benefit principle — the pardon belongs to the public, in the Court's own words.
- **The mechanism behind "federal offenses only" is dual sovereignty, reaffirmed 7–2 in [*Gamble v. United States* (2019)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/17-646)** ("where there are two sovereigns, there are two laws, and two offences" — Alito, J., for the Court, June 17, 2019). The limit was tested live in the current term: on **December 11, 2025** Trump announced a "full pardon" for Tina Peters, imprisoned on **Colorado state** convictions; Colorado's attorney general called a presidential pardon of a state conviction something that "has no precedent in American law … and will not hold up," and Gov. Polis said he would "not even consider a pardon" ([Colorado Sun, Dec. 11, 2025](https://coloradosun.com/2025/12/11/tina-peters-trump-pardon/)). Peters was ultimately released only by a **state** commutation (Polis halved her nine-year sentence on May 15, 2026) ([Colorado Sun, May 15, 2026](https://coloradosun.com/2026/05/15/tina-peters-sentence-reduced-by-colorado-governor-jared-polis/)). Useful for the record: the state-crime limit held under direct presidential pressure.
- ***Trump v. United States* (2024) reshaped the "bribery-for-pardon is already prosecutable" premise.** The majority named the pardon among the President's "conclusive and preclusive" powers ("To the executive alone is intrusted the power of pardon" — quoting *Klein*), and, in footnote 3, drew the evidentiary line for a pardon-bribery case: the prosecutor "may point to the public record to show the fact that the President performed the official act" and "may admit evidence of what the President allegedly demanded, received, accepted, or agreed to receive," but "may not … admit testimony or private records of the President or his advisers probing the official act itself" ([opinion, 603 U.S. ___ (2024)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_e2pg.pdf)). Justice Barrett, concurring in part, warned that excluding the official act "would hamstring the prosecution" — "the jury must be allowed to hear about both the quid and the quo"; Justice Sotomayor's dissent put it as "Takes a bribe in exchange for a pardon? Immune." Two consequences for the memo: (i) the memo's line that DOJ "already treated" sold pardons as prosecutable is true but now incomplete — a president's own bribed pardon is prosecutable only on the public record plus evidence of the quid; (ii) this strengthens the case for Principle 1: a constitutional **publication-with-reasons** requirement is what guarantees there *is* a public record for such a prosecution to point to, and Principle 3's "void" clause operates without any inquiry into the President's private deliberations.
- **The strongest scholarly counter to "statutory reform is dead on arrival":** Bob Bauer and Jack Goldsmith ([Lawfare, "How to Reform the Pardon Power," Feb. 26, 2020](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/how-reform-pardon-power); developed in *After Trump* (2020)) argue that "Congress has much more room to regulate abuses of the pardon power than is commonly assumed" and that targeted statutes — criminalizing pardons sold "for personal financial benefit or a campaign contribution, or for the benefit of an immediate family member," declaring self-pardons ineffective, and criminalizing pardons used "in a corrupt scheme to obstruct a judicial proceeding" — "are not ruled out by the strong formulations of the pardon power found in some old Supreme Court cases." They explicitly reject the need for an amendment. The memo should engage this directly: the foundation's answer is that (a) *Trump v. United States* (above) has since hardened the "conclusive and preclusive" reading, and (b) statutes can regulate the *conduct around* a pardon but cannot make a pardon *void* — only an amendment can.

### Extends: The Abuse Record (both parties — this balance is the argument)

- **2026 update — the donor pattern continued and became an industry.** *Forbes*' running tally of pardoned donors and business allies ([Jan. 16, 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/alisondurkee/2026/01/16/trumps-pardon-list-puerto-rico-governors-co-conspirator-latest-big-time-donor-sprung-free-by-president/)) includes Nikola founder Trevor Milton (~$946,000 to Trump since 2016; wife gave $927,900 in Oct. 2024; pardoned Mar. 27, 2025), nursing-home executive Paul Walczak (mother gave $1 million at a fundraiser; pardoned Apr. 23, 2025), and banker Julio Herrera Velutini (daughter gave $3.5 million to the MAGA super PAC; pardoned Jan. 16, 2026), and estimates "more than $1 billion" in restitution and fees erased. On **July 3, 2026** Trump pardoned Abramoff-scandal figure Adam Kidan (~$270,000 to Trump-affiliated committees in 2024; $217 million restitution order with Abramoff) three days after Kidan's donation to a Pennsylvania congressman ([Spotlight PA, July 2026](https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/07/adam-kidan-pardon-trump-ryan-mackenzie-house-bob-brooks-federal-government/)). The *Wall Street Journal* reported in December 2025 that "lobbyists say their going rate is $1 million" to advocate for a pardon, with success fees offered "as much as $6 million" ([WSJ, as excerpted by Political Wire, Dec. 23, 2025](https://politicalwire.com/2025/12/23/inside-the-new-fast-track-to-a-presidential-pardon/)). This is the fact base for the "pardon-lobbying disclosure" item in **Inherently statutory** and for Principle 3. Balanced-pair use: Marc Rich / Denise Rich (2001) remains the Democratic exemplar; a spokesperson should never cite Walczak or Kidan without it.
- **The Biden autopen dispute — a record-integrity episode from the other party.** On **October 28, 2025** the House Oversight Committee's final report declared Biden's autopen-signed executive actions, including pardons, should be held "null and void" and asked AG Bondi to review them; Bondi said DOJ "has already initiated a review" ([ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Politics/comer-calls-biden-autopen-actions-held-null-void/story?id=126913394)). On **December 2, 2025** Trump posted that Biden's autopen pardons were "fully and completely terminated, and is of no legal effect." PolitiFact rated the claim **False**: a 2005 OLC opinion permits a subordinate to affix the President's signature by autopen; an 1869 federal ruling holds "when a pardon is complete, there is no power to revoke it"; and there is no constitutional mechanism to undo a delivered pardon ([PolitiFact, Dec. 4, 2025](https://politifact.com/factchecks/2025/dec/04/donald-trump/revoke-pardon-biden-autopen/)). Relevance: the fight was possible only because federal clemency has no constitutional **record-of-decision** requirement — Principle 1's publication clause (and the statutory document-preservation item) would have made the authorization trail public at the time of grant, and the finality rule cuts both ways: it protects Biden's grants and Trump's alike.
- **A blanket grant forced the courts to define its own scope.** Proclamation 10887 pardoned "individuals convicted of offenses related to events that occurred at or near the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021." DOJ first said (Feb. 6, 2025) it did not cover Dan Wilson's separate 2022 Kentucky firearms convictions, then reversed (Feb. 25); the district court and the D.C. Circuit (order of Apr. 2, 2025, Rao, J., dissenting) held the pardon reaches only offenses "tethered to a specific time – January 6, 2021 – and place – at or near the U.S. Capitol," not offenses "only connected to January 6 by the happenstance that it was uncovered during investigation" ([D.C. Cir. No. 25-3041](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/orders/docs/2025/04/25-3041LDSN.pdf)). Trump then issued Wilson a **second, individual** pardon in November 2025 ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/dan-wilson-donald-trump-january-6-capitol-attack-pardon)). Direct evidence for Principle 4: categorical pardons of unnamed persons generate litigation over who and what is covered; naming-and-specifying is what ended the dispute.

### Extends: Prior Amendment Proposals (with history)

- **A bipartisan Mondale-style amendment now exists in the 119th Congress — bearing on the held-back congressional-disapproval provision.** [H.J.Res.135](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/135), introduced **December 18, 2025** by Rep. Johnny Olszewski (D-MD) with **Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE)** as original cosponsor (six further Democratic cosponsors through July 2026; referred to House Judiciary, no action) ([bill status, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hjres/BILLSTATUS-119hjres135.xml)). Text ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres135ih/xml/BILLS-119hjres135ih.xml)): the President "shall transmit a notification of a grant of a Reprieve or Pardon within 3 days" to the Senate President pro tempore and the Speaker; Congress may within 30 days introduce, and within 60 days pass by **two-thirds of both Houses**, a bill to nullify; absent nullification the pardon "shall become effective" 90 days after notification; a nullification bill signed by 20 Representatives or 5 Senators must receive a vote; and "if the President fails to notify … within 3 days, the Reprieve or Pardon shall have no force or effect." Two lessons: the **notification-or-void** clause is the closest existing congressional text to the memo's Principle 1 ("no reprieve or pardon shall take effect until so published"), and the disapproval mechanism now has a Republican name on it — but one retiring Republican is not a bipartisan floor, and the 90-day suspension of effect is exactly the machinery the memo warned about (it delays every act of legitimate mercy, not just suspect ones). It does not move the held-back conclusion by itself; it is a data point that the Mondale check has live cross-party interest.
- **Statutory track, 2026:** the [No Payoffs For Pardons Act (Aug. 3, 2026)](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/blumenthal-and-schumer-lead-senate-democrats-in-legislation-to-end-unprecedented-presidential-corruption-stop-pay-to-play-pardons) (Blumenthal, Schumer, Hirono, Cortez Masto, Schiff; Rep. Morelle in the House) would require pardon recipients to disclose substantial financial gifts connected to their clemency at grant and for four years after, clarify that federal bribery law applies to the President and Vice President, and set a 10-year limitations period for clemency bribery — **no Republican cosponsors**, i.e., the same Democratic-caucus packaging problem the memo documents for the Cohen amendments and PODA. The gift-disclosure-by-recipient design is worth noting for the "pardon-lobbying disclosure" statutory item: it reaches the *quid* side without touching the pardon.
- **Ford's October 17, 1974 testimony is the accountability precedent for public justification.** Five weeks after the Nixon pardon, Ford "appeared on Capitol Hill before the House Judiciary Committee's Subcommittee on Criminal Justice (Hungate Committee), becoming the first sitting President to give sworn congressional testimony," answering questions on "when and with whom he had discussed the pardon, and why he decided to grant it" ([Ford Library topic guide](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/digital-research-room/library-collections/topic-guides/nixon-pardon)). The most controversial pardon in US history was followed, voluntarily, by exactly what Principle 1 would require of every pardon: the grantor explaining his reasons on the public record. Cite it as bipartisan-era proof that public justification is compatible with — and dignifies — the power.

### Extends: State Models

- **Rhode Island has a live legislative-consent model — and it is a cautionary tale for the Mondale check.** [R.I. Const. art. IX, §13](https://rilegislature.gov/riconstitution/Constitution/C09.aspx): "The governor, by and with the advice and consent of the senate, shall hereafter exclusively exercise the pardoning power, except in cases of impeachment." The Collateral Consequences Resource Center reports the result: "No pardon to a living person has been issued in many years," the Senate-consent requirement "evidently discourages exercise of power," and the only recent pardon (2011) was posthumous ([CCRC Rhode Island profile](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/rhode-island-restoration-of-rights-pardon-expungement-sealing/)). This is the mercy-destruction trap operating in real time and belongs next to the held-back congressional-disapproval provision as the strongest reason to keep any legislative check *post hoc* and supermajority-gated rather than a consent requirement.
- **Alabama belongs in Model 1, and the CCRC taxonomy sharpens the memo's counts.** Alabama's 1939 constitutional amendment eliminated "the governor's ability to grant any prisoner a pardon or parole," vesting it in a three-member Board of Pardons and Paroles with "complete control and authority," the governor retaining only reprieves and commutations in death cases ([Encyclopedia of Alabama](https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/alabama-board-of-pardons-and-paroles/); [CCRC](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/50-state-comparisoncharacteristics-of-pardon-authorities-2/)) — a second deep-red-tradition state that removed the governor's pardon power within four years of Georgia's 1943 amendment. CCRC's current map counts **six** states where an independent board holds the power outright (Alabama, Connecticut, Georgia, Idaho, South Carolina, Utah), **22** where the governor shares it with a board (sitting on it — Florida, Minnesota, Nebraska, Nevada — or gated by its recommendation), **eight** requiring mandatory but non-binding consultation (Alaska, Arkansas, Kansas, Michigan, Missouri, Montana, Ohio, Washington), and **five** jurisdictions with no statutory advisory process at all: D.C., Maine, Oregon, Wisconsin — and the **federal** government. (Note for the founder: the memo's "about nine" board-exclusive states is closer to six pure cases plus the shared-board states.) Minnesota's 2023 reform (board decisions "need no longer be unanimous, but the governor must always be in the majority," plus a nine-member Clemency Review Commission) is a fresh example of *adding* clemency capacity while keeping a multi-member check.

### Extends: International Models

- **Brazil (2023) — a supreme court voided a president's pardon of a political ally for "deviation of purpose."** On April 21, 2022, President Bolsonaro pardoned federal deputy Daniel Silveira the day after the Supreme Federal Tribunal sentenced him to 8 years 9 months for threatening democratic institutions and the court itself. On **May 10, 2023** the STF annulled the pardon **8–2**, holding (per rapporteur Rosa Weber) that it was granted "com desvio de finalidade" — as a personal favor to an ally rather than legitimate clemency — and that the pardon power is subject to judicial review when abused; the two dissenters argued the constitution placed the power beyond judicial oversight ([Brasil de Fato, May 10, 2023](https://www.brasildefato.com.br/2023/05/10/stf-cancela-perdao-da-pena-que-bolsonaro-deu-a-ex-deputado-federal-daniel-silveira/)). Add to Abstraction Lesson 3 (bad-faith review) and Lesson 8: another peer democracy treats a pardon of the president's own political instrument as voidable, on essentially the theory of the held-back conspirators clause.
- **Poland (2015–2024) — a peer democracy's highest ordinary court held that a pardon before final conviction has "no legal effect."** President Duda pardoned interior minister Mariusz Kamiński and deputy Maciej Wąsik on Nov. 15, 2015, before their appeals were heard; the Supreme Court resolved in 2017, and ruled again in June 2023, that "the right of pardon may only be exercised for persons whose guilt has been established by a final court ruling," while the (contested) Constitutional Tribunal held on June 2, 2023 that pardons are "the exclusive competence of the president and not subject to review" ([Notes from Poland, June 6, 2023](https://notesfrompoland.com/2023/06/06/polish-supreme-court-rejects-presidents-pardon-of-minister-accused-of-abusing-powers/)). After reconviction and imprisonment, Duda pardoned both men a **second time on January 24, 2024** ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2024/01/24/polands-president-pardons-opposition-mps-for-a-second-time-in-bitter-political-dispute)). Two lessons for Principle 4: Poland is the clearest modern example of a democracy rejecting *pre-conviction* pardons of the executive's own ministers (Texas's "after conviction" rule has a European counterpart), and the episode is also a warning that a rule enforced by courts against a determined president produces a constitutional standoff — a reason the memo's naming/specification approach (categorical, self-executing) is preferable to a reviewability rule.

### Extends: Constitutional Abstraction Candidates — gate check (polling)

- **New independent poll with party breakdowns — and a caution on the family ban's floor.** YouGov, **Jan. 28–Feb. 3, 2025** (n=2,167 adult citizens, ±2.9), asked whether it is acceptable or unacceptable for a U.S. president to take various actions ([toplines/crosstabs PDF](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Acceptability_of_President_Actions_poll_results.pdf); [YouGov write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/51548-where-americans-draw-the-line-on-presidential-behavior)). "Issuing pardons to family members": **59% unacceptable** overall — **72% of Republicans, 42% of Democrats** (30% of Democrats "acceptable," 28% not sure). "Issuing pardons to their political allies": **53% unacceptable** — **39% of Republicans, 67% of Democrats**. Fielded weeks after the Hunter Biden and Biden-family pardons and the January 6 grants, each party's floor collapsed on the provision that had just been used by its own president. **Gate-check bearing:** the family ban's 77% (64R/88D) in Protect Democracy's mid-2024 poll — the memo's strongest number — was measured *before* a Democratic president used the family pardon; the Democratic floor on the same principle was 42% eight months later on a differently worded question. This does not remove the family ban from the package (the *Republican* floor rose to 72%, and the principle now has fresh grievances on both sides), but it means the family ban is not immune to the incumbent-coding trap the memo assigns only to the self-pardon ban, and it raises the priority of commissioning an independent poll that asks the family, sale, naming, and public-justification provisions in *forward-looking, both-parties* wording ("any future president") in the same instrument. The associates figure (39R/67D) confirms the held-back status of the conspirators clause on independent data.

### Flagged corrections (verified — applied 2026-08-18)

- **"Acceptance implies guilt" (Constitutional doctrine bullet on *Burdick*).** The memo presents *Burdick*'s "carries an imputation of guilt; acceptance a confession of it" as doctrine and proposes it as a messaging frame ("preemptive pardons brand their recipients"). The Tenth Circuit in [*Lorance v. Commandant*, No. 20-3055 (10th Cir. Sept. 23, 2021)](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010110580824.pdf) held, as a matter of first impression, that acceptance of a presidential pardon "does not have the legal effect of a confession of guilt": the *Burdick* language "was dictum," arose "in the context of discussing personal" disgrace and public opinion, and "although various federal courts have parroted *Burdick*'s statement … none have given formal, legal effect to such an implied confession"; the court "reject[ed] that draconian reading." The D.C. Circuit's en banc *United States v. Schaffer*, 240 F.3d 35 (2001), likewise says only that acceptance "may imply" a confession and that a pardon "does not, standing alone, render Schaffer innocent" ([FindLaw text](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dc-circuit/1047547.html)). Suggested fix: reword the bullet to "*Burdick* (1915): a pardon may be refused, and its dictum that acceptance 'carries an imputation of guilt' has rhetorical but not legal force (*Lorance*, 10th Cir. 2021)" — and do not build public messaging on "acceptance = confession," which will be debunked.
- **Model 1 count ("About nine states").** The Collateral Consequences Resource Center's current 50-state comparison counts six states with an independent board holding the pardon power outright (Alabama, Connecticut, Georgia, Idaho, South Carolina, Utah); Nebraska, Nevada, Minnesota, and Florida are shared-board states in which the governor sits on the board ([CCRC](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/50-state-comparisoncharacteristics-of-pardon-authorities-2/)). Suggested fix: "six board-exclusive states plus four in which the governor is one vote on a board."
- **"Bribery-for-pardon is already prosecutable" (Constitutional doctrine bullet).** Not wrong, but now materially incomplete after *Trump v. United States* (2024) — see the *Trump* addition above for the evidentiary limits (public record and quid evidence admissible; the President's private records and advisers' testimony "probing the official act itself" not). Suggested fix: add a clause noting the 2024 limits and that a prosecution of the *recipient* or *broker* is unaffected.

## Implementation: the law today and the day after (2026-08-18)
*Amendment-specific companion to [RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md](RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md) (supremacy, self-execution, enforcement clauses, trigger laws, effective dates). This section applies that general law to the five-section pardon draft in [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) §8; it does not restate it. Every new URL below was opened and checked on 2026-08-18.*

### 1. Federal law today

**Constitutional text and controlling doctrine (what the article would confirm, narrow, or leave alone).**

- **Art. II, §2, cl. 1** — "Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment." The article leaves the impeachment exception and the federal-offenses-only limit untouched (dual sovereignty per [*Gamble v. United States* (2019)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/17-646), already in the memo).
- **Scope: [*Ex parte Garland* (1866)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/71/333/)** — the power reaches "every offence known to the law, and may be exercised at any time after its commission … and is not subject to legislative control." Two consequences: (i) Section 2's final clause ("no reprieve or pardon shall extend to offenses not yet committed") *confirms* the "after its commission" reading rather than changing it; (ii) the "not subject to legislative control" holding — restated in [*Schick v. Reed* (1974)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/419/256/) ("cannot be modified, abridged, or diminished by the Congress") — is what makes the article necessary and is *narrowed only to the extent of the article's own text*; it otherwise stands.
- **Amnesty = pardon: [*Knote v. United States* (1877)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/95/149).** The distinction between pardon and amnesty "is not … recognized in our law," and a pardon "does not affect any rights which have vested in others" and "cannot touch moneys in the treasury of the United States, except expressly authorized by act of Congress." Bearing: Section 2's naming rule reaches categorical amnesties precisely because the Constitution treats them as pardons; and *Knote*'s vested-rights/Treasury limits are untouched by the article.
- **Delivery and acceptance: [*United States v. Wilson* (1833)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/32/150).** Marshall: a pardon is "a deed, to the validity of which, delivery is essential, and delivery is not complete, without acceptance," and one "not having been brought judicially before the court … cannot be noticed by the judges." Bearing on Section 1 ("shall not take effect until so published"): existing law already makes a pardon's effect turn on an event after signature (delivery); the article adds a second, public one. Courts would have to reconcile "granted," "delivered," and "published" (see §3(f)). *Burdick* (acceptance/refusal) and [*Biddle v. Perovich* (1927)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/274/480) (commutation needs no consent; clemency "is not a private act of grace") remain in force.
- **Reach: [*Ex parte Grossman* (1925)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/267/87)** — "Nothing in the ordinary meaning of the words 'offenses against the United States' excludes criminal contempts" (Taft, C.J., Mar. 2, 1925). Untouched.
- **[*United States v. Klein* (1871)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/80/128/)** — Congress cannot direct courts to treat a pardon as inoperative or as proof of guilt. After ratification, a court treating a Section 4 pardon as void applies *constitutional* text, not a statute, so *Klein* is no obstacle; *Klein* continues to bar Congress from legislating pardon-voiding rules beyond the article.
- **Preemptive pardons: [*Murphy v. Ford* (W.D. Mich. 1975)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1966699/murphy-v-ford/)** — pre-charge pardons upheld at the only level litigated. Section 2 leaves an individual, offense-specified preemptive pardon lawful; it bars only unnamed-recipient and future-offense grants.
- **[*Trump v. United States* (2024)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_e2pg.pdf)** — pardon among the "conclusive and preclusive" powers; fn. 3 lets a bribery prosecutor "point to the public record" and prove the quid, but not admit evidence "probing the official act itself." Bearing on Section 4: the "void" clause and the "may be punished as bribery" clause operate on the transaction and the public record, which is what fn. 3 leaves admissible; Section 1's publication requirement guarantees such a record exists.

**Statutes and regulations.**

- **No statute requires publication, naming, or reasons for individual clemency.** The only general publication law is the Federal Register Act, [44 U.S.C. §1505(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/44/1505), which requires publication of "Presidential proclamations and Executive orders" having "general applicability and legal effect." Mass grants issued *as proclamations* therefore appear in the Federal Register — e.g., [Proclamation 10887 (Jan. 20, 2025), 90 FR 8331](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-29/html/2025-01950.htm), granting "a full, complete and unconditional pardon to all other individuals convicted of offenses related to events that occurred at or near the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021," and directing the Attorney General to "administer and effectuate the immediate issuance of certificates of pardon" to them. Individual warrants are not proclamations and are not covered. In practice the Office of the Pardon Attorney posts lists of grants ([e.g., the 2025– list](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-grants-president-donald-j-trump-2025-present), whose columns are "NAME and WARRANT / DISTRICT / SENTENCED / OFFENSE" — no reasons column); that practice is administrative, not required by law.
- **The DOJ clemency regulations, 28 C.F.R. Part 1**, are the entire federal "process": petitions go to the Pardon Attorney ([§1.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/1.1)); a five-year post-release waiting period for pardon petitions ([§1.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/1.2)); investigation, victim notification, and a written recommendation from the Attorney General to the President ([§1.6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/1.6)); the Pardon Attorney's functions ([28 C.F.R. §0.35](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/0.35)). And [§1.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/1.11): the rules "are advisory only and for the internal guidance of Department of Justice personnel," "create no enforceable rights in persons applying for executive clemency, nor do they restrict the authority granted to the President under Article II, section 2 of the Constitution." Nothing in Part 1 conflicts with the article; it would stand unchanged. ([CRS R46179, Jan. 14, 2020](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R46179.html): the regulations "are merely advisory and do not affect the President's ultimate authority to grant relief"; on the 116th-Congress disclosure bills, "it is unclear whether such legislation would be viewed by the courts as an impermissible imposition on an area of executive authority," with requirements aimed at subordinate officials on firmer footing than those aimed at the President.)
- **Bribery, [18 U.S.C. §201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/201).** "Public official" is defined as a Member of Congress "or an officer or employee or person acting for or on behalf of the United States"; "official act" is "any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy"; bribery carries up to 15 years and disqualification from office. [*McDonnell v. United States* (2016)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/15-474) (unanimous, June 27, 2016) confines "official act" to "a formal exercise of governmental power" on "something specific and focused" — a grant of clemency plainly qualifies; arranging a meeting does not. Whether §201's "public official" reaches the President has never been adjudicated, which is why both the [Protecting Our Democracy Act (H.R. 5314, 117th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/5314) and the [No Payoffs for Pardons Act (Aug. 3, 2026)](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/blumenthal-and-schumer-lead-senate-democrats-in-legislation-to-end-unprecedented-presidential-corruption-stop-pay-to-play-pardons) contain clauses stating that federal bribery law covers clemency and the President. Section 4's "may be punished as bribery" clause presupposes a bribery statute; §201 exists, and the article would remove any *constitutional* objection to applying it to a sold pardon (the "notwithstanding any grant" clause), but Congress would still be the body that decides the statute's terms.
- **Kinship measure.** Section 3's "within the third degree of kinship" mirrors the federal judicial-recusal statute, [28 U.S.C. §455(b)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/455) ("a person within the third degree of relationship to either of them, or the spouse of such a person"), with §455(d)(2) computing degrees "according to the civil law system" — an existing federal body of interpretation courts could borrow.
- **Pending and prior bills the article would render partly moot, partly still needed.** [H.R. 252 (117th), Presidential Pardon Transparency Act of 2021](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-117hr252ih/xml/BILLS-117hr252ih.xml) (Krishnamoorthi, Jan. 11, 2021; also H.R. 3489, 115th; H.R. 1348, 116th; S. 318, 117th): "Not later than 3 days after the date on which the President grants any reprieve or pardon … the Attorney General shall publish in the Federal Register and on the official website of the President" the recipient's name, the date, and the full text. [S. 256 (119th), Pardon Transparency and Accountability Act of 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s256is/xml/BILLS-119s256is.xml) (Blumenthal, Jan. 25, 2025): the President must "publish in the Federal Register and on the official website of the President a written explanation of the reasons for granting such executive clemency" on the date of grant; Pardon Attorney "Justice Impact Statement" within 30 days, victim contact, lobbyist registration within 2 days. [H.J.Res.135 (119th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres135ih/xml/BILLS-119hjres135ih.xml) (amendment): notification to Congress within 3 days or the grant "shall have no force or effect." None has passed. These are the closest existing drafts of the enabling statute described in §3(d).

### 2. State law today

- **Structure (count and source).** The [Collateral Consequences Resource Center 50-state comparison](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/50-state-comparisoncharacteristics-of-pardon-authorities-2/) (verified in the 2026-08-17 review) counts **six** states where an independent board holds the pardon power outright (Alabama, Connecticut, Georgia, Idaho, South Carolina, Utah), **22** where the governor shares it with a board (sitting on it or gated by its recommendation), **eight** requiring mandatory but non-binding consultation, and **five** jurisdictions with no advisory process at all — D.C., Maine, Oregon, Wisconsin, and the federal government.
- **Reporting-with-reasons provisions (the Section 1 analogue).** Verified constitutional texts: [California art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V) ("stating the pertinent facts and the reasons for granting it"); [New York art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution) (annual report of each grant with name, crime, sentence, dates); [Delaware art. VII](https://delcode.delaware.gov/constitution/constitution-08.shtml) (governor to "fully set forth in writing the grounds of all reprieves, pardons and remissions … and laid before the General Assembly"); [Michigan art. V, §14](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-Article-V-14) ("He shall inform the legislature annually of each reprieve, commutation and pardon granted, stating reasons therefor"); [Ohio art. III, §11](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-constitution/section-3.11) ("communicate to the general assembly, at every regular session, each case of reprieve, commutation, or pardon granted, stating the name and crime of the convict, the sentence, its date, and the date of the commutation, pardon, or reprieve, with the Governor's reasons therefor"); [Wisconsin art. V, §6](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/misc/lrb/reading_the_constitution/exececutive_clemency_power_in_wis_7_3.pdf) (Legislative Reference Bureau reading: annual report of "each case of reprieve, commutation or pardon granted," including name, crime, "and the governor's reasons for granting clemency"). The [Council of State Governments Midwest (Feb. 13, 2021)](https://csgmidwest.org/2021/02/13/all-governors-have-clemency-powers-but-process-varies-in-midwest-because-of-distinct-rules-constitutional-language/) reports that "Governors in Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Ohio and Wisconsin must report their pardon decisions to the legislature." **Count to use publicly: "at least six state constitutions" (the six verified texts) require reporting with reasons; a full 50-state count of reasons-required reporting clauses was not located in a single citable source and should not be asserted.**
- **Board-gated and post-conviction-only designs** (Pennsylvania, Texas, Delaware, Florida; Texas "after conviction") are already in the memo. Note that no state constitution surveyed contains a family-kinship pardon bar or a "sold pardon is void" clause; the states' answer to sale has been structural (boards) rather than categorical.
- **State laws that would conflict with the article and become unenforceable: none identified.** The article regulates federal clemency: Section 3 names "the President," Section 5 refers to "this article," and Sections 1, 2, and 4 sit in an article about the Article II power. State clemency law is a reserved state power and would be unaffected. (See §3(f) for the drafting point that Sections 1, 2, and 4 do not themselves say "by the President.")

### 3. The day after ratification

**(a) What becomes unenforceable or moot.** No federal statute or regulation authorizes secret, unnamed, family, or sold pardons, so nothing on the federal books is struck; what changes is *doctrine and practice*. From certification: (i) an unpublished pardon has no effect (Section 1); (ii) a grant that does not name its recipient and specify offenses — the form of Proclamation 10887's "all other individuals" clause — is invalid as to unnamed persons (Section 2), and courts rather than repeal would say so, case by case as pardons are pleaded; (iii) a pardon to a spouse or relative within the third degree fails (Section 3); (iv) a sold or self-shielding pardon is void (Section 4), and a court can so hold on the public record without any statute (*Klein* does not bar a *constitutional* voidness rule). *Garland*/*Schick*'s "not subject to legislative control" survives except as the article itself provides. Prior categorical proclamations (Carter's 1977 draft amnesty; Proclamation 10887) are completed acts and are not reopened — see (c).

**(b) What stands.** Art. II's impeachment exception and federal-offenses-only limit; *Wilson* delivery, *Burdick* refusal, *Biddle* (commutations), *Grossman* (contempts), *Knote* (vested rights/Treasury), *Murphy* (individual preemptive pardons); 28 C.F.R. Part 1 in full (still advisory — the article does not constitutionalize the Pardon Attorney or any waiting period); 18 U.S.C. §201 as written; 44 U.S.C. §1505; every state clemency provision. Section 5 preserves the power "to any person not described herein," including commutations and reprieves for ordinary applicants.

**(c) The draft's own transition/enforcement/effective-date clauses.** There are none. The article has **no enforcement clause** ("Congress shall have power to enforce…" is absent), **no effective-date clause**, and **no transition clause**. The only timing text is Section 1's "No reprieve or pardon shall take effect until so published" — an internal condition on each grant, not a delayed effective date for the article. Consequences: the article applies to grants made after certification; grants completed before certification are governed by the finality rule ("when a pardon is complete, there is no power to revoke it," per the 1869 federal ruling cited in [PolitiFact, Dec. 4, 2025](https://politifact.com/factchecks/2025/dec/04/donald-trump/revoke-pardon-biden-autopen/)); and any grant signed before certification but not delivered/announced falls in a gap the courts would resolve (see (f)). Section 5 is a savings/construction clause, not an enforcement clause.

**(d) Enabling legislation Congress would need or likely pass.** Sections 1–4 are self-executing prohibitions and conditions; Congress is not required to act for them to bind. But Section 1 says a pardon "shall be published" and "shall be reported to Congress" in the passive voice, naming no publisher and no recipient. Absent a statute, the President would satisfy it however the President chose (a White House posting, a Federal Register notice, a letter to the presiding officers). Congress would therefore likely enact: (i) a publication statute designating the vehicle and deadline — the existing H.R. 252 model ("Federal Register and … the official website of the President," within 3 days) plus S. 256's "written explanation of the reasons" — with the CRS caution that a duty laid on the Attorney General/Pardon Attorney is on firmer ground than one laid on the President (post-ratification, the constitutional command itself supplies the footing the CRS memo found lacking); (ii) a transmission-to-Congress procedure (H.J.Res.135's 3-day notice to the President pro tempore and Speaker is the closest existing text; a statute could route it through the Clerk and Secretary); (iii) a bribery clarification stating that §201 covers the President and clemency (PODA title / No Payoffs for Pardons Act) so that Section 4's "may be punished as bribery" clause has an unambiguous statute behind it; (iv) optionally the memo's **Inherently statutory** items (record preservation, clemency-docket publication, lobbying disclosure). Congress's authority for (i)–(iii) would rest on the Necessary and Proper Clause and Article I, not on an enforcement clause the article does not have — a drafting gap worth flagging (see notes).

**(e) State "trigger" or conforming legislation.** The mechanism (a state statute effective on certification) is available but has no natural application here: the article governs federal clemency only, and no state law needs conforming. No actual state trigger law on this subject exists; none should be cited. The only plausible state activity is *parallel* reform of state clemency (e.g., Minnesota's 2023 board/commission restructuring, already in the memo), which is independent of ratification.

**(f) Litigation questions the wording leaves to the courts (candid list).**
1. **"Granted" vs. delivered vs. published.** Section 1 conditions *effect* on publication; *Wilson* conditions validity on delivery and acceptance. Is a pardon signed and delivered but unpublished a nullity, or merely suspended until published? Can a recipient publish it? What of a grant signed before certification and disclosed after?
2. **Scope of "reprieve or pardon."** Sections 1–4 say "reprieve or pardon"; Section 5 says "reprieves, pardons, or commutations." Under *Schick* and *Biddle* a commutation is a lesser included form of pardon, so courts would probably read Sections 1–4 to cover commutations and remissions — but the asymmetry invites the argument that commutations were deliberately left outside Sections 1–4.
3. **"A statement of the reasons."** No adequacy standard is stated (by design — the memo's disclosure-not-approval choice). Courts will be asked whether a one-line reason suffices; the drafting intent says yes, and the text supplies no reviewer.
4. **Sections 1, 2, and 4 do not say "by the President."** Read in context they reach only federal clemency; a literalist challenge to a state governor's grant is conceivable and should fail, but "by the President" would foreclose it.
5. **Section 2 and categorical grants.** Does a proclamation that attaches a named schedule of beneficiaries satisfy "name the individual"? Does "specify the offenses" require statutory citations or suffice with a description? The Wilson (D.C. Cir. 2025) dispute over Proclamation 10887's reach (memo, Review Additions) shows the litigation Section 2 is meant to end.
6. **Section 3's "third degree of kinship."** Civil-law versus canon-law computation (the §455(d)(2) civil-law rule is the obvious borrow); adoptive, step-, and half-relations; former spouses.
7. **Section 4's purpose clause.** "For the purpose of shielding the President from legal liability or advancing the President's private financial interest" is a motive test; *Trump v. United States* fn. 3 limits the evidence available against a President, so a challenger (who? — standing to attack a third party's pardon is itself open) may be confined to the public record. The transaction half ("in exchange for anything of value") is likelier to be litigated on ordinary bribery proof.
8. **Who may sue, and when.** Pardons are usually raised defensively (*Wilson*: "by plea, motion or otherwise"); a Section 1–4 defect would most often be adjudicated when a recipient pleads the pardon and the government or a court questions it. Whether Congress or a member could seek a declaration is unsettled.
