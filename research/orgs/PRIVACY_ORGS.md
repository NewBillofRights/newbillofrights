# Privacy — Organizational Landscape and Language Comparison

Companion to [PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md](../PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md) and Article 4 of [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md). Compiled 2026-08-19. Every factual claim below was opened at its source in this session; anything that could not be verified is listed at the end.

## Founder direction (2026-08-19, recorded verbatim)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

## The headline finding for this article

**No organization has drafted a federal constitutional privacy amendment.** Not one of the seed orgs, and none found in a search of congress.gov, GovTrack, and the advocacy landscape. This is the one amendment area in the package where the adopt-their-language instruction has no national org text to adopt, because none exists. PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md §"Prior Federal Privacy Amendment Proposals" reached the same conclusion from the congressional record; this pass confirms it from the organizational side.

What does exist, and what this memo recommends borrowing from, is a different and in some ways stronger asset: **state constitutional text that voters have already ratified by margins of 75%, 81%, and 89%**, plus **two model bills** (ACLU's state-level Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act; EPIC/Consumer Reports' State Data Privacy Act) that are statutory analogs of our §2 and §3 respectively. The national orgs are coalition and validation assets, not text sources.

A second finding worth the founder's attention: the New Hampshire legislature, drafting in public in 2018, made **exactly the narrowing edit our memo's decisional-autonomy trap note prescribes** — and did it under live opposition that the amendment was a stealth abortion measure. That is the closest thing to a real-world test of our §1 drafting choice that exists anywhere. Detail in §3.

## 1. The national organizations — what each actually offers

### American Civil Liberties Union

- **Identity:** Founded 1920. The c4 (American Civil Liberties Union, Inc., EIN 13-3871360) reported $146,699,874 revenue, $174,521,367 expenses, and $181,410,698 assets for FY2023. The c3 (ACLU Foundation, EIN 13-6213516) reported $185,146,988 revenue, $201,684,585 expenses, and $828,604,389 assets for FY2024. A separate national c4 entity (EIN 13-5628799) reported $4,965,830 revenue for FY2023. State affiliates file separately. ([ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/api/v2/organizations/133871360.json), [c3](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/api/v2/organizations/136213516.json))
- **Proposed language:** No constitutional amendment. But the ACLU publishes a **State-Level Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act** (document dated May 1, 2024), a complete model statute "Adapted from S.1265, 117th Congress (2021-2022)." Its operative rule, verbatim:

  > "In connection with any criminal, civil, or other investigatory or enforcement activity: (1) In exchange for anything of value, a law enforcement entity may not obtain or receive access to any covered individual's personal data from a third party."

  It defines "Obtain in exchange for anything of value" as "to obtain or receive access to personal data (1) in exchange for money or other valuable consideration, (2) in connection with services or benefits being provided as consideration, or (3) as part of the provision of a fee, including an access fee, service fee, maintenance fee, or licensing fee." It carries seven exceptions — a probable-cause warrant, exigent circumstances ("an emergency involving imminent danger of death or serious physical injury to a person that requires disclosure without delay"), data "lawfully available to the public through government records or widely distributed media," data the individual voluntarily made public, case-specific express consent (two forms), and data to or from the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. Its enforcement section states: "Any violation of this Act constitutes an injury, and any person may institute proceedings for injunctive relief, declaratory relief, a writ of mandate, and/or attorney fees," plus a suppression rule. ([ACLU model bill PDF](https://assets.aclu.org/live/uploads/2024/08/State-4th-Amendment-Is-Not-For-Sale-Act.pdf), [landing page](https://www.aclu.org/documents/aclu-state-level-model-fourth-amendment-is-not-for-sale-act))
- **Track record:** Statutory and litigation, not Article V. On the state side, the ACLU of Missouri was a named "Yes on 9" supporter of the 2014 amendment, and the ACLU of Michigan's political director backed Proposal 2 in 2020 (§3).
- **Partisan coding:** Codes left in general press framing, but on *government* surveillance it has co-signed with Americans for Prosperity and FreedomWorks (§2). Association on the government-facing side is the safest of any large org here; association on the commercial side codes left.

### Electronic Frontier Foundation

- **Identity:** Founded 1990 (IRS ruling date July 1990), San Francisco. FY2023: $17,954,838 revenue, $19,156,013 expenses, $46,707,648 assets. ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/api/v2/organizations/43091431.json))
- **Proposed language:** **None** — no constitutional amendment and no model statute published on its privacy issue page. EFF's method is litigation, agency comment, and bill-by-bill advocacy across a long list of issue areas (biometrics, locational privacy, face surveillance, mandatory data retention, §702). ([EFF privacy issues](https://www.eff.org/issues/privacy))
- **Track record:** Deep on the *Carpenter* line of doctrine and on state legislation; EFF documented Montana SB 282 (2025) as the first state law closing the law-enforcement data-broker loophole, which PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md §2 already treats as the enacted analog of our §2. ([EFF on Montana](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/05/montana-becomes-first-state-close-law-enforcement-data-broker-loophole))
- **Partisan coding:** Cross-partisan on government surveillance; codes left on commercial regulation (it favors a full private right of action and opt-in consent, both of which are the exact machinery that killed APRA).

### Electronic Privacy Information Center

- **Identity:** Founded 1994, Washington DC (IRS ruling August 2000). FY2023: $3,816,503 revenue, $3,310,717 expenses, $6,195,334 assets. ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/api/v2/organizations/522225921.json))

EPIC's Fourth Amendment issue page describes an amicus practice and names the Government Surveillance Reform Act as its priority; it contains no proposed constitutional text. ([EPIC Fourth Amendment](https://epic.org/issues/privacy-laws/fourth-amendment/)) EPIC does co-author, with **Consumer Reports**, the **State Data Privacy Act** model bill (published September 25, 2024; text updated April 2025), whose stated goals are to "Limit ubiquitous online tracking; Encourage more privacy-protective methods of online advertising; Protect the most sensitive data, including data about kids and teens; Use language from existing state laws; and Allow for meaningful enforcement of the law to ensure compliance." It is entirely commercial-facing — it does not address government access — and it includes a private right of action with a small-business exemption. ([EPIC/CR model bill](https://epic.org/the-state-data-privacy-act-a-proposed-model-state-privacy-bill/), [full text PDF](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/State-Data-Privacy-Act-EPIC-CR-April2025.pdf))

## 2. The left–right coalition behind the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act

This is the organizational evidence that our §2 sits on a real cross-spectrum floor rather than a coincidence of polling.

**The bill.** H.R. 4639 (118th), sponsored by Rep. Warren Davidson (R-OH-8), introduced July 14, 2023. Its operative prohibition, verbatim: "A law enforcement agency of a governmental entity and an element of the intelligence community may not obtain from a third party in exchange for anything of value a covered customer or subscriber record or any illegitimately obtained information." It defines the key phrase as "to obtain by purchasing, to receive in connection with services being provided for consideration, or to otherwise obtain in exchange for consideration, including an access fee, service fee, maintenance fee, or licensing fee." It passed the House 219–199 on April 17, 2024 and died in the Senate. ([H.R. 4639 text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/4639/text))

**The coalition.** The January 26, 2022 sign-on letter to the Judiciary Committee chairs and ranking members of both chambers carried 50 organizations. The full signatory list, verbatim from the letter: Access Now; Advocacy for Principled Action in Government; Afghans for a Better Tomorrow; American Civil Liberties Union; American Muslim Empowerment Network; **Americans for Prosperity**; Brennan Center for Justice at NYU School of Law; CAIR Minnesota; Center for Democracy & Technology; Center for Human Rights and Privacy; Constitutional Alliance; Defending Rights & Dissent; Demand Progress; Due Process Institute; Electronic Frontier Foundation; Electronic Privacy Information Center; Fight for the Future; Free Press Action; **FreedomWorks**; Global Network Initiative; Government Information Watch; Indivisible Plus Washington; Indivisible Washington's 8th District; Just Futures Law; Kairos Action; Media Alliance; Muslim Justice League; National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers; National Coalition Against Censorship; New America's Open Technology Institute; OCA Greater Chicago; Oakland Privacy; Open Media; Open the Government; PEN America; ProgressNow New Mexico; Project for Privacy and Surveillance Accountability; Project On Government Oversight; **R Street**; Ranking Digital Rights; Restore the Fourth; RootsAction.org; S.T.O.P. – The Surveillance Technology Oversight Project; Secure Justice; United We Dream; Wallingford Indivisible; Woodhull Freedom Foundation; X-Lab. ([Coalition letter PDF](https://www.brennancenter.org/sites/default/files/2022-01/FANFSA%20sign-on%20letter%201-26-22.pdf), [Brennan Center page](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/coalition-letter-calls-congressional-hearings-fourth-amendment-not-sale))

**What it establishes for us.** Americans for Prosperity, FreedomWorks, and R Street signing the same letter as the ACLU, Color of Change, and United We Dream is the kind of full-spectrum evidence the foundation's bar asks for — and it is *organizational* evidence, independent of the polling. It also tells us the coalition is issue-scoped: these groups align on the government-purchase rule specifically, not on commercial data regulation, where AFP and the ACLU are opponents. Our §2/§3 split tracks that seam precisely.

## 3. State constitutional campaigns — the ratified language

This is where the "already-invested language" actually lives. Three states put government-facing informational-privacy text to voters in the last twelve years and won by landslides, with cross-partisan legislative coalitions in each.

### Missouri Amendment 9 (August 5, 2014) — 74.75%

- **Result:** 729,752 yes (74.75%) to 246,515 no (25.25%), certified. ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Missouri_Electronic_Data_Protection,_Amendment_9_(August_2014)))
- **Route:** SJR 27, sponsored by Sen. Rob Schaaf (R-34), cosponsored by Sen. Bob Dixon (R-30). Senate 31–1 (April 1, 2014); House 114–28 (May 16, 2014).
- **The text, verbatim** (amended Art. I §15; bold added, bracketed removed, as the Secretary of State published it):

  > "Section 15. That the people shall be secure in their persons, papers, homes [and], effects, **and electronic communications and data,** from unreasonable searches and seizures; and no warrant to search any place, or seize any person or thing, **or access electronic data or communication,** shall issue without describing the place to be searched, or the person or thing to be seized, **or the data or communication to be accessed,** as nearly as may be; nor without probable cause, supported by written oath or affirmation."

- **The ballot title voters answered, verbatim:** "Shall the Missouri Constitution be amended so that the people shall be secure in their electronic communications and data from unreasonable searches and seizures as they are now likewise secure in their persons, homes, papers and effects?"
- **The coalition — the striking part.** Named supporters were Protect Our Privacy, OffNow, the **ACLU of Missouri**, United for Missouri, the **American Legislative Exchange Council**, and the Constitution Party of Missouri. ALEC and the ACLU on the same side of a ratified constitutional amendment is as clean a full-spectrum artifact as this research has produced in any category. Ballotpedia identified only one organizational opponent, the Southwest Missouri Democrats, and the opposition argument was cybercrime-investigation difficulty.

### Michigan Proposal 2 (November 3, 2020) — 88.75%

- **Result:** 4,472,671 yes (88.75%) to 567,130 no (11.25%), officially certified. ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Michigan_Proposal_2,_Search_Warrant_for_Electronic_Data_Amendment_(2020)); [Michigan certified results](https://mielections.us/election/results/2020GEN_CENR.html))
- **Route:** SJR G, introduced by Sen. Jim Runestad (R-15) on June 12, 2019. **Senate 38–0** (June 11, 2020) — 22 Republicans and 16 Democrats, no dissent. **House 106–0** (June 24, 2020), 3 not voting. A two-thirds requirement met unanimously in both chambers. ([Senate Fiscal Agency analysis](https://www.senate.michigan.gov/SFA/Publications/BallotProps/Proposal20-2.pdf))
- **The text, verbatim** (amended Art. I §11; the added words are "and electronic data and electronic communications" and "or to access electronic data or electronic communications"):

  > "The person, houses, papers, possessions, and electronic data and electronic communications of every person shall be secure from unreasonable searches and seizures. No warrant to search any place or to seize any person or things or to access electronic data or electronic communications shall issue without describing them, nor without probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation."

- **Coalition:** Supporters spanned Sen. Runestad (R), the Green Party of Michigan, the North Oakland Democratic Club, Americans for Tax Reform, and the ACLU of Michigan. Ballotpedia identified no organized opposition and no opposing editorial boards.
- **The fiscal finding worth quoting to skeptics.** The Senate Fiscal Agency concluded Proposal 20-2 "would have a negligible fiscal impact on State and local law enforcement agencies, as it is current practice that agencies seek search warrants to gain access to electronic data or electronic communications." That is a neutral legislative-branch analyst answering the administrative-disruption objection directly — useful for our §2 trap note.

### New Hampshire Question 2 (November 6, 2018) — 81%

Note: the task brief called this "Q1"; the measure was **Question 2** on the 2018 ballot. Corrected here.

- **Result:** 409,325 yes to 96,019 no (about 81%). New Hampshire requires two-thirds of those voting. ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/New_Hampshire_Question_2,_Right_to_Live_Free_from_Governmental_Intrusion_in_Private_and_Personal_Information_Amendment_(2018)))
- **Route:** CACR 16, sponsored by Rep. Neal Kurk (R, Hillsborough 2) and Rep. Robert Cushing (D, Rockingham 21) — LegiScan classifies it as a bipartisan bill. House 235–96 (February 22, 2018); Senate 15–9 (May 2, 2018).
- **The enacted text, verbatim** (new Part I, Art. 2-b):

  > "[Art.] 2-b. [Right to Privacy.] An individual's right to live free from governmental intrusion in private or personal information is natural, essential, and inherent."

- **The drafting history is the finding.** CACR 16 **as introduced** read:

  > "[Art.] 2-b. [Right to Privacy.] An individual's right to live free from governmental interference in private or personal matters is fundamental."

  House Amendment 0118h, adopted February 22, 2018, made three changes at once: "interference" → "**intrusion**"; "private or personal **matters**" → "private or personal **information**"; and "is fundamental" → "is **natural, essential, and inherent**." ([as introduced](https://legiscan.com/NH/text/CACR16/id/1656833); [as amended](https://legiscan.com/NH/text/CACR16/id/1758741))

  The first two changes are precisely the narrowing our memo's **decisional-autonomy trap** note prescribes for our §1 — moving the right off "matters" (which reads as substantive autonomy) and onto "information" (which keeps it in Fourth-Amendment-adjacent territory). A state legislature reached that drafting judgment independently, in public, on a live record.
- **And the trap fired anyway.** Cornerstone Action PAC campaigned against Question 2 on exactly the ground our memo anticipates: "Privacy matters. So does the right to life. Don't let a ballot question trick you into picking the former over the latter. If we are to have a right to privacy explicitly recognized in the New Hampshire constitution, it must be abortion neutral. Say no to Question 2." The lesson for us is two-sided: the narrowing edit did **not** prevent the attack, but the amendment still carried about 81% with the attack in the field. This is the best available evidence on how much the abortion shadow actually costs an informational-privacy framing — and it is much less than the framing risk alone would suggest. It also argues for our §7 savings clause doing more explicit work than NH's text does, since NH has no savings clause at all.

### Massachusetts Right to Privacy Amendment (2026) — a cautionary text

An initiative petition for a constitutional privacy amendment (#22-12) was filed for the Massachusetts 2026 cycle by an individual, Kirstin Beatty, with no organizational sponsor identified; it did not qualify. Its text is worth reading precisely because it does almost everything our trap notes warn against — it borrows Montana's compelling-interest standard, then adds "ambient privacy," a constitutionally mandated private right of action barring arbitration, constitutional-level business data-minimization and consent mechanics, a definition list naming "gait recognition" and "keystroke dynamics," and a legislative mandate. Verbatim opening:

> "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest, of which convenience and financial returns have no part. Nor shall any compelling state interest be lightly provided, without evidence and justification. The right to privacy is the right to ambient privacy, not only right to privacy in one's home. For transgressions on this right, the people shall have a private right of action which cannot be replaced by arbitration."

It does carry a savings clause covering public records, officials' conflicts of interest, archiving, and "the liberty of the press" — the one element our §7 shares. ([Petition text, Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/doc/22-12-initiative-petition-for-a-constitutional-amendment-for-privacy/download); [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Massachusetts_Right_to_Privacy_Amendment_(2026)))

This is the concrete illustration of the memo's "constitutionalize principles, not machinery" rule: a draft that puts arbitration clauses and biometric definitions into a constitution, filed with no organizational backing, that went nowhere.

## 4. Language comparison against our Article 4

There is no rival *amendment* to compare section by section, so the comparison runs against the four texts that exist: the three ratified state amendments and the ACLU model statute (with the EPIC/CR model bill as the commercial-side reference).

### Section by section

| Our § | Nearest existing language | Coverage relative to ours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **§1** (freestanding right; aggregation/third-party clause) | NH Art. 2-b (whole-sentence right over "private or personal information"); MO/MI (only within search-and-seizure clauses) | NH is a one-sentence right and nothing more: no enumeration, no aggregation clause, no third-party rule. MO/MI do not create a freestanding right at all — they extend an existing search clause. **Nothing in the existing corpus states our second sentence** (the *Carpenter*/anti-third-party-doctrine rule). Ours is broader on every axis. |
| **§2** (acquisition/purchase rule + bright line) | ACLU model statute §2; H.R. 4639 | Very close on the purchase rule — the ACLU model's "in exchange for anything of value… may not obtain or receive access to any covered individual's personal data from a third party" is our first sentence in statutory dress, and its exceptions map onto our "judicial authorization, consent, or… exigent circumstances." **Neither contains our second sentence** (the flat ban on generalized suspicionless collection). The ACLU model adds two things ours delegates: a suppression rule and an AG purge-procedure duty. |
| **§3** (access, correction, purpose limitation; legislative power over private holders) | NH Art. 2-b (informational framing only); EPIC/CR State Data Privacy Act (commercial only) | No state constitution contains access/correction or purpose limitation. The EPIC/CR bill covers the commercial suite our third sentence delegates, but is statutory and government-silent. **Ours is the only text uniting the government and commercial sides.** |
| **§4** (sensitive core, "as further defined by law") | None constitutional; EPIC/CR bill has a statutory sensitive-data tier | No existing constitutional text enumerates a sensitive core. Ours is unique here — and deliberately open-ended where the MA petition froze its list. |
| **§5** (quality-of-law + tiered compelling-interest review) | Montana Art. II §10 (single-tier compelling interest); MA petition (copies Montana, adds nothing) | Montana is the only US precedent and it is **single-tier** — the exact configuration our single-tier trap note rejects. Ours tiers it and adds the ECHR "public, accessible, and foreseeable" legality requirement, which no US text has. |
| **§6** (redress, equitable relief; further remedies to legislatures) | ACLU model §3 ("Any violation of this Act constitutes an injury, and any person may institute proceedings for injunctive relief, declaratory relief, a writ of mandate, and/or attorney fees") | Closest match in the corpus, and it does more than ours: the ACLU model also mandates suppression. Ours deliberately leaves suppression and damages to legislatures — the litigation-bonanza trap note. |
| **§7** (speech/press savings + enforcement power) | MA petition's savings clause (press, public records, archiving); state amendments have none | The three ratified amendments carry **no savings clause and no enforcement clause**. Ours is more complete. |
| **§8** (prospective retention/use) | None anywhere | Unique to ours. No state text addresses the transition. |

### If we adopted their language 100%

| | Gain | Lose | Would need to add back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Adopt NH Art. 2-b as our §1** | Text ratified by ~81% under live abortion-framed attack; 21 words; unimpeachable "already-invested" provenance | The aggregation/third-party clause (our highest-value sentence per the memo); the enumeration of home/communications/movements/information; "unreasonable" and the two centuries of doctrine it imports | Effectively all of our §1's second sentence and most of the first |

| **Adopt the ACLU model statute as our §2** | Precise, tested drafting; definitions; seven worked exceptions; a coalition that already owns it | The bright-line ban on generalized suspicionless collection (the clause the memo flags as immune to balancing); constitutional altitude — it is a statute, with statutory-grade specificity that ages badly in a constitution | Our §2's second sentence; and we would have to strip the definition list and the suppression mandate |
| **Adopt the MA petition text** | Nothing we do not already have | Nearly every trap note simultaneously | Not recommended in any part except its press/public-records savings instinct, which our §7 already covers |

**The synthesis.** None of these is adoptable whole. Two are adoptable in part, and the borrowing is more valuable as *vocabulary* than as wholesale text: NH's "private or personal information" is the phrase that survived a ratification campaign against the abortion attack, and MO/MI's "electronic communications and data" is the phrase that carried 75% and 89%. Our §1 and §3 currently say "personal information," which is close to NH's already. The strongest evidence-backed change available is to align our vocabulary with the ratified phrasings where doing so costs nothing in substance.

## 5. Adversaries

There is no organized opposition to a constitutional privacy amendment, because there is no amendment to oppose. The opposition that exists attaches to the components:

- **The executive branch, under both parties — the most consequential adversary.** Already documented in PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md's Review Additions: the Biden White House "strongly oppose[d]" the §702 warrant amendment in April 2024 as "exceedingly narrow and unworkable in practice," and in April 2026 the Trump administration sought a clean 18-month extension without a warrant amendment. This is an institutional adversary, not a partisan one, which is the veil-of-ignorance argument for our §2 rather than an argument against it.
- **The business coalition, on the commercial side only.** The U.S. Chamber of Commerce's stated red line (2022) is opposition to legislation "that fails to provide meaningful preemption or any proposal that creates a blanket private right of action," and it led a 20-organization letter against APRA in June 2024. The **State Privacy and Security Coalition** is the multi-industry group working the same position in the states. Our §3's third sentence delegates the commercial suite to legislatures rather than creating a self-executing right, which is the design choice that keeps this adversary aimed at statutes rather than at us — but the Chamber-facing question flagged in the memo's gate-check note (whether §6's "redress in the courts" reads as a constitutional private right of action) is exactly where this opposition would concentrate. ([Chamber 2022](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/data-privacy/u-s-chamber-warns-it-will-oppose-any-privacy-legislation-that-creates-a-blanket-private-right-of-action); [SPSC](https://thespsc.org/))
- **Pro-life organizations, on the framing.** Cornerstone Action PAC's Question 2 campaign is the documented instance. The result — about 81% yes anyway — is the best available measure of the cost.
- **Law-enforcement objections, which are argument rather than organization.** In both Missouri and Michigan the opposing argument was cybercrime-investigation difficulty (Missouri) or redundancy (Michigan), advanced by individual legislators and analysts rather than by a campaign. No law-enforcement association campaign against either measure was found.

## 6. Partnership and conflict

- **Safe to align with, on the government-facing side:** the whole FANFSA coalition. It is pre-validated as full-spectrum by its own signatory list, and endorsing H.R. 4639-style policy codes us with Americans for Prosperity and R Street as much as with the ACLU. Due Process Institute and Restore the Fourth are the two whose association carries the least partisan coding of any org in the field; PPSA and Demand Progress are a natural pairing to cite together.
- **Handle with scoping, on the commercial side:** EFF's and EPIC's commercial-privacy positions (full private right of action, opt-in consent) are the exact machinery that killed ADPPA and APRA and that the Chamber names as its red line. Citing EPIC's model bill as *evidence of the settled substantive core* is safe; endorsing it is not, and would trade our §3 delegation design for a side in a live statutory fight.
- **No Article V conflict exists here.** Unlike campaign finance and term limits, no privacy organization is pursuing a convention route, so there is no convention-versus-Congress split to navigate and no group whose Article V position would code us. This is a materially cleaner partnership landscape than the other articles.
- **The state-campaign relationship is the most promising and the least developed.** Missouri's and Michigan's campaigns were legislature-led with ad hoc committees ("Protect Our Privacy," "Yes on 9") that no longer operate. The reusable assets are the ratified texts and the vote records, not living organizations. Supporting a future state amendment on the Michigan/Missouri model is the Brazil-sequencing move the memo recommends — and it is currently unclaimed by any national org.
- **One caution on endorsing the ACLU model statute.** It is genuinely close to our §2 and adopting a supportive posture toward it is low-risk. But its suppression mandate and fee-shifting are precisely what our §6 declines to constitutionalize, so any endorsement should be of the purchase rule, not of the enforcement architecture, or we undercut our own drafting rationale.
