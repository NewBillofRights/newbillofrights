# Term Limits — Organizational Landscape and Language Comparison

*Research pass 2026-08-19. Covers [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) Article 2 in all four of its parts: congressional term limits (§1), the eighteen-year Supreme Court seat with a confirmation-refusal default (§2), a judicial retirement age at seventy-five (§3), and the anti-evasion clause (§4). Companion to [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md). Every factual claim below was verified at its source in this session; page snapshots are indexed in [research/cache/index.jsonl](../cache/index.jsonl).*

## Founder direction (recorded verbatim, 2026-08-19)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

**The finding this memo returns on that direction:** Article 2 is the one article in the corpus where no single organization's text is adoptable, because no organization drafts the article we have. The landscape is split cleanly in two. Congressional term limits are owned by U.S. Term Limits, whose amendment is drafted, sponsored in both chambers, and pledged to by candidates — but at three House terms, not six, and USTL's pledge language forecloses our number by name. Supreme Court terms are owned by a statutory-route coalition (Fix the Court, Brennan Center) whose leading vehicles are bills, not amendments, and whose model text contains the deemed-confirmation clause our own memo expressly declines to draft. The two halves have never been drafted together by anyone. Two texts — the Welch–Manchin joint resolution and the National Constitution Center's Amendment XXXI — are close enough to our §2 to be worth clause-level borrowing, and one of them is the only cross-ideological drafted amendment text in the whole field.

## Per-org detail

### 1. U.S. Term Limits — the founder's named priority org

**Identity.** Founded 1992, headquartered in Washington, D.C. Two entities: U.S. Term Limits, Inc., a 501(c)(4) (EIN 52-1701352, listed in Bala Cynwyd, PA) with $7,617,749 in 2024 revenue, and the U.S. Term Limits Foundation, a 501(c)(3) (EIN 52-1729739, Washington DC) with $2,064,535 revenue and $2,058,082 in assets in 2024, tax-exempt since June 1991. President Philip Blumel; board chair Marshall Stocker; executive director Nick Tomboulides. It is the plaintiff in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton* (1995) — the case that made an amendment the only road, and the case our own §1 exists because of.

**Their amendment text — verbatim.** USTL's about page states without qualification that Rep. Ralph Norman and Sen. Ted Cruz "are sponsoring the USTL amendment on Capitol Hill." The vehicles are [H.J.Res. 12](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/12/text) (introduced January 6, 2025) and its identical Senate companion [S.J.Res. 1](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-joint-resolution/1/text) (January 7, 2025):

> "**section 1.** No person who has served 3 terms as a Representative shall be eligible for election to the House of Representatives. For purposes of this section, the election of a person to fill a vacancy in the House of Representatives shall be included as 1 term in determining the number of terms that such person has served as a Representative if the person fills the vacancy for more than 1 year.
>
> "**section 2.** No person who has served 2 terms as a Senator shall be eligible for election or appointment to the Senate. For purposes of this section, the election or appointment of a person to fill a vacancy in the Senate shall be included as 1 term in determining the number of terms that such person has served as a Senator if the person fills the vacancy for more than 3 years.
>
> "**section 3.** No term beginning before the date of the ratification of this article shall be taken into account in determining eligibility for election or appointment under this article."

**The pledge, and why the number is not negotiable for them.** USTL's [congressional pledge](https://www.termlimits.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2024-Congressional-Pledge.pdf) reads: "I, ___, pledge that as a member of Congress, I will cosponsor and vote for the U.S. Term Limits Amendment of three (3) two-year House terms and two (2) six-year Senate terms **and no longer limit**." The final four words are the operative ones for us. The pledge does not merely prefer 3/2; it commits signatories to oppose a longer limit. Our six House terms is a "longer limit" within the plain meaning of the pledge every USTL-endorsed member has signed. This is the same fracture the 1995 floor fight produced, and [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) already recorded that "U.S. Term Limits famously scored the 12-year version as a betrayal."

**Route and track record.** Two tracks at once. On the congressional track, the Cruz–Norman resolution has been reintroduced each Congress since 2017; its 118th-Congress predecessor got a House Judiciary markup on September 28, 2023 and was voted down 17–19. On the Article V track, USTL's [progress page](https://termlimits.com/progress/) (last modified August 11, 2026) claims **fourteen states** have passed its single-subject congressional term limits application — Florida, Alabama, Missouri, West Virginia, Oklahoma, Wisconsin, Tennessee, Louisiana, North Carolina, South Dakota, Indiana, South Carolina, Kansas, and Arizona — against the 34 required, with resolutions introduced in at least fifteen states during 2026. It separately claims twenty states have passed congressional-term-limits language inside multi-subject applications, which it does not count toward the fourteen. *Treat the count as advocacy-sourced.* Wikipedia's list of the same states, last edited May 11, 2026, names thirteen (omitting Kansas) while its prose says "about 14," and press coverage of the Kansas resolution in early 2026 variously called Kansas the thirteenth and the sixteenth state. The model application USTL circulates is [ALEC's](https://alec.org/model-policy/draft-model-application-under-article-v-of-the-u-s-constitution-to-propose-a-congressional-term-limits-amendment/), and note what it does *not* do: it applies for a convention "to set a limit on the number of terms" without naming any number. The number is settled by the pledge, not by the application.

**Partisan coding.** Genuinely mixed at the sponsor level and asymmetrically so. H.J.Res. 12's twenty-eight House cosponsors include three Democrats — Landsman, Perez, and Golden of Maine. S.J.Res. 1's twelve Senate cosponsors are all Republicans. USTL's own hall of fame pairs Ron DeSantis and Bruce Rauner with Pat Quinn. The organization describes itself as non-partisan and its issue polls with the smallest partisan gap we have measured (90% R / 86% D, Pew 2023). But its Article V vehicle travels through ALEC and shares state-legislative sponsors with Convention of States, and its rhetorical register ("Power to the People, Not the Politicians") is not ours.

### 2. Convention of States Action

**Identity.** Founded 2013 by Mark Meckler and Michael Farris; 501(c)(4), EIN 47-2245708, Houston TX; $7,565,773 revenue against $7,476,523 expenses in 2024. Meckler is president and CEO ($209,084); Richard J. Santorum is listed as senior advisor at $335,483.

**No drafted amendment text.** COS has an *application*, not an amendment. Its resolution asks for a convention limited to proposals that "limit the power and jurisdiction of the federal government, impose fiscal restraints, and place term limits on federal officials." What those term limits would be — how many terms, which officials, whether judges count — is left entirely to the convention. There is no COS model amendment to adopt, and this is by design.

**Track record.** Twenty states as of Kansas on January 22, 2026, per its own [progress page](https://conventionofstates.com/states-that-have-passed-the-convention-of-states-article-v-application): Georgia, Alaska, Florida, Alabama, Tennessee, Indiana, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Arizona, North Dakota, Texas, Missouri, Arkansas, Utah, Mississippi, Wisconsin, Nebraska, West Virginia, South Carolina, Kansas. Fourteen more needed.

**Partisan coding — the disqualifying finding.** COS is coded right by its own materials, not by opponents' characterization. Its petition page offers "How did you hear about us" options consisting almost entirely of conservative media figures and outlets, and its "Physicians for COS" testimonial is an argument against the Affordable Care Act. Whatever the merits of its three subjects, association with COS would code the foundation in exactly the way [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) exists to prevent. **Recommendation: no partnership, no citation as a fellow traveler.**

### 3. Fix the Court

**Identity.** 501(c)(3), EIN 86-1840317, Brooklyn NY, founded 2014. Revenue $201,039 in the 2025–26 filing — two orders of magnitude smaller than USTL or COS. It is an expert shop, not a grassroots operation.

**No drafted amendment — and that is their position, not an omission.** Fix the Court's [term limits page](https://fixthecourt.com/fix/term-limits/) argues at length that 18-year terms can be achieved *by statute*, because a term-limited justice keeps the office and merely rotates duties as a "senior justice," which they contend satisfies "good Behaviour." Their [model statute outline](https://fixthecourt.com/2020/09/tl-statute/) has four elements: biennial appointments in odd-numbered years; 18-year terms for future justices with sitting justices exempt (so the Court may exceed nine for a time); a senior justice role permitting service on any federal court; and senior justices returning to the Court, in reverse order of seniority, to fill vacancies.

**The vehicle they endorse contains the clause we reject.** Fix the Court's site banners [H.R. 1074](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1074/text) (Khanna, February 6, 2025) as its endorsed bill. Its proposed new 28 U.S.C. § 9 reads:

> "**§ 9. Waiver of advice and consent authority.** If the Senate does not exercise its advice and consent authority with respect to a President's nominee to the Supreme Court within 120 days after the nomination, the Senate shall be deemed to have waived its advice and consent authority with respect to such nominee, and the nominee shall be seated as a Justice of the Supreme Court."

That is the **deemed-confirmed variant**, which [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) §(iii) instructs in terms: "Do **not** draft the deemed-confirmed variant, and do not let the clause be described as one." Our §2 answers the same problem in the opposite direction — the Senate keeps the full power to reject and loses only the ability to make refusal pay.

**Partisan coding.** H.R. 1074's nine cosponsors are all Democrats. Fix the Court's framing ("Our freedoms sit on a razor's edge," the Court is "polarized along partisan lines") is register we could not reproduce under [COPY_VOICE_GUIDE.md](../../COPY_VOICE_GUIDE.md). The organization is a serious research source; it is not a cross-partisan partner.

### 4. Brennan Center for Justice

**Position: statute, not amendment.** The Brennan Center's [policy solution](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/policy-solutions/supreme-court-term-limits) (Bannon and Milov-Cordoba, June 20, 2023) sets out the same senior-justice architecture and rests on the claim that Congress can act by statute — a position it says more than fifty constitutional scholars and retired judges have affirmed. Its July 2026 [model state resolution](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/policy-solutions/model-state-resolution-urging-18-year-term-limits-supreme-court-justices) is a memorializing resolution asking Congress to legislate, whose operative line is a single sentence: "This legislature supports the establishment of term limits of 18 years of active service for justices of the United States Supreme Court." There is no Brennan amendment text.

**One design element runs directly against ours.** Brennan lists as an advantage that under its scheme "an unexpected death or retirement would not create a new seat to fill; instead, a senior justice would temporarily step in." Our §2 does the reverse on purpose: the seat sits empty and the Court proceeds "as then constituted." Their backfill preserves nine at all times and removes the cost of delay; our knock-out imposes that cost deliberately, which is the whole mechanism.

**Two facts worth carrying regardless of partnership.** First, the Brennan Center's finding that 339 of 344 state supreme court justices serve limited terms, face mandatory retirement, or both — 49 of 50 states — is the strongest domestic precedent evidence for both §2 and §3, and it is already cited in our memo. Second, Brennan is a signatory to the Common Cause coalition statement opposing *any* Article V convention (below), while advocating SCOTUS term limits by statute. That is not incoherent, but it means Brennan is simultaneously an evidentiary ally on §2 and an adversary on one of the two routes by which §2 could be enacted.

### 5. The National Constitution Center drafting project — the cross-ideological amendment

**What it is.** Not an advocacy organization: a project of the National Constitution Center (Philadelphia), funded by Jeff Yass, which in 2020 commissioned three teams — libertarian, progressive, and conservative — to draft ideal constitutions, then reconvened them for a virtual convention on August 29 and 31, 2022 to agree on amendments. Five emerged. The first is Supreme Court term limits.

The signatories are the reason this matters. Team Libertarian: Ilya Shapiro (Manhattan Institute), Timothy Sandefur (Goldwater Institute), Christina Mulligan (Brooklyn Law). Team Progressive: Caroline Fredrickson (Georgetown), Jamal Greene (Columbia). Team Conservative: Ilan Wurman (ASU), Robert P. George (Princeton), Michael McConnell (Stanford), Colleen A. Sheehan (ASU). Robert George and Jamal Greene agreeing on constitutional text is a cross-partisan fact of a kind nothing else in this landscape offers.

**Their text — [Amendment XXXI, Appointments](https://constitutioncenter.org/media/files/The_Proposed_Amendments_AMENDMENTS.pdf), the term paragraph verbatim:**

> "There shall be nine judges of the supreme court, who shall hold their offices for staggered terms of eighteen years, such that every two years there shall be a vacancy. In the event of a vacancy resulting from death, resignation, impeachment, or other inability to perform the duties of the office, a new judge shall be appointed for the duration of the term only. After a term of office has expired, the judge whose term has expired may elect to sit on an inferior court during good behavior, which court is to be determined by the Chief Justice or as Congress shall direct."

**Their confirmation default, verbatim, from the same amendment's §1:**

> "Nominations shall be deemed to have received the advice and consent of the Senate unless disapproved by majority vote within three months of the nomination; but any Senator shall have the right to bring any nomination to the floor for debate and vote prior to that time. Any nomination made within the last three months of the President's term shall lapse at the end of that term, unless sooner approved by the Senate."

So the one cross-ideological drafted text in the field also chose **deemed confirmation** — the variant we reject — and extended it to *all* appointments, not just justices. Two independent, ideologically opposite groups (the NCC teams and the Khanna–Fix the Court camp) reached for the same tool our memo rules out. That is a finding worth stating plainly on the page: our knock-out clause is not the consensus answer to the Garland problem, it is a minority answer, and the case for it rests on the argument our memo already makes rather than on anyone else's endorsement.

**And its transition is the opposite of ours.** Amendment XXXI §3 requires the senior-most sitting justice to retire by the next presidential inauguration, then the most senior remaining justice every two years for sixteen years. That is a scheduled removal of the sitting bench — precisely the grandfathering trap our memo names, and the reason our §2 exempts every justice in office at ratification.

### 6. Welch–Manchin — the closest amendment-route analogue to our §2

Filed December 2024 in the 118th Congress by Sen. Peter Welch (D-VT) and Sen. Joe Manchin (I-WV); the [full joint resolution text](https://www.welch.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Proposed-Constitutional-Amendment-to-Establish-Supreme-Court-Term-Limits.pdf) is published on Welch's Senate site. Manchin has since left the Senate, so the vehicle currently has no Senate sponsor and was never assigned a resolution number in the published draft.

Eight sections. §1 fixes the Court at nine. §2 starts the first post-ratification term on July 1 of the first odd-numbered year after ratification, ending June 30 eighteen years later. §3 runs each subsequent seat's term from July 1 of the next odd-numbered year, expressly excluding remainder appointees from resetting the sequence. §§4–5 handle the transition from the sitting bench. §6 gives remainder-of-term appointment for post-ratification vacancies. §7 makes the Chief Justiceship rotate to the most senior sitting justice. §8 makes the term non-renewable.

Its endorsers are the cross-partisan asset here: retired Seventh Circuit Judge Diane P. Wood (director of the American Law Institute), Kermit Roosevelt (Penn), and Richard Painter — chief White House ethics lawyer in the George W. Bush administration. Manchin's own framing, on the record: "This is not a progressive idea."

Its gap is precisely ours: **no confirmation-refusal default at all.** [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) already made this point about the Whitehouse–Booker statute, and it applies with equal force here — a biennial calendar with no answer for Senate inaction is a calendar any Senate can stop.

### 7. Judicial retirement age (our §3) — the finding is that nobody advocates it federally

Searched for and did not find any national organization advocating a mandatory retirement age for federal judges. The activity is entirely at state level and runs the *other* way: bar associations and legislatures pushing to *raise* existing state caps from 70 to 75 (Maryland 2025–26, New Jersey, North Carolina, New Hampshire). The Maryland State Bar Association's posture is neutral monitoring. The federal-level advocates of judicial tenure reform (Fix the Court, Brennan) pursue fixed terms and do not propose an age cap.

**A sixth ballot result, which the memo's five do not include.** The [New Hampshire Increase Mandatory Judicial Retirement Age Amendment](https://ballotpedia.org/New_Hampshire_Increase_Mandatory_Judicial_Retirement_Age_Amendment_(2024)), on the ballot November 5, 2024, would have raised the cap from 70 to 75. It was **defeated with 452,307 yes (65.60%) to 237,221 no (34.40%)**, officially certified — because New Hampshire requires two-thirds for constitutional amendments, and 65.60% fell about 1.07 points short. This case does not run the same direction as the five in [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md): a clear majority of New Hampshire voters *did* want the age raised. **The "five certified ballot results… one direction" line should be revised before it reaches a public page.** The honest formulation is that in six certified results across both partisan leans, voters have never *approved* loosening a judicial retirement age except in Pennsylvania in 2016 (51.09%), and in New Hampshire a 65.60% majority to loosen was defeated only by a supermajority rule.

### 8. Congressional judicial-term-limits vehicles beyond the advocacy orgs

Two more drafted amendment texts exist with no organization behind them, and they are useful for the partisan-symmetry file:

- **[H.J.Res. 174](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/174/text)** (Olszewski, D-MD, May 4, 2026), one paragraph: "The tenure in office of a justice of the Supreme Court may not exceed 18 years. In the case of any justice who is serving as of the ratification of this amendment, if the tenure in office of that justice is 18 years or more, that term of that justice shall be terminated. If such a justice is the Chief Justice, the position shall be filled in accordance with law." It reaches sitting justices, has no staggering rule, no biennial calendar, and no confirmation default. It is the grandfathering trap in its purest form.
- **Rep. Tom Barrett (R-MI)**, [draft joint resolution dated January 29, 2026](https://barrett.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/barrett.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/final_20-year-term-limit-fed-judges.pdf): "Each judge of the Supreme Court and each inferior court shall be appointed to serve during good behavior for a term of 20 years," non-renewable in the same court, applying "only to an appointment occurring on or after the date of the ratification of this article." Twenty years rather than eighteen, and it reaches all Article III judges — but it is prospective, and it is a Republican member drafting judicial term limits, which is the single most useful data point in this memo for the claim that §2 is not a one-party proposal.

Also worth recording: Pennsylvania **HR 21**, described by the John Birch Society's own alert as "a bipartisan resolution," applies for conventions both on congressional term limits *and* on Supreme Court justice term limits — the two halves of our Article 2 in one state application, which is the closest thing in the field to a state-level endorsement of our bundle.

## Language comparison against our draft, section by section

### Our §1 vs. the USTL amendment (S.J.Res. 1 / H.J.Res. 12)

| | Ours | USTL |
|---|---|---|
| House cap | six terms (12 years) | three terms (6 years) |
| Senate cap | twice (12 years) | two terms (12 years) — **same** |
| Limits election or service? | election ("No person shall be **elected**") | eligibility for election ("shall be **eligible for election**") |
| Partial terms | silent | counted if vacancy served >1 yr (House) / >3 yrs (Senate) |
| Appointed senators | silent — reaches election only | expressly covered ("election **or appointment**") |
| Prospective | yes — "no term to which a person was elected before the ratification… shall be counted" | yes — "No term beginning before the date of the ratification… shall be taken into account" |
| Anti-evasion | yes (our §4) | **absent** |
| Ratification deadline | absent | seven years |

**What their language does that ours does not.** Three things, and two of them are real improvements.

1. **The partial-term rule.** USTL's "more than 1 year" / "more than 3 years" tests are the 22nd Amendment's device, adapted. Our §1 says nothing about partial terms, which leaves open whether a member elected in a special election to a fourteen-month remainder has used a term. This is a genuine gap in our text.
2. **Appointed senators.** USTL reaches "election or appointment"; ours reaches election only. [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) records this as deliberate ("the appointed-senator question (elections, not years) remains by design"), but USTL's drafting shows what the alternative looks like if the founder wants to revisit it.
3. **A seven-year ratification deadline.** Ours has none. Every amendment in the NCC package carries one too. This is a live drafting question for the whole corpus, not just Article 2.

**What ours does that theirs does not.** The anti-evasion clause (§4), which has no counterpart anywhere in the USTL text or in any other org's language reviewed here. Ours also limits *election* rather than *eligibility for election*, tracking the 22nd Amendment's actual wording more closely.

**The trap notes.** USTL's text clears the grandfathering trap (prospective, same as ours) and the partial-term trap (which ours does not address). It does not address the self-interest trap beyond grandfathering, and it has no anti-evasion machinery at all — which our memo identifies as "the single clearest lesson of the international literature." On the dose-response question, USTL's 6-year House cap sits squarely inside the 6–8-year band where, as our memo documents, the worst lobbyist-empowerment findings cluster, and which both California (Prop 28, 2012) and Michigan (Prop 1, 2022) moved *away from* by consolidating to 12 years.

### Our §2 vs. Welch–Manchin and NCC Amendment XXXI

| Element | Ours | Welch–Manchin | NCC XXXI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Term length | 18 years | 18 years | 18 years |
| Staggering | one term commencing each odd-numbered year | July 1 of successive odd-numbered years | "staggered terms of eighteen years, such that every two years there shall be a vacancy" |
| Court size fixed in text | **no** | yes — "composed of nine justices" | yes — "There shall be nine judges" |
| Sitting justices | exempt | exempt (transition §§4–5) | **not exempt** — scheduled removal over 16 years |
| Remainder rule | yes | yes (§6) | yes |
| Non-renewable | implied by single term | express (§8) | not express |
| Post-term service | "as Congress shall by law provide" | silent | judge "may elect to sit on an inferior court during good behavior" |
| Chief Justice | silent | rotates to most senior (§7) | silent |
| Term runs from | fixed calendar date regardless of filling | fixed calendar date | not specified |
| **Confirmation-refusal default** | **knock-out — Court sits as constituted** | **none** | **deemed confirmation after three months** |

Ours is the only one of the three that answers Senate inaction without deeming a nominee confirmed. Welch–Manchin's calendar is the closest technical match to ours and is drafted with more precision on dates (July 1 / June 30 anchors, which ours leaves to "the day appointed for it"); its §8 non-renewability clause and §7 Chief Justice rule both close gaps our text leaves open.

**Two clauses worth considering for borrowing, if the founder wants them:**

- **Welch–Manchin §8** (non-renewability, express): our "a single term of eighteen years" implies it, but does not say a former justice may not be reappointed to a later seat.
- **Welch–Manchin §7** (Chief Justice succession): this is open litigation question 8 in our own memo — "whether a sitting Justice elevated to Chief within a few years of seventy-five begins a new eighteen-year term." Welch–Manchin resolves it by making the Chief Justiceship follow seniority rather than a fresh appointment. Adopting the *idea* would moot our question 8; adopting their *text* would be a larger change to Article II than our article otherwise makes.

**Court size.** Both comparison texts fix the Court at nine; ours does not. Ours instead leaves Congress its ordinary power over the Court's size, subject to §4. This is a deliberate difference and it interacts with our knock-out clause: a fixed nine plus a knock-out default would create a textual tension (the Court "shall be composed of nine" while also sitting "as then constituted" with fewer). Our omission is the coherent choice given our §2, and should be described that way rather than as a gap.

### Our §3 (retirement age) — no comparator exists

No organization in this landscape has drafted a federal judicial retirement age. The Barrett draft, the NCC amendment, Welch–Manchin, H.J.Res. 174, H.R. 1074, and the Brennan and Fix the Court models all omit it. Our §3 is original to us in the current field. The evidence supporting it is state-level and comparative, as the memo already documents — with the New Hampshire correction above.

### Our §4 (anti-evasion) — no comparator exists

Nothing in any org's drafted text does what §4 does. USTL, Welch–Manchin, NCC, Barrett, and H.J.Res. 174 all rely on prospectivity alone. Our §4 is the article's most distinctive clause and the field offers no precedent for it — which is a defensible position given our memo's international evidence on evasion, but it means we cannot point to anyone else's text to defend the drafting.

### If we adopted each org's language 100%

| Adopt | We would gain | We would lose | We would need to add back |
|---|---|---|---|
| **USTL (S.J.Res. 1 / H.J.Res. 12)** | A drafted, sponsored, dual-chamber vehicle with a candidate pledge behind it; a partial-term rule; appointed senators covered; a ratification deadline | Our 12-year House cap, and with it the dose-response rebuttal our memo builds on California and Michigan; the anti-evasion clause; all of §§2–3 | §§2, 3, 4 entirely — USTL's text is §1 only |
| **Welch–Manchin** | Precise calendar anchoring; express non-renewability; a Chief Justice succession rule; endorsements from a retired federal judge and a Republican-administration ethics lawyer | The confirmation-refusal default entirely; the ability to leave court size to Congress | §§1, 3, 4 entirely, plus the knock-out clause |
| **NCC Amendment XXXI** | The only cross-ideological drafted text in the field; a clean staggering formulation; a post-term inferior-court service rule in text | Grandfathering — it removes the sitting bench on a schedule; and we would be adopting deemed confirmation, which our memo rules out | §§1, 3, 4 entirely; a grandfathering clause; a knock-out clause replacing their deemed-consent rule |
| **Fix the Court / Brennan model** | Nothing adoptable — these are statutes and resolutions, not amendment text | The amendment route itself, which is our comparative advantage over both | everything |

## Adversaries

The organized opposition in this space is not against term limits. It is against the Article V convention route, and it comes from both directions at once.

**The [Common Cause coalition](https://www.commoncause.org/resources/coalition-statement-opposing-an-article-v-convention/)** — released April 2017, signer list updated March 2019, over 240 national, state, and local organizations. Its position is categorical: it opposes any Article V convention regardless of subject, on the ground that "there is no language in the U.S. Constitution to limit a convention to one issue," and it urges legislatures both to reject new applications and "to rescind any application for an Article V constitutional convention." National signatories include the **Brennan Center for Justice**, the Campaign Legal Center, CREW, Democracy 21, the League of Women Voters, the NAACP, People For the American Way, the AFL-CIO, SEIU, and the NEA. It quotes Laurence Tribe (a convention would put "the whole Constitution up for grabs"), Chief Justice Warren Burger, and Justice Scalia.

**The [John Birch Society](https://jbs.org/alert/oppose-pennsylvania-article-v-con-con-resolutions/)** opposes the same route from the right, and — importantly for us — opposes the *single-subject term limits* applications specifically, not merely the COS three-subject one. Its Pennsylvania alert names HR 111 (congressional term limits convention), HR 112 (Supreme Court justice term limits convention), and the bipartisan HR 21 as targets alongside the COS-worded resolutions, and urges rescission of past applications "regardless of the desired amendment(s)." It quotes Scalia's 2015 "this is not a good century to write a constitution" and Goldwater's 1979 warning.

**What this means for us.** There is no organized opposition to term limits as a policy that we found; the polling explains why. The opposition is procedural, it is bipartisan, and it is aimed at the route USTL has spent a decade building. Since our own route is a congressionally proposed amendment rather than a convention, this opposition does not reach us directly — but it reaches any org we stand next to. The substantive counterarguments to congressional term limits remain the political-science findings our memo already engages (PPIC, Sarbaugh-Thompson), which are academic literature rather than organized advocacy.

## Partnership and conflict

**The conflict map is unusually clean.** The congressional-term-limits camp and the SCOTUS-term-limits camp are not merely separate; on the Article V question they are opposed, with the Brennan Center advocating our §2 while signing a statement urging states to rescind the applications USTL is collecting for our §1. Any coalition that included both would have to route around that. Our bundling of the two halves in a single article — which our memo justifies on cross-partisan grounds ("each wing gets its priority") — has no organizational precedent, and the reason appears to be that the two constituencies do not talk to each other.

**On U.S. Term Limits specifically.** They are the right partner on §1's *existence* and the wrong partner on §1's *number*. The pledge's "and no longer limit" language means a USTL endorsement of our text is not available while we hold at six House terms, and pursuing one would mean abandoning the 12-year figure that the UMD deliberative survey produced and that California and Michigan converged on from experience. The honest position is the one our memo already stakes out: our version is the answer to the credible criticisms of theirs. That is a distinctive, defensible posture — and it is also a public disagreement with the largest organization in the field, which should be stated plainly rather than elided. Their research, their state-count reporting, and their legal history (they are the *Thornton* plaintiff) remain citable throughout.

**On Fix the Court and the Brennan Center.** Cite, do not join. Both are high-quality evidentiary sources for §2 and both have chosen the statutory route, which is the route our memo identifies as our comparative disadvantage to argue against: any statute would be reviewed by the very justices it limits. Their partisan sponsor lists (all-Democratic on both H.R. 1074 and S. 3096) would code us if we joined their coalition. Fix the Court's endorsed bill contains a clause we reject.

**On the National Constitution Center.** This is the one genuinely promising relationship in the landscape, and it is not an advocacy relationship. The NCC is a nonpartisan constitutional institution whose drafting project produced cross-ideological agreement on 18-year Supreme Court terms among scholars from the Manhattan Institute, the Goldwater Institute, Princeton, Stanford, Columbia, Georgetown, and NYU. We disagree with their confirmation default and their transition, but the existence of their text is the strongest available evidence that our §2's core is not a partisan proposal. **Recommended: cite Amendment XXXI prominently as cross-ideological precedent for the 18-year term, and name our two departures from it (grandfathering, and knock-out rather than deemed confirmation) as deliberate.** The NCC scholars are also the most plausible pool of expert reviewers in the field.

**On Convention of States.** No association, in either direction.

## Corrections and open items this pass surfaced for the parent memo

1. **The New Hampshire 2024 result** (65.60% yes, defeated by a two-thirds rule) is a sixth certified judicial-retirement-age ballot result and it does not run the same direction as the five listed in [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md). The "five certified state ballot results… one direction" formulation needs revising before any public use.
2. **The USTL state count is contested.** USTL says fourteen single-subject states as of August 2026; Wikipedia's enumeration lists thirteen while its prose says "about 14"; press coverage of Kansas in 2026 called it both the thirteenth and the sixteenth. Any published number should be attributed to USTL and dated.
3. **Two independent, ideologically opposite drafting efforts chose deemed confirmation** (NCC Amendment XXXI §1; H.R. 1074's new 28 U.S.C. § 9). Our memo's instruction not to draft that variant stands, but the page should not imply the knock-out clause is the field's consensus answer. It is a minority answer.
4. **Our §1 has no partial-term rule.** USTL's text and the 22nd Amendment both have one. This is a real gap, independent of the 6-vs-3 numbers question.
5. **No amendment text in the corpus carries a ratification deadline.** Every comparator reviewed here does (USTL: seven years; NCC: seven years). Corpus-wide drafting question.
