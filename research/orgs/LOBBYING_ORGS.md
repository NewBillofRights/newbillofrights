# Lobbying — Organizational Landscape and Language Comparison

*Org-landscape pass, 2026-08-19. Covers Article 5 (Lobbying) of [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md); companion to [LOBBYING_RESEARCH.md](../LOBBYING_RESEARCH.md).*

## Founder direction (2026-08-19, recorded verbatim)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

## The headline finding: there is no language to adopt

**No organization has drafted constitutional amendment text on lobbying or the revolving door.** This pass checked the five seed organizations, the Article V groups, the campaign-finance amendment organizations, and the adversary side. Every organization working this issue pursues one of three routes — model *statutes* for cities and states, federal *bills*, or *litigation and disclosure complaints*. Not one has published constitutional language.

This is the structural difference between Article 5 and the articles where the founder direction has real purchase. On campaign finance, American Promise has a drafted amendment and a state-resolution count; on term limits, U.S. Term Limits has drafted amendment text and an Article V application campaign. On lobbying there is no equivalent. The nearest thing to a language benchmark is RepresentUs's **American Anti-Corruption Act (AACA)** — explicitly self-described as "an aspirational document" setting a standard for ordinary law, drafted by a former FEC chairman "with a sharp eye toward avoiding provisions that could be overturned by the courts" ([RepresentUs](https://represent.us/anticorruption-act/)). It is drafted to survive the First Amendment, not to change it. That is the opposite of what Article 5 exists to do.

The memo's own [Prior Amendment Proposals](../LOBBYING_RESEARCH.md) section already recorded this as "essentially open ground at the federal constitutional level." This pass confirms it against the organizations themselves, and adds the reason it matters: **the field's most sophisticated drafters have chosen the statutory route knowing it is vulnerable** — and the vulnerability keeps materializing. That is the argument for Article 5, and the orgs' own defeats are the evidence for it.

## RepresentUs — the language benchmark

**Identity.** Founded 2012, headquartered in Florence, Massachusetts. Operates a c4 (Represent Us, EIN 26-2369596) paired with a c3 education fund (Represent Us Education Fund, EIN 26-3088283), a standard advocacy structure. FY2023: c4 revenue $2,199,180 against $497,021 expenses with $5,777,071 in end-of-year assets; c3 revenue $5,496,155 against $7,753,629 expenses with $6,627,073 in assets. The c3 spent above revenue in FY2023 and FY2022 revenue was substantially higher ($9,911,725), indicating a contraction.

**The American Anti-Corruption Act.** Crafted in 2011 by **Trevor Potter**, former Federal Election Commission chairman and general counsel to John McCain's presidential campaigns, in consultation with reform leaders and constitutional attorneys across the spectrum; unveiled 2012 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Anti-Corruption_Act)). The Potter provenance is the AACA's principal cross-partisan credential and is genuine — a Republican election lawyer is the author of the document the reform left organizes around.

**Provision 3 — the revolving door clause, verbatim** ([AACA full provisions list, 12.9.2019](https://represent.us/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/AACA-Revised-Full-Provisions-List-%E2%80%93-12_9_19.pdf)):

> **PROVISION 3: CLOSE THE REVOLVING DOOR**
>
> ● Extend the existing revolving door limitations applicable to Members of Congress and congressional staff to 5 years for former Members, and 5 years for former congressional staffers who are either (1) paid at a rate of 75% or more of a Member's salary, or (2) whose duties are not primarily secretarial in nature.
>
> ● Prohibit Members of Congress and senior congressional staff from negotiating or having any arrangement concerning prospective private employment, including engaging in any preliminary or exploratory discussions regarding such employment, while a Member of Congress or senior congressional staff.

**Provision 13 — the shadow-lobbying fix, verbatim** (same source; this is the provision that matters most for our Section 1):

> ● Ensure that paid lobbyists are identified. Current law turns on two requirements to trigger lobbyist status: (1) two lobbying contacts, and (2) 20% of time for client spent engaging in lobbying activities. Instead, set the threshold as (1) two lobbying contacts or providing strategic advice to lobbying efforts or directing or supervising the provision of strategic advice to lobbying efforts, and (2) 12 hours or more spent engaging in lobbying activities.

**On foreign lobbying: the AACA has no provision at all.** The full provisions list runs to 14 provisions in four parts, and foreign governments, foreign political parties, FARA, and foreign agents appear nowhere in it. This was verified against the complete document text, not a summary. Our Section 2 — the lifetime foreign-agent bar, and the plank the memo identifies as the one where Republicans lead (71% favor, 70.5R / 73.7D) — **has no counterpart in the field's flagship model law.**

**Route and track record.** Explicitly bottom-up statutory. The strategy page frames the theory of change as accumulation toward a federal statute: "Every state we win gets us one crucial step closer to passing the American Anti-Corruption Act in the federal government" ([RepresentUs strategy](https://represent.us/the-strategy-to-end-corruption-0/)). No Article V application, no constitutional amendment campaign.

The [wins page](https://represent.us/our-wins/) lists 174 entries across 34 states (summed from the page's own per-state index), but the great majority are **non-binding municipal and county resolutions** endorsing the AACA rather than enacted law — the page's own entries describe them that way (e.g. Raritan Borough, NJ, "Non-binding City Resolution"). The binding wins that bear on lobbying:

- **Tallahassee, FL (Nov. 4, 2014)** — first city Anti-Corruption Act, passed by a 2-to-1 margin; contribution limits, an independent ethics board, a conflict-of-interest code.
- **Seattle (2015)** — three-year post-departure ban on elected officials and staff lobbying the city.
- **South Dakota Initiated Measure 22 (Nov. 8, 2016)** — the only statewide AACA. Passed **51.63% (180,634) to 48.37% (169,199)**; included a **two-year** revolving-door ban on state officials and high-level employees plus lobbyist gift limits. **Preliminarily enjoined December 8, 2016** (Judge Mark Barnett), then **repealed outright by the legislature via House Bill 1069, signed February 2, 2017** ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/South_Dakota_Revision_of_State_Campaign_Finance_and_Lobbying_Laws,_Initiated_Measure_22_(2016))). The 2018 follow-up, Amendment W, failed.

**Measure 22 is the single most useful precedent in this memo, and it cuts our way.** A voter-approved state anti-corruption act with a revolving-door ban was enjoined within a month and erased by the legislature within three. Alongside *Miller v. Ziegler* erasing Clean Missouri's two-year ban and *Garcia v. Stillman* reaching Florida's in-office restriction, it completes the pattern the memo already identified: **statutory and even state-constitutional lobbying reform is repeatedly won at the ballot and then removed by a court or a legislature.** Article 5 exists precisely because that keeps happening. Note also the honest read on the margin — 51.63% is a narrow win, not a mandate, and materially weaker than Florida Amendment 12's ~79%.

**Partisan coding.** Genuinely cross-partisan by our standard, with caveats. The Potter authorship, the Tallahassee coalition (which included the Chair of the Florida Tea Party Network and the former President of the Florida League of Women Voters), and the c3/c4 structure all support it. But the AACA bundles ranked choice voting, open primaries, independent redistricting, and public campaign financing (Provisions 6–9) with the ethics provisions — a package that reads as left-coded to many conservatives regardless of its authorship, and one that carries far more contested content than Article 5's subject matter. Association with the *package* would code us; citation of Provision 3 would not.

**Current status.** A caution on freshness: the flagship provisions document is dated **December 9, 2019**, and **anticorruptionact.org — the domain printed in that document's own footer — did not respond to repeated connection attempts on 2026-08-19** (connection timeout, not a 403 or a redirect). RepresentUs itself remains active, with its 2026 work described around a Congressional Courage Campaign, but the AACA appears to be a maintained legacy platform rather than the org's live program. Treat the AACA as a documentary benchmark, not as an active drafting partner.

## Public Citizen — the org that argues *against* our Section 1 length

**Identity.** Founded 1971 by Ralph Nader; the oldest and one of the largest organizations in this set. c4 (Public Citizen Inc, EIN 23-7104508, FY2023 revenue $5,728,329) plus c3 (Public Citizen Foundation, EIN 52-1263996, FY2023 revenue $13,389,271, assets $33,737,010).

**Position — and the conflict.** Public Citizen's revolving-door recommendations ([Slowing the Federal Revolving Door](https://www.citizen.org/article/slowing-the-federal-revolving-door/)) are the most detailed in the field, and they are **more cautious than our draft in one direction and sharper in another**:

- Cooling-off: "Extending all cooling-off periods to a minimum of two years – at least a full congressional cycle – and preferably even longer," with a six-year Florida-style ban named as "a more realistic approach." **Two years minimum, six years as the ambitious ask — our five years sits inside their range.**
- On a lifetime ban, Public Citizen says it is **"politically impractical and constitutionally suspect."** They are describing a lifetime ban on *domestic* lobbying, which our Article 5 also declines to adopt — but the phrase is quotable against a lifetime bar of any kind, including our Section 2's foreign-agent bar, and an opponent will use it that way.
- The sharpest recommendation, and the one that most directly validates our Section 1: banning compensation for **"lobbying activity," such as of conducting research, preparation, planning and supervision of a lobbying campaign, as well as banning "lobbying contacts" during the cooling-off period.** This is the same target as our "advocating before, *or advising others in advocating before*" — reached by different words.
- Coverage: "Applying the ban on lobbying by former elected officials and very senior staff across the board to all agencies and both the legislative and executive branches" — matching our Section 1's reach to "the Congress or the executive agencies."

**No model text and no constitutional proposal.** Recommendations reference the For the People Act (H.R. 1) rather than supplying drafted language.

**Track record.** Public Citizen's 2019 *Revolving Congress* study (already cited in the memo) is the empirical backbone of the whole reform push, and it precipitated the AOC–Cruz exchange that produced the bipartisan lifetime-ban bills. Their research is the field's evidence base even where their policy recommendation is more modest than the bills it inspired.

**Partisan coding.** Left-coded. Nader provenance, consistent alignment with progressive coalitions. Their *research* is citable and widely credited; their *brand* would code us if we joined a coalition under it.

## POGO — foreign-influence expertise, statutory remedies only

**Identity.** Project On Government Oversight, founded 1981, Washington DC, c3 (EIN 52-1739443), FY2023 revenue $7,933,632, assets $11,690,657. Nonpartisan in self-presentation and in reputation, with a genuine history of Republican-side credibility on defense oversight — the most useful partisan profile in this set.

**Position.** POGO is the deepest institutional expert on the **FARA** side, which is our Section 2's territory. Their [foreign lobbying fact sheet](https://www.pogo.org/analyses/fact-sheet-foreign-lobbying-reform) documents the enforcement collapse that our Section 4 responds to, with figures worth carrying:

> The Department has only pursued criminal charges for FARA violations *seven times in the last 50 years*.
>
> The Department has not pursued a civil injunction for FARA violations *since 1991*.

Plus a 2016 DOJ Inspector General finding that compliance was "unacceptable": 62% of new registrants 2013–2015 filed late, over 50% failed to file disclosure information timely, 47% failed to properly disclose they were working for a foreign client.

**Their three recommendations are all statutory FARA amendments**, verbatim in substance: give DOJ authority to levy **civil fines** scaled to severity and number of infractions; require **more detailed disclosure** of political activities including meeting logs (who, when, what issues); and monitor DOJ's review of **FARA exemptions**. POGO proposes *disclosure and enforcement*, not prohibition. **No ban, no drafted text, no constitutional proposal.**

**Bearing on our draft.** POGO's evidence is the strongest available support for our Section 4's independent-enforcer requirement — it is the documented case that an unenforced foreign-influence regime is decorative. But note the gap in the other direction: **POGO does not ask for what our Section 2 does.** The field's leading foreign-influence organization asks for fines and forms where we impose a lifetime bar. That is a real distance, and if we cite POGO's evidence we should not imply POGO's endorsement of the remedy.

## Campaign Legal Center — litigation, and the *reverse* revolving door

**Identity.** Founded 2002 by Trevor Potter — the same Republican former FEC chairman who drafted the AACA. c3 (EIN 04-3608387, FY2023 revenue $21,481,014, assets $45,817,252) plus a small c4, CLC Action (EIN 83-3411170). The best-resourced organization in this set by a wide margin.

**Position.** CLC's [ethics program](https://campaignlegal.org/issues/ethics) works by litigation, formal complaints to inspectors general and ethics offices, and guidance to state and local ethics commissions. Its revolving-door work runs mostly in the **opposite direction from ours** — lobbyists entering government, not officials leaving it. As of April 2, 2026 CLC identified at least 47 former lobbyists nominated for or serving as senior political appointees, more than 15 of whom would have been barred or limited under the ethics pledges of the prior three administrations, and calls for legislation barring former lobbyists from such roles and from participating in matters they lobbied on within two years before appointment.

That direction is explicitly **outside** our Article 5 — the memo assigns "golden-parachute and reverse-revolving-door rules for people *entering* government" to the inherently-statutory list. CLC's flagship ethics work is therefore adjacent to our article rather than overlapping it.

**No amendment text, no model bill.** CLC also intervened in the South Dakota Measure 22 repeal fight on the pro-reform side.

**Partisan coding.** The strongest cross-partisan credential in the set on paper — Potter founder, Republican and Democratic board history — though its litigation docket has drawn right-side criticism as directionally partisan. Best-in-class as a technical resource; a plausible reviewer of drafted text rather than a source of it.

## Issue One — convening power, not drafting

**Identity.** Founded 2014, Washington DC. c3 (EIN 32-0384285, FY2023 revenue $7,870,934) plus c4 Issue One Action (EIN 46-0792299; no Form 990 financial data returned by the ProPublica API). Runs the **ReFormers Caucus** — the largest bipartisan assembly of former members of Congress, governors, and cabinet officials working on democracy reform.

**Position.** Issue One's published issue areas as of 2026-08-19 are Constitutional Defense, Free & Fair Elections, Technology Reform, Money in Politics, and Democracy Community ([issues page](https://issueone.org/issues/)). **Lobbying and ethics are not a standalone program.** Its lobbying-adjacent output is investigative journalism — e.g. mapping revolving-door lobbyists employed by social media platforms — and its Money in Politics program (directed by Michael Beckel) comments on foreign-lobbying developments. No policy platform on the revolving door, no model text, no amendment.

**Value to us.** Issue One is the wrong organization to seek *language* from and the right one to understand for *coalition* purposes. Its ReFormers Caucus is composed of exactly the population Article 5 Sections 1 and 2 would restrict — former members of Congress. Their reaction is a live test of the "chilling effect on talented people serving" objection the memo already tracks (found convincing by 44.0%, 45.3R / 41.4D). A ReFormers Caucus endorsement would be disproportionately valuable precisely because the endorsers bear the cost; their objection would be a serious early warning.

## Adversaries

**Institute for Free Speech** (founded 2005 as the Center for Competitive Politics) is the organized opposition and the most consequential actor for Article 5's drafting. It litigates against lobbying restrictions on First Amendment grounds and is working to narrow the doctrine our reform depends on. Its amicus brief in *Sullivan v. Texas Ethics Commission* argues that *United States v. Harriss* (1954) "no longer reflects modern First Amendment jurisprudence," and that "the government cannot burden the First Amendment right to speak anonymously because disclosure is convenient for elected officials" ([IFS](https://www.ifs.org/news/institute-for-free-speech-urges-supreme-court-to-revisit-outdated-precedent-on-lobbying-disclosure/)). If IFS succeeds in narrowing *Harriss*, even the disclosure floor that everyone in the reform field currently treats as safe becomes contestable — which strengthens rather than weakens the case for constitutionalizing.

**The plaintiffs' bar in practice.** The decisive opposition to date has not been an organization but individual former officials suing: Rocky Miller and John LaVanchy in *Miller v. Ziegler* (Missouri's two-year ban, struck down July 2024), and the five Florida local officials in *Garcia v. Stillman*. Article 5 should expect to be attacked by sympathetic individual plaintiffs, not by a corporate villain — which is a framing problem as much as a legal one.

**No organized adversary on the foreign-agent plank.** This pass found no organization arguing publicly that former officials should be free to represent foreign governments. That asymmetry matches the memo's polling (the counterargument against a foreign-government ban convinced only 29.0%, 30.9R / 26.0D) and supports the memo's existing recommendation to lead the public case with Section 2.

## Language comparison against Article 5

The AACA is the only drafted text in the field, so it is the benchmark — with the standing caveat that it is a model *statute* aimed at surviving the First Amendment, while Article 5 is constitutional text aimed at settling it. Comparing them tests coverage, not drafting register.

| Article 5 provision | AACA equivalent | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **§1** — 5-year ban, Members + senior officers, Congress *and* executive agencies | Provision 3 — 5 years, Members + senior congressional staff; **congressional only** | **Same number, narrower scope.** The five-year figure matches exactly. But the AACA does not reach the executive branch, and it reaches staff where we reach "officer of the United States in a senior position defined by law." |
| **§1** — "advising others in advocating before" (shadow-lobbying reach) | Provision 13 — trigger includes "providing strategic advice to lobbying efforts or directing or supervising the provision of strategic advice" | **Convergent, and their phrasing is a gift.** Same target, and their formula names the supervisory tier explicitly. |
| **§1 savings clause** — own-behalf and uncompensated petitioning preserved | None | **Ours only.** A statute does not need it; constitutional text does. |
| **§2** — lifetime foreign-agent bar | **Nothing** | **Ours only, entirely.** The AACA has no foreign-lobbying provision of any kind. |
| **§3** — citizen-petitioner firewall (associations, religious/labor/charitable/membership) | None | **Ours only.** No model text in the field contains an equivalent firewall. |
| **§4** — enforcement, independent officer, non-waiver | Provision 13 (2-year disqualification for non-compliance); Provision 14 (replace FEC with an empowered Federal Election Administration using administrative law judges) | **Partial, different mechanism.** The AACA's instinct matches ours — an enforcer with real power — but it names an agency, exactly the over-specification our memo's counter-trap warns against in constitutional text. Its **disqualification remedy** (barred from lobbying for 2 years after non-compliance) is a penalty design we had not considered. No non-waiver clause. |
| **§5** — prospective application only | Provision 3 is silent; S. 4524 §2(d) uses the same prospective limit | **Ours is explicit.** Convergent with the bills. |
| — | Provisions 1, 2, 4 (lobbyist contribution ban, bundling ban, fundraising during working hours) | **Theirs only** — campaign-finance territory, our Article 1's subject, not Article 5's. |
| — | Provisions 6–11 (redistricting, public financing, open primaries, RCV, term limits, AVR) | **Theirs only** — spans four of our other articles; irrelevant to Article 5 and a coalition liability if adopted as a package. |

### If we adopted the AACA's language 100%

| | |
|---|---|
| **Gain** | A named drafter with cross-partisan standing (Trevor Potter, former FEC chair). A sharper shadow-lobbying formula — "strategic advice … or directing or supervising the provision of strategic advice" names the supervisory tier our text reaches only by implication. An in-office job-negotiation ban we do not currently have. A disqualification-style penalty concept for Section 4's implementing legislation. |
| **Lose** | Section 2 entirely — the lifetime foreign-agent bar, the plank where Republicans lead at 70.5%, has no AACA counterpart. Executive-branch coverage (AACA Provision 3 is congressional only). The Section 3 citizen-petitioner firewall — the campaign-killer trap the memo flags — which no model text in the field contains. The Section 1 savings clause. The non-waiver rule that answers the EO 13770 midnight-revocation problem. |
| **Need to add back** | Everything that makes it constitutional text rather than a bill: the foreign-agent bar, the firewall, the savings clause, the non-waiver rule, executive-branch scope, and prospective application. Also everything that keeps it a *lobbying* article: the AACA's other thirteen provisions span campaign finance, redistricting, term limits, and election administration. |
| **Net** | **Adoption is not available.** There is no constitutional text to adopt. The AACA is worth mining for two phrases and one penalty concept, and worth citing for the Potter provenance. It cannot serve as a base draft, because a model statute engineered to avoid First Amendment problems cannot do the one job Article 5 exists to do. |

### Trap-note check

Against the memo's own trap notes, the field's texts perform as follows:

- **Petition Clause collision** — no org's text addresses it; all are drafted to *avoid* the collision rather than resolve it. Our Section 1 savings clause has no counterpart. **Ours handles it; theirs sidestep it.**
- **Shadow-lobbying definition trap** — the one trap the field handles well. AACA Provision 13 and Public Citizen's "lobbying activity" recommendation both reach past registered contacts, converging with our "advising others in advocating before." **Convergent; theirs is usefully more explicit.**
- **Definition trap, foreign edition** (cutouts, SOEs, sovereign funds) — **no org addresses it at all**, because no org drafts a foreign-agent prohibition. POGO's exemption-review recommendation is adjacent but is about disclosure. Our Section 2 plus the memo's "principal beneficiary" note for implementing legislation remain unmatched in the field.
- **Citizen-petitioner overbreadth trap** — **no org's text contains a firewall.** Model statutes do not need one; constitutional text does. Unmatched.
- **Enforcement trap** — AACA Provision 14 shares the instinct but names an agency (over-specification) and has no non-waiver rule. Public Citizen and POGO both document the enforcement failure without proposing a structural fix. **Ours is stronger; theirs supply the evidence.**
- **Over-specification counter-trap** — the AACA fails this decisively, as a statute properly should: 20%-versus-12-hours thresholds, 75%-of-salary staff tiers, named agencies. **This is the clearest demonstration of why the field's text cannot be lifted into the Constitution.** Their precision is a virtue in a bill and a fossil in an amendment.

## Partnership and conflict

**Can we endorse anyone's text?** No — there is none to endorse. This is the finding that distinguishes Article 5 from the articles where the founder direction applies straightforwardly.

**Where citation is safe and valuable:**

- **POGO** is the best-positioned partner: nonpartisan reputation with real Republican-side credibility, the deepest FARA expertise, and evidence (7 prosecutions in 50 years; no civil injunction since 1991) that directly supports our Section 4. Cite the evidence; do not imply they endorse a prohibition, because they do not propose one.
- **Public Citizen's** *Revolving Congress* research is the field's evidence base and already load-bearing in our memo. Their brand is left-coded; the research is citable, the coalition is not, and their "constitutionally suspect" line on lifetime bans should be anticipated as an opposition quote.
- **RepresentUs** supplies the Potter provenance and two useful phrases. The AACA's bundling of RCV, open primaries, and public financing makes package-level association a coalition liability for us. Cite Provision 3; do not adopt the platform.
- **CLC** is the strongest technical reviewer in the field and the best-resourced. Its own agenda is largely the reverse revolving door, which our memo puts outside Article 5.
- **Issue One** matters for endorsement rather than language. Its ReFormers Caucus is drawn from the population Article 5 would restrict, which makes its reaction an early and honest test of the recruitment objection.

**Conflict to expect.** The reform field's own settled position is that a lifetime ban is "constitutionally suspect" (Public Citizen) and that model law should be drafted to avoid judicial review (RepresentUs). Article 5 rejects both premises — it constitutionalizes a lifetime bar for foreign agents and is designed to overrule the doctrine the field drafts around. **The friction with these organizations will be over ambition, not direction.** Our answer is the record their own defeats compiled: South Dakota Measure 22 enjoined in a month and repealed in three, Clean Missouri's ban struck down in *Miller v. Ziegler*, Florida's in-office restriction reached in *Garcia v. Stillman*. The field has run the statutory experiment for a decade. Article 5 is the conclusion drawn from its results.

**One structural note on Article V groups.** Convention of States Action's application is limited to fiscal restraints, federal power and jurisdiction, and term limits on officials and members of Congress; U.S. Term Limits' applications are single-subject term limits. Neither covers lobbying. There is no Article V vehicle for this article, and no convention route to inherit.
