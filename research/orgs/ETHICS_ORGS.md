## The headline finding: there is no language to adopt

**For Article 3, the founder's adopt-don't-draft preference has nothing to adopt.** This is the sharpest result of the pass, and it is a negative one.

Campaign finance has American Promise with drafted amendment text. Term limits has U.S. Term Limits with drafted amendment text. **Ethics has no counterpart.** Across every organization advocating on official self-dealing, judicial ethics, and executive conflicts of interest, we found **zero drafted constitutional amendment text** — not a model amendment, not a proposed section, not a one-sentence formulation.

Three independent tests, each run against a primary source this session:

1. **Congress.gov, 119th Congress, joint resolutions, keyword "constitutional amendment stock trading OR conflict of interest OR ethics"** — "No results found."
2. **Congress.gov, 119th Congress, joint resolutions, policy area "Congress," keyword "stock"** — "No results found."
3. **Article V convention groups.** The Convention of States application is limited to amendments that "impose fiscal restraints on the federal government, limit the power and jurisdiction of the federal government, and limit the terms of office for its officials and for members of Congress." Ethics and self-dealing are outside its scope; it has no ethics amendment.

Every organization in this space works the statutory route, and says so plainly. This is not an oversight on their part — it is a considered strategic judgment, and the memo's own reasoning is why it deserves respect rather than dismissal.

**What this means for Article 3.** There is no established-org text to adopt, adapt, or endorse. Our draft is not competing with a better-developed alternative; it is the only constitutional formulation of these principles we found. The adoption question for this article is therefore not "whose text do we take?" but "do we borrow *definitional* work from statutory drafts that ours delegates to Congress anyway?" That is a narrower and much lower-stakes question, addressed in the comparison section.

## Per-organization detail

### Campaign Legal Center (CLC)

The most substantive drafting analyst in the space, and the closest thing to a design authority on the trading ban — but entirely at the statutory level.

CLC's [Pros and Cons of STOCK Act Reform Proposals](https://campaignlegal.org/sites/default/files/2022-03/Pros%20and%20Cons%20of%20STOCK%20Act%20Reform%20Proposals.pdf) rates five reform architectures. Two findings bear directly on our Section 1.

On the absolute-ban option, its only stated drawback is political: "This approach has very little downside from an effectiveness standpoint, however there may be challenges building consensus without first trying other options that face less opposition from lawmakers."

On the qualified-blind-trust option — **the safe harbor our Section 1 writes into constitutional text** — CLC's stated con is: "A poorly implemented qualified blind trust requirement could result in a worst-case scenario where the member in fact continues to control and direct holdings while the public does not know any of the investments."

That is a direct challenge to a design choice our memo made deliberately, and it is addressed in the comparison table below.

CLC also names five loopholes any reform must close, of which two are live for us: covered individuals ("members, senior staff, and their spouses and dependent children should be subject to the legislation") and excepted investments ("Any investment that a covered individual is prohibited from trading directly should also apply to investments held indirectly in an investment fund, trust, or similar entity, if the covered individual controls and directs those financial vehicles").

On enforcement, CLC's [STOCK Act analysis](https://campaignlegal.org/update/congressional-stock-trading-and-stock-act) reports the penalty is $200, that no member has ever been prosecuted for insider trading under the Act, and that 95% of Congress still owns stock. Its recommendation is legislative: pass the Restore Trust in Congress Act. No constitutional amendment appears anywhere in CLC's material we reviewed.

### Project On Government Oversight (POGO)

POGO holds the **broadest** substantive position of any org here — and it is the one that collides with our memo's breadth trap.

In [testimony to the House Administration Committee](https://www.pogo.org/testimonies/congress-can-earn-back-public-trust-by-banning-stock-trading) (Liz Hempowicz, hearing "Examining Stock Trading Reforms for Congress," March 16, 2022), POGO stated: "POGO would like to see a much more restrictive ban applied to elected officials and top employees of all three branches of government."

Two features of that position matter to us. First, the three-branch scope matches our Article 3 ambition more closely than any other org's position. Second, "top employees" crosses the line our memo draws: extending coverage beyond elected and appointed constitutional officers to civil servants is the change that collapses support from 86% to 39.9% (41.6% R / 37.1% D / 42.2% I). POGO's instinct is our documented failure mode.

POGO also rejects the grandfathering our draft does not contain: "we recommend that you require Members of Congress and their spouses to divest a broad array of investments with no exception for those held before taking office." Note the tension with the memo's wealth-penalty trap — forced sale of existing holdings polled as going too far for 69% in 2016.

POGO's sequencing is explicitly incrementalist: pass the pending congressional bills first, "then turn to a set of stricter prohibitions applicable to all three branches."

### Fix the Court

The specialist on the judicial half of our article, and the org whose diagnosis maps most cleanly onto our Sections 3 and 4 — though again, as statute and internal-process reform, never as amendment text.

Fix the Court is "a nonpartisan, 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization (EIN: 86-1840317)," founded in 2014 by Gabe Roth, who still directs it; it received c3 designation in 2021.

Its [Code of Ethics](https://fixthecourt.com/fix/code-of-ethics/) page identifies exactly the gap our Section 3's no-self-judging clause targets. On the Court's 2023 Code: "Though this a positive first step, the text leaves much to be desired." The enumerated failures: "there remains today no way for a lawmaker or member of the public to file a complaint against a justice for misconduct. There's no way for an outside source to advocate for recusal when a justice participates in a case despite an obvious conflict. There's no internal Supreme Court Ethics Office to offer consistent advice when ethics issues arise. And there's no investigatory body, whether internal or external (like an inspector general) that can look into allegations of misconduct and help the justices fix their errors and omissions."

Its finding that "while none of the justices has committed a removal offense, all nine are culpable of various ethical oversights" is useful to us for a specific reason: it is a symmetric, all-nine framing, which is the posture our memo's politicization trap calls for.

On partisan coding, Fix the Court has a genuine cross-spectrum operating record: "We've co-hosted events with the conservative Federalist Society and the liberal American Constitution Society at countless law schools, and we've hired advisers with varied backgrounds, including a former chief of staff to the House Republican Whip and a former national labor organizer." Its funder list, however, reads left-of-center to a hostile reader (Hewlett, Park, Proteus, Rockefeller Brothers, New Venture Fund).

Scale is the caveat: total revenue of $201,039 against expenses of $263,319 for the fiscal year ending February 2026. This is a two-person-scale organization with outsized research output, not a campaign vehicle.

### CREW

CREW convenes the coalition and litigates. Its Supreme Court ethics [report](https://www.citizensforethics.org/reports-investigations/crew-reports/building-public-confidence-how-the-supreme-court-can-demonstrate-its-commitment-to-the-highest-ethical-standards/) (August 9, 2023) recommends the Court "adopt both a formal code of ethics and an independent oversight mechanism that will enable justices to obtain impartial advice about ethical questions," built around a panel of three retired federal judges appointed by the Chief Justice to review disqualification motions and issue advisory opinions.

That architecture — review inside the judiciary, by judges, with no political appointing authority — is substantially the answer our memo's judicial who-enforces trap arrives at. CREW proposes it as voluntary adoption and statute; we propose the principle as constitutional text. **CREW's panel design is the best available model for the implementing legislation our Section 3 would authorize.**

CREW is also the institutional memory on why constitutional ethics text without a remedy fails: it litigated *CREW v. Trump*, dismissed as moot with lower-court rulings vacated. That history is the direct evidentiary basis for our Section 4's enforcement principle.

### Issue One

Issue One's distinctive asset is the **ReFormers Caucus** — former members of Congress from both parties. On the Restore Trust in Congress Act it published paired statements from Ambassador Tim Roemer (D-IN) and Congressman Charles Boustany (R-LA), and it notes that "more than 40 members of the bipartisan ReFormers Caucus called on Senate leadership to pass similar legislation."

Issue One cites the same 86% figure our polling memo uses, sourced to the University of Maryland. Its framing — "Members of Congress have a responsibility to hold themselves to the highest ethical standards" — is valence-neutral and compatible with our voice.

### National Taxpayers Union

The right-of-center validator, and the most important org in this memo for our full-spectrum bar.

NTU has endorsed congressional trading restrictions repeatedly, including H.R. 7008, where it urged a "YES" vote and called the measure "This ethics reform is a much-needed step toward restoring public trust in government" (Alexander Ciccone, July 20, 2026). Critically for our Section 1, **NTU's support is conditioned on precisely the safe harbor our draft contains** — it backs continued investment through diversified vehicles such as mutual funds and ETFs on the ground that pooled investments do not create the same conflicts, and that this keeps Congress open to entrepreneurs and ordinary Americans rather than deterring non-wealthy candidates.

That is our wealth-penalty trap, articulated by a conservative taxpayer group in its own words. It is strong evidence that the diversified-fund/blind-trust safe harbor is what makes this reform cross-partisan — and correspondingly strong evidence against CLC's and POGO's preference for an absolute ban.

NTU proposes no constitutional amendment.

### The coalition

The November 19, 2025 letter to the House Administration Committee, signed by ten organizations — 20/20 Vision, Campaign Legal Center, CREW, Common Cause, Issue One, Progressive Change Institute, Project on Government Oversight, Public Citizen, RepresentUs, Stand Up America — sets out the shared platform. It asks for a ban on "both trading and owning individual stocks, bonds, digital assets or other securities, including coverage of private equity, venture capital and hedge funds, with very limited exceptions for diversified mutual funds, ETFs and index funds"; coverage of "members of Congress, their spouses and their dependent children"; "a clear and workable enforcement mechanism that can serve as a functional deterrent and does not require too high a bar for intent (such as the knowingly standard, which would make enforcement difficult)"; and exclusion of "overly broad loopholes."

Two observations. The asset list — "digital assets," private equity, hedge funds — is broader and more concrete than our Section 1's "individual security, digital asset, or contract of speculation," and is a candidate source for definitional borrowing. And the intent-standard point (avoid "knowingly") is a drafting warning our text does not currently address, because our text delegates penalties to Congress entirely.

The coalition's scope is **Congress only**. No coalition letter we reviewed asks for coverage of the President, Vice President, or judiciary in the same instrument.

## Route and track record

The statutory route has been tried continuously for fourteen years and has produced, as of this writing, one chamber's passage of a partial bill.

- **ETHICS Act (S.1171, 118th Congress).** Sen. Merkley, introduced April 17, 2023. Reported by Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs with an amendment in the nature of a substitute, S. Rept. 118-309; placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar, Calendar No. 729, December 19, 2024. **Never received a floor vote; died with the Congress.**
- **Restore Trust in Congress Act (H.R. 5106, 119th Congress).** Rep. Chip Roy, introduced September 3, 2025, with 16 original cosponsors alternating by party. As of this session: **142 cosponsors — 106 Democratic, 36 Republican** — and still "Referred to the House Committee on House Administration," its only action, with no markup nearly a year later. This is the coalition's preferred vehicle.
- **Stop Insider Trading Act (H.R. 7008, 119th Congress).** Rep. Bryan Steil, introduced January 12, 2026. **Passed the House 232–198 on July 22, 2026 (Roll no. 280)**; read the second time in the Senate and placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar, Calendar No. 548, August 6, 2026.

The H.R. 7008 outcome is the most instructive event in this landscape, and it cuts in our favor on nearly every design question our memo raised:

- **It bans purchases, not holdings.** Members may keep existing portfolios. Rep. Bonnie Watson Coleman objected that it "fails to actually end the ownership or sale of stock by members."
- **It exempts the President and Vice President** — the §208 carve-out survives untouched.
- **It covers no judges.**
- **CLC, whose preferred vehicle it displaced, opposed it**: "The Stop Insider Trading Act purportedly seeks to address widespread concerns around congressional stock trading. Yet, it would not actually ban lawmakers from trading stocks..."
- **A voter-identification provision was attached**, and the reform that polls 86% with both parties passed on a near-party-line vote: 232–198, with 218 Republicans, 13 Democrats and one independent in favor.

That last fact is the strongest single argument in this memo for the constitutional route. **A reform with 86–87% cross-partisan public support (87R/88–90D, UMD 2023) became a party-line vote the moment it entered the legislative process attached to an unrelated rider.** The statutory path has now demonstrated, on the floor, both of the failure modes our memo predicted: the covered class gets negotiated down to Congress alone, and the vehicle gets loaded with a partisan rider that destroys the consensus.

## Language comparison vs. our draft

No org has constitutional text, so this compares our Article 3 against the **best statutory drafts and org platforms** — the only comparators that exist.

### Section by section

**Section 1 (the trading ban).** Ours covers President, VP, Senators, Representatives, Justices and judges. Every org platform covers **Congress only**; POGO alone argues for three branches, and would add "top employees." Ours reaches an "individual security, digital asset, or contract of speculation whose value their official conduct may affect"; the coalition's list is more concrete ("individual stocks, bonds, digital assets or other securities, including coverage of private equity, venture capital and hedge funds"). Ours permits holding through "a diversified fund or qualified blind trust"; the coalition permits "diversified mutual funds, ETFs and index funds" and CLC warns the QBT route can be a "worst-case scenario." Ours omits spouses and dependent children entirely, delegating that to Congress; **every** org platform names them explicitly.

**Section 2 (ending the presidential exemption).** No org has drafted text. The Brennan Center presses the divestment/recusal distinction our section encodes. No bill that has moved covers the President; H.R. 7008 expressly does not.

**Section 3 (judicial code).** CREW's three-retired-judge panel and Fix the Court's four enumerated gaps are the substantive counterparts. Both are statutory/internal; neither claims Congress's power to prescribe a binding code as a constitutional matter, which is the contested question our text settles.

**Section 4 (independent enforcement).** Closest org counterpart is the coalition's demand for "a clear and workable enforcement mechanism that can serve as a functional deterrent." No org generalizes this into a cross-branch no-self-judging principle. This section is ours alone.

**Section 5 (disclosure).** Well covered statutorily; the coalition and CLC both press searchable electronic filing and meaningful penalties. Nothing constitutional.

**Section 6 (effective dates).** No org counterpart. CLC warns against the inverse risk: "a proposal should not authorize ethics committees to waive or extend compliance deadlines beyond a very short period."

### Trap notes: does org language avoid them?

| Trap (from ETHICS_RESEARCH.md) | Org language | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Breadth trap** — stop at constitutional officers | Coalition stops at members + spouses + dependents (narrower than ours in office-type, broader in family). POGO would extend to "top employees of all three branches" | Coalition **avoids**; POGO **falls into it** |
| **Wealth-penalty trap** — keep the safe harbor | Coalition and NTU both keep diversified-fund exceptions; NTU makes it the condition of its support. CLC and POGO prefer absolute ban / divest-with-no-exception | NTU + coalition **avoid**; CLC and POGO **fall into it** |
| **Definition trap** — broad principle, delegate details | Org texts are statutes: maximally specific by design, and correspondingly obsolescence-prone | N/A — inapplicable to statutory drafting, and the reason their text can't be lifted |
| **Who-enforces (presidential)** | No org text reaches the President | **Unaddressed everywhere** |
| **Who-enforces (judicial)** | CREW's retired-judge panel: inside judiciary, no political trigger-puller | CREW **avoids** — best model available |
| **Who-enforces (structural)** | No org proposes a single national ethics commission | **Avoided by silence** |
| **Politicization trap** | Fix the Court's "all nine are culpable" framing is symmetric | Fix the Court **avoids** |

### If we adopted org language 100%

There is no constitutional text to adopt, so this table reads: *what if we replaced our Article 3 with the coalition platform / Restore Trust in Congress Act architecture?*

| | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Gain** | Concrete asset taxonomy (private equity, venture capital, hedge funds, digital assets). Explicit spouse and dependent-child coverage. An enforcement-standard warning we lack (avoid "knowingly"). Immediate coalition alignment with ten organizations and 142 House cosponsors. Battle-tested statutory language that has survived committee drafting. |
| **Lose** | The President and Vice President (Section 2 disappears). The entire judiciary (Section 3 disappears). The cross-branch independence principle (Section 4 disappears — no org has it). Constitutional status itself: everything becomes repealable by simple majority, which is what the last fourteen years show is not durable. The 87% presidential extension and the 90% judicial-ethics number — our two strongest polling assets — would have no text to attach to. |
| **Need to add back** | Essentially the whole article. Sections 2, 3, 4 and 6 have no org counterpart at all. |

**Conclusion: adoption is not available for Article 3.** The gain column is entirely definitional and belongs in implementing legislation, which our text already delegates to Congress. The lose column is the article.

## Adversaries

There is no organized adversary org in this space — no counterpart to the groups that organize against campaign-finance or term-limits amendments. Opposition is diffuse and comes from three places:

1. **Officeholders themselves**, which is the structural point of the whole article. The record is bipartisan: fourteen years of statutory failure under both parties' control, and the *Restore Trust in Congress Act* sitting without a markup for nearly a year despite 142 cosponsors.
2. **Argument-level opposition**, not organizational: that members should be free to participate in markets like other citizens, and that a ban deters people of means from public service. NTU's carve-out for diversified funds is the reform movement's own answer to this, and it is why our safe harbor matters.
3. **Reform orgs opposing each other's vehicles.** CLC publicly opposed H.R. 7008 — the bill that actually passed — as insufficient. This is the live conflict in the space: incrementalists versus comprehensive-bill advocates.

We found no Article V convention opposition specific to ethics, because no ethics convention effort exists to oppose.

## Partnership vs. conflict

**Endorsement is low-risk and mutual-gain.** These are the most-cited nonpartisan good-government organizations in the field, and our memo already relies on CLC, Fix the Court, POGO and CREW as sources. Nothing in their public positions conflicts with our draft; they are simply working a different instrument.

**Partisan-coding risk is real but manageable.** The coalition of ten skews left in composition (Progressive Change Institute, Stand Up America) and in funding. Two counterweights are available and both are load-bearing for our full-spectrum bar: **NTU**, which endorses the reform on taxpayer-and-trust grounds and independently validates our safe harbor, and **Issue One's ReFormers Caucus**, which supplies paired former-member validators from both parties. Any public alignment we make should name a right-of-center endorser in the same breath, and the H.R. 5106 original-cosponsor list — sixteen members alternating by party, sponsored by a Republican — is the cleanest cross-partisan artifact in the entire ethics landscape.

**The genuine conflict is scope, and we should expect it.** Our article covers three branches; the coalition's bill covers one. If we join their coalition, we are the group asking to reopen a scope compromise they spent months negotiating — Issue One describes the Restore Trust in Congress Act as arriving "after months of negotiations." Endorsing their bill while advocating our amendment is coherent (statute now, durability later, and POGO's own sequencing says the same), but it must be said that way explicitly or it reads as undercutting them.

**The second conflict is the safe harbor.** CLC rates the QBT route as risking a "worst-case scenario," and POGO wants divestment with no pre-office exception. Our Section 1 constitutionalizes the safe harbor they are lukewarm on. Our justification is polling — the 69% "goes too far" finding on forced sale, and NTU's conditional support — and it is a defensible disagreement, but it is a real one with our closest allies, and it should not be papered over.

**Fix the Court is the natural first partner.** It is small, specialist, has the cleanest cross-spectrum operating record, and works the one half of our article where we most need credibility against the politicization trap. Its research output on state and peer-democracy conduct commissions is already load-bearing in our memo.
