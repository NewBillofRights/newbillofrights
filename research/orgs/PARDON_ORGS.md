## The headline finding for this article

**There is no American Promise or U.S. Term Limits for pardon reform.** The founder's adoption preference presumes an org that has invested deeply in *amendment language* on its issue. On this issue that org does not exist. The landscape splits cleanly into two groups, and neither is the thing the direction contemplates:

1. **Organizations with deep pardon expertise that have deliberately declined to draft an amendment.** Protect Democracy, the Campaign Legal Center, CREW, and the Brennan Center have all published substantial pardon work, and every one of them frames its recommendations as things Congress can do *without* amending the Constitution. The Campaign Legal Center says so in terms: there are "solutions Congress could pursue without a constitutional amendment." Their considered institutional position is the opposite of ours — they argue the pardon power is *already* limited and that the deficit is enforcement, not text.
2. **Members of Congress with drafted amendment text and no organization behind it.** Rep. Steve Cohen (five introductions since 2017) and Rep. Johnny Olszewski (H.J.Res.135, the "Pardon Integrity Act") have actual constitutional language. Neither has a hearing, a markup, or an advocacy organization that has adopted the text as its own campaign. Advocacy groups have supplied endorsement quotes for press releases; none has run a ratification campaign.

The one place where drafted pardon-transparency language has actually **passed a legislature and reached voters** is a state: Kentucky's SB 10, on the November 3, 2026 ballot, passed 36-0 in the Senate and 82-12 in the House. It is the closest thing in the landscape to a tested, cross-partisan clemency-transparency text — and it is state, not federal.

**Adoption bearing:** for Article 8 the honest answer is that there is no established org text to adopt wholesale. The nearest federal text (Cohen) is a categorical-exclusions amendment with no transparency provision at all, drafted by one caucus, and it omits our lead section. The nearest bipartisan federal text (Olszewski/Bacon) is a congressional-veto mechanism our own corpus rejected on polling on 2026-08-18. Retaining our draft is the evidence-supported outcome here; the useful borrowing is at the phrase level, not the article level.

## Per-org detail

### Protect Democracy — the seed org, and the most consequential negative finding

**Identity and scale.** Protect Democracy Project, 501(c)(3), Washington DC, EIN **81-4777062**, tax-exempt since August 2017. Latest Form 990 on ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer (fiscal year ending December 2024, filed Nov. 11, 2025): revenue **$55,482,925**, expenses **$34,368,875**, total assets **$94,771,289**, net assets $93,052,687; other salaries and wages $14,817,272. President/Executive Director **Ian Bassin** ($377,276). This is by a wide margin the best-resourced organization in the landscape — roughly eight times CREW's budget.

**Proposed language: none.** This is the finding that matters. The org's flagship pardon publication is **"Checking the Pardon Power: Constitutional limitations and options for preventing abuse" (April 2024)**, authored by **Grant Tudor and Justin Florence** with contributions from Genevieve Nadeau, Erica Newland, Aisha Woodward, Amanda Carpenter and Lara Hicks. The paper's thesis is that the pardon power is *already* constrained and that the remedy is to enforce existing limits. Its own summary of recommendations, verbatim from the "Putting Pardons in Check" page:

> **Congress**
> • Investigate potential abuses of the pardon power and mandate reporting requirements to assist its oversight functions
> • Amend federal bribery and obstruction of justice laws to clarify that they apply to the president and to granted or offered pardons
> • Impeach and remove from office a president who abuses the pardon power
>
> **The Judiciary**
> • Adjudicate whether certain pardons are valid and void those that are not
> • Provide remedies to victims whose rights have been infringed upon by unconstitutional pardons
> • Develop case law to guide the president and potential pardon applicants
>
> **The Executive**
> • Through the Department of Justice, investigate potentially unlawful uses of the pardon power
> • Prosecute criminal uses of the pardon power, as when pardons are used to obstruct justice or as part of a bribery scheme
> • Among attorneys who advise and assist with pardons, faithfully uphold one's oath of office to support and defend the Constitution

The paper's third congressional recommendation is a **Sense of Congress resolution**: Congress "should delimit the constitutional boundaries of the pardon power through a Sense of Congress resolution. While non-binding, the resolution would clarify the legislature's understanding of appropriate limits of the president's power in order to uphold the Constitution." A non-binding resolution is the *weakest* instrument in the landscape, and it is what the best-funded org in the field recommends. The word "amendment," in the Article V sense, does not appear as a recommendation anywhere in the paper's recommendations or conclusion. The conclusion instead argues: "THE PARDON POWER IS not unlimited. It cannot be used to place the president above the law … Like any executive power, it is constrained by the rest of the Constitution."

**The endorsement, which is not the same thing.** Protect Democracy's Grant Tudor supplied a supportive quote for Cohen's January 9, 2025 reintroduction release:

> "The pardon power — which is not limitless — has been abused by presidents of both parties and with increasing frequency in recent years. This proposal to reform and clarify the President's pardon power is urgently needed to ensure that presidents cannot place themselves, their political interests, their families and other close contacts above the law. It is Congress' constitutional prerogative and responsibility to both prevent and expose pardons which are granted not to further public welfare but for personal and corrupt reasons."

Note what the quote does and does not do: it endorses *this proposal* as urgently needed, and it uses the "both parties" framing our own mission uses. It does not commit the organization to an amendment campaign, and the organization's own paper — published nine months earlier and never withdrawn — recommends against needing one. A partnership built on this quote would be building on a press release, not a program.

**Route and track record.** Statutory and litigative. Protect Democracy is the principal institutional backer of the **Protecting Our Democracy Act**, whose pardon title would clarify that a self-pardon "has no legal effect and shall not deprive a court of jurisdiction over the pardoned offense," require the attorney general to submit pardon-related investigative materials to Congress for self-, family-, and obstruction-adjacent pardons, and amend the federal bribery statute to "explicitly include the offering or granting of a pardon or commutation by the president or vice-president as a possible predicate for criminal liability." PODA passed the House 220-208 on Dec. 9, 2021 with one Republican and died in the Senate (already in [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md)).

**Partisan coding.** Founded 2017 by former Obama White House counsel staff (Bassin, Florence); consistently presents itself as cross-partisan and its board has included **Kori Schake** (of the American Enterprise Institute), which is a real cross-partisan signal rather than a cosmetic one. Its coalition work on the Security from Political Interference in Justice Act ran with Republicans for the Rule of Law, Stand Up Republic, the Niskanen Center, and TechFreedom alongside Brennan, Common Cause, CREW, POGO, and Public Citizen. The org reads center-left in practice with genuine right-of-center relationships — better than most in the field, and better than CREW.

**One caveat for our corpus.** Protect Democracy is also the source of the 2024 battleground polling this project already labels advocacy-commissioned (77% family ban, 64R/88D). Using their polling *and* their coalition *and* their framing would concentrate our evidentiary dependence on a single center-left institution. [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) already flags the polling as needing independent replication; the May 2026 YouGov instrument has since supplied it.

### Campaign Legal Center — the most explicit "no amendment" position

**Identity and scale.** Campaign Legal Center Inc., 501(c)(3), Washington DC, EIN **04-3608387**; affiliated 501(c)(4) **Campaign Legal Center Action**, EIN 83-3411170. Latest ProPublica-extracted 990 (FY2023): revenue **$21,481,014**, expenses **$17,725,339**, total assets **$45,817,252**. CLC Action FY2023 revenue $62,705, expenses $470,616.

**Proposed language: none, by design.** CLC's **"Campaign Legal Center's Proposed Solutions to Corruption of the Presidential Pardon Power" (June 8, 2026)**, by **Brendan Fischer, Nicole Hansen, and Eric Kashdan**, states the position directly:

> "Although the Constitution grants broad power to the president to issue pardons, there are solutions Congress could pursue without a constitutional amendment to address many of the worst abuses and help restore the rule of law. These include:
> 1. Strengthening disclosure requirements for pardon recipients and lobbyists;
> 2. Clarifying civil and criminal liability for pardon seekers who corruptly seek to buy a pardon; and,
> 3. Establishing a congressional commission to study presidential pardons."

The document's design logic is worth recording because it is a genuine strategic insight and it bears on our Section 4. CLC routes entirely around the president, whom it treats as unreachable after *Trump v. United States*: Congress should legislate on "pardon-seekers, intermediaries, or government officials other than the president (who likely has immunity for the exercise of core constitutional powers, including the pardon power, following the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. United States*)." Its lead proposal requires **pardon recipients** to disclose to DOJ anything of value — "any contribution, donation, gift, service, payment, transfer, contract, investment, goods, or other benefit" — provided to the president, the president's immediate family, entities financed or controlled by them or advancing their "financial, political, electoral, or reputational benefit," entities in which they hold a financial interest, or "any lobbyist, intermediary, or other person in connection with seeking or obtaining executive clemency," with DOJ publishing the reports on a public website, a suggested inflation-adjusted **$10,000** threshold, and a reporting window covering the year before the president took office plus four years after the pardon.

**Track record and framing.** CLC endorses the **No Payoffs For Pardons Act** (Blumenthal, Schumer, Morelle, Aug. 3, 2026 — no Republican cosponsors, per [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md)). Its public framing is "Presidential pardon power is meant to correct injustice, not reward wealthy donors or political loyalists." Its March 12, 2026 analysis is titled "Inside the Pardon Playbook: An Analysis of President Trump's Clemency Abuses."

**Partisan coding.** CLC was founded by **Trevor Potter**, a Republican former FEC chairman, which is its historic cross-partisan credential and a real one. Its current pardon work does not carry that balance: the June 2026 document's evidentiary base is Trump-era grants almost exclusively, with Clinton's Marc Rich pardon appearing once as a historical analogue ("President Trump is not the first president to misuse the pardon power"). By our mission's paired-abuse standard the document is one-sided in emphasis even though it is not one-sided in principle.

### CREW — statutory, and the most left-coded of the four

**Identity and scale.** Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington Inc., 501(c)(3), Washington DC, EIN **30-0445391**. Latest ProPublica-extracted 990 (FY2023): revenue **$6,685,736**, expenses **$8,017,420**, total assets **$10,662,836**. The smallest budget of the four national orgs here.

**Proposed language: none.** CREW's pardon recommendations appear in **"Five key ways Congress can ensure accountability during a lawless era"** (February 2025), framed entirely as additions to PODA. Recommendation 3, "Shed light on the president's use of the pardon power," proposes: incorporating Sen. Cortez Masto's standalone bill so the **DOJ Inspector General** investigates covered pardons; extending the disclosure and investigation provisions to "any pardon granted to a former president"; and requiring **pardon recipients to file an annual financial disclosure form for three years** narrowly limited to "any gifts to the president who issued the pardon," administered through a simplified Office of Government Ethics online process.

CREW's own legal analysis states the constraint that explains why it does not draft an amendment: "under Supreme Court case law Congress cannot limit the effect of a president's pardon or restrict to what 'class of offenders' a pardon applies. But Congress can criminalize a president's corrupt agreement to grant a pardon in exchange for a payment … without preventing the president from exercising their exclusive authority to grant a pardon or affecting the validity of any accepted pardon." That is a precise statement of the *Garland* problem — and CREW's answer is to accept it and work around it, where ours is to amend past it.

**Partisan coding.** CREW is the most openly adversarial of the four. The February 2025 document opens by characterizing an administration as having "little to no desire to abide by the Constitution," and CREW's 2024 policy report is titled "Countering authoritarianism." Association would code us. Its analytical work is nonetheless careful and its 990 is clean; the issue is framing, not rigor.

### Public Citizen and the Brennan Center — endorsers, not drafters

**Public Citizen.** Public Citizen Foundation Inc. (c3), EIN **52-1263996**, FY2023 revenue $13,389,271, expenses $18,066,747; Public Citizen Inc. (c4), EIN **23-7104508**, FY2023 revenue $5,728,329, expenses $7,135,504. Co-President **Lisa Gilbert** endorsed the Cohen amendment in his January 9, 2025 release — "Public Citizen strongly supports Representative Cohen's common-sense legislation to limit pardon power" — in a quote whose surrounding sentences are about January 6 and one named president, which is precisely the incumbent-coding [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) identifies as the trap that kills this amendment. Public Citizen has no drafted amendment text of its own on this issue and also endorsed the Schumer/Blumenthal statutory bill.

**Brennan Center for Justice.** Its standing explainer, "How to Prevent Abuse of the President's Pardon Power" (**February 24, 2021, Caroline Fredrickson**), *describes* the Cohen amendment but places its own weight on statutes: PODA, a transparency bill requiring presidents to "publish the issue date, recipient, and full text of each pardon or reprieve," and congressional oversight hearings. No Brennan-drafted amendment text exists. Note the date: the Brennan piece is five years old and predates *Trump v. United States*, the 2025 January 6 grants, and the Biden family pardons. Do not cite it as a current institutional position without re-verifying.

### Rep. Steve Cohen — the only sustained federal amendment text, and its full drafting history

This is the seed the assignment named, and the drafting history turns out to be more useful than the current text. **All five introductions were fetched as govinfo XML and the operative language compared.**

**H.J.Res.13, 119th Congress, introduced January 9, 2025** (Cohen, for himself, **Mr. Johnson of Georgia** and **Mr. Levin** — two cosponsors), referred to the Committee on the Judiciary. Full verbatim operative text:

> **1.** The President shall not have the power to grant pardons and reprieves to—
> (1) the President's self;
> (2) any person, up to a third degree relation, of the President, or a spouse thereof;
> (3) any current or former member of the President's administration;
> (4) any person who worked on the President's presidential campaign as a paid employee;
> (5) any person or entity for an offense that was motivated by a direct and significant personal or pecuniary interest of any of the foregoing persons; or
> (6) any person or entity for an offense that was at the direction of, or in coordination with, the President.
> Any pardon issued for a corrupt purpose shall be invalid.
>
> **2.** The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

**The drafting history, verified across all five texts.**

| Resolution | Congress | Introduced | Form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [H.J.Res.120](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/house-joint-resolution/120) | 115th | Oct. 31, 2017 | Single sentence, enumerated relatives |
| [H.J.Res.8](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/house-joint-resolution/8) | 116th | Jan. 3, 2019 | Identical to 2017 |
| [H.J.Res.4](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-joint-resolution/4) | 117th | Jan. 4, 2021 | Restructured to (1)–(6); "third degree relation"; corrupt-purpose sentence added |
| [H.J.Res.77](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-joint-resolution/77) | 118th | June 21, 2023 | Identical to 2021 |
| [H.J.Res.13](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13) | 119th | Jan. 9, 2025 | "himself or herself" → "the President's self"; **seven-year ratification deadline dropped**; **§2 enforcement clause added** |

Three drafting lessons are worth taking even though we are not adopting the text:

1. **The 2017/2019 text enumerated kinship by name** — "brother, sister, brother-in-law, sister-in-law, spouse, parent, child, or grandchild or to the spouse of the President's grandchild, to the President's aunt, uncle, nephew or niece … first or second cousin … mother-in-law, father-in-law, son-in-law, or daughter-in-law." In 2021 Cohen replaced the entire list with "up to a third degree relation, of the President, or a spouse thereof." **Our Section 3 uses the same solution** ("within the third degree of kinship … or to the spouse of any such relative"), and our version is the better-drafted of the two because it names both the President's relatives and the President's spouse's relatives and because our memo anchors the measure to [28 U.S.C. §455(b)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/455)'s existing civil-law computation. Independent convergence on the third-degree measure by the only two serious drafters in the field is a point of confidence for our Section 3.
2. **The 119th text dropped the seven-year ratification deadline** that all four prior versions carried. Our draft carries no deadline either; this is a live drafting choice a sponsor has now made in the same direction.
3. **The 119th text added a congressional enforcement clause.** Our article has none. This is the single clearest thing we could borrow if the founder wants one — but note our Section 5 savings clause is doing different work, and an enforcement clause plus a "void" clause interact in ways the memo has not analyzed.

**Route and track record.** Congressional Article V route. **No hearing, no markup, and no floor vote in any of five Congresses since 2017.** Cosponsors have been Democrats throughout (H.J.Res.77 in the 118th was introduced with *none*). This is the packaging failure [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) already documents.

**Partisan coding — with one genuinely mitigating fact.** The text is a Democratic-caucus artifact by cosponsorship. But Cohen's own account, in his own words on January 9, 2025, is the strongest cross-partisan claim available for it:

> "I first introduced a version of this amendment during the 115th Congress, in 2017. I have been interested in rooting out corrupt uses of the clemency power for decades. In 1977, as Vice President of the Tennessee Constitutional Convention, I offered reforms to the governor's pardon power to address questionable pardons by a Democratic governor. Party has never been a factor; it's a matter of principle."

The 1977 Tennessee reference is checkable and, if it holds, is a rare instance of a pardon reformer whose first target was a governor of his own party — a fact worth keeping in the record for the "both parties" argument, though we should not put it on the site without verifying the Tennessee convention record independently (see **Unverifiable**, below).

### Rep. Olszewski's Pardon Integrity Act (H.J.Res.135) — the only bipartisan federal amendment

**H.J.Res.135, 119th Congress, introduced December 18, 2025** by Rep. Johnny Olszewski (D-MD-2) "for himself and Mr. Bacon," referred to House Judiciary. Verbatim operative text:

> **1.** The President shall transmit a notification of a grant of a Reprieve or Pardon within 3 days to the President pro tempore of the Senate and the Speaker of the House of Representatives. Upon receipt of such notification, Congress may, within 30 days, introduce a Bill to nullify the Reprieve or Pardon. Upon introduction of a Bill to nullify, Congress may pass, within 60 days, the Bill by two-thirds vote of both Houses. If a Bill to nullify a Reprieve or Pardon is not passed by Congress, the Reprieve or Pardon shall become effective on the date that is 90 days after the date in which the President notifies the President pro tempore of the Senate and the Speaker of the House of Representatives of a Reprieve or Pardon.
> **2.** Any Bill to nullify a Reprieve or Pardon that is signed by 20 Representatives or 5 Senators shall be voted on.
> **3.** If the President fails to notify the President pro tempore of the Senate and the Speaker of the House of Representatives of a grant of a Reprieve or Pardon within 3 days, the Reprieve or Pardon shall have no force or effect.
> **4.** The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon of an offense against the United States that was previously nullified by Congress.
> **5.** The Congress shall have power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the provisions of this article.

**Correction to the existing corpus.** [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) records "Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE) as original cosponsor (six further Democratic cosponsors through July 2026)." Verified against the live congress.gov cosponsor page on 2026-08-19, the roster is **seven cosponsors total, of whom Bacon is the one original and the one Republican**: Bacon (R-NE-2, 12/18/2025, original), Walkinshaw (D-VA-11, 3/27/2026), Levin (D-CA-49, 4/15/2026), Thanedar (D-MI-13, 5/15/2026), Julie Johnson (D-TX-32, 5/15/2026), Elfreth (D-MD-3, 5/20/2026), Whitesides (D-CA-27, 7/09/2026). Party split on the page: Democratic 6, Republican 1. So "six further Democratic cosponsors" is right in substance; the count to state publicly is **seven cosponsors, six Democrats and one Republican**. Latest action remains referral, 12/18/2025 — still no hearing. *(Note: the govinfo bulk BILLSTATUS XML for this resolution, updateDate 2026-07-18, does not surface Bacon in the same cosponsor field pattern as the others; congress.gov is the authority and shows him as original. Prefer the congress.gov page for any cosponsor claim.)*

**The bipartisanship is real but thin, and both sponsors framed it carefully.** From Olszewski's February 16, 2026 release announcing Bacon's support — Bacon's own words:

> "Presidential pardons are an important constitutional authority, but like all powers held by the executive branch, these authorities benefit from the appropriate checks and balances the Constitution envisioned. Across multiple administrations, we've seen legitimate questions raised about how this authority has been used at the same time, the ability of Congress to provide oversight has weakened. Frankly, it is clear to me the pardon authority has been abused. I'm pleased to cosponsor Rep. Olszewski's Pardon Integrity Act, a constitutional amendment that establishes a narrow, commonsense guardrail that preserves the pardon power while ensuring Congress can fulfill its constitutional role to provide accountability and uphold the rule of law."

"Across multiple administrations" and "preserves the pardon power" are exactly the register our COPY_VOICE_GUIDE requires, and Bacon is the only sitting Republican in the landscape on record supporting *any* pardon amendment. That is the single most valuable relationship datum in this memo.

**Why we still cannot adopt it.** The mechanism is the congressional-disapproval check our own corpus **rejected on 2026-08-18** — YouGov May 13–15, 2026 read "giving Congress the power to reverse a pardon" at 48% overall, **30% R** / 46% I / 73% D, the weakest item in the entire pardon battery and thirty-one points below the self-pardon clause ([PARDON_SELF_PARDON_CLAUSE.md](../candidates/clauses/PARDON_SELF_PARDON_CLAUSE.md) §2). Beyond polling, the text's §1 suspends the effect of **every** pardon for 90 days, which is the mercy-destruction trap [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) names, and Rhode Island's senate-consent model is the recorded evidence that a legislative gate suppresses clemency to near-zero.

**The one clause worth stealing.** H.J.Res.135 §3 — "If the President fails to notify … within 3 days, the Reprieve or Pardon shall have no force or effect" — is a **notification-or-void** rule, structurally identical to our Section 1's "No reprieve or pardon shall take effect until so published." Ours is better: it conditions effect on *publication to the public with reasons*, not on private transmission to two congressional officers, and it does not impose a waiting period. But the existence of a bipartisan federal text using the same enforcement architecture is meaningful support for our Section 1's design being drafitable and defensible.

### Kentucky SB 10 (2026) — the tested cross-partisan transparency text

Not an org, but the assignment covers "major state campaigns whose ballot language passed," and this is the strongest single piece of persuasion evidence produced by this pass. **Kentucky Senate Bill 10 (2026)** places an amendment to **Kentucky Constitution §77** on the ballot for **November 3, 2026**. Verbatim, with the measure's own underline/strike convention (underlined text added, struck text deleted):

> (1) The Governor ~~He~~ shall have power to ~~:~~
> (a) Remit fines and forfeitures; and ~~,~~ (b) Commute sentences and ~~,~~ grant reprieves and pardons, except: 1. In case of impeachment; or ~~,~~ 2. For the period beginning sixty days prior to the date of the gubernatorial election and ending the fifth Tuesday succeeding the election.
> (2) The Governor ~~and he~~ shall file with each application therefor a statement of the reasons for his or her decision thereon, which application and statement shall always be open to public inspection.
> (3) In cases of treason, the Governor ~~he~~ shall have power to grant reprieves until the end of the next session of the General Assembly, in which the power of pardoning shall be vested; but he or she shall have no power to remit the fees of the Clerk, Sheriff or Commonwealth's Attorney in penal or criminal cases.

**Route and margins.** Legislatively referred constitutional amendment; Kentucky requires a 60% vote in one session. Introduced January 8, 2026; passed the Senate **36-0** on January 20, 2026; passed the House **82-12** on April 1, 2026 with amendments (Republicans 73-2, Democrats 9-10); Senate concurred **36-1** the same day. Ballotpedia's partisanship index reads it Lean Republican (+44.0), with **98.1% Republican support and 54.2% Democratic support**. Sponsor **State Sen. Chris McDaniel (R-23)**: "My message to Kentucky voters is simple: If you were outraged by the heinous late-hour pardons of the past, take those motivations to the ballot box. Let's reform our state constitution to better respect the will of Kentucky's justice system." Ballotpedia has located **no organized opposition campaign**.

**Why this matters for Article 8.** Our memo's persuasion strategy for Republican audiences rests on the claim that conditioning clemency is a conservative governance tradition. Until now that claim rested on constitutions ratified in 1939–1943 (Alabama, Georgia). Kentucky 2026 is the same argument in the present tense, carried by Republican votes, in the state whose 2019 governor-alone pardon scandal [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) already cites. Note carefully what Kentucky's clause (2) requires — a **statement of reasons, always open to public inspection** — which is our Section 1's core, and note what it adds that we deliberately do not have: an **election-window blackout**, which our memo explicitly declined ("do not substitute a lame-duck blackout (which would also bar legitimate end-of-term mercy)"). Kentucky's voters will render a verdict on the blackout design on November 3, 2026, and that result should be re-checked and added to the corpus after the election.

| **§1** Publication with recipient, offenses, reasons; report to Congress; no effect until published | **Absent entirely** | Partial — 3-day *notification to two officers*, void if not sent; no reasons, no publication | **Yes** — statement of reasons, "always open to public inspection" |
| **§2** Individual naming; specified offenses; no future offenses | **Absent** | Absent | Absent |
| **§3** No spouse/third-degree-kin pardons | **Yes** — "up to a third degree relation … or a spouse thereof" | Absent | Absent |
| **§4** Void if sold or self-shielding; bribery notwithstanding grant | Partial — "direct and significant personal or pecuniary interest"; "corrupt purpose shall be invalid" | Absent | Absent |
| **§5** Commutations included; savings clause | Absent (no savings clause) | Absent; §4 bars re-pardon of nullified offense | Covers commutations by structure |
| *Not in ours* | Self-pardon ban; administration officials; paid campaign employees; coordination clause; congressional enforcement clause | Congressional two-thirds nullification; 90-day suspension; 20-member/5-senator trigger | Election-window blackout |

### Trap-by-trap: does their language avoid what our memo warns about?

[PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) identifies four traps. Cohen's text is the one with drafted categorical exclusions, so it is the fair comparison.

- **Incumbent-coding trap.** *Cohen: fails.* Five introductions, no Republican cosponsor of note, no hearing in nine years, and endorsement quotes framed around January 6 and one named president. *Olszewski: partially avoids* — Bacon's "across multiple administrations" framing is the correct register, and a Republican original cosponsor is a real asset. *Kentucky: avoids entirely* — 98% Republican support for a clemency-transparency rule.
- **Mercy-destruction trap.** *Cohen: avoids* — categorical exclusions add no decision-maker and no delay, consistent with the Hamiltonian "sole fiat" answer our memo already drafted. *Olszewski: fails squarely* — the 90-day suspension of effect delays every act of legitimate mercy, and Rhode Island is the evidence. *Ours: avoids* by design (publication is a disclosure condition, not an approval condition).
- **Review-standard trap.** *Cohen: fails worst.* "Any pardon issued for a corrupt purpose shall be invalid," plus "motivated by a direct and significant personal or pecuniary interest," plus "at the direction of, or in coordination with, the President" are three separate motive/factfinding tests, each requiring a court to excavate presidential intent. Our memo already identified this as the reason to hold back the conspirators clause and to draft our §4 as a *transaction* test with the purpose half as the severable fallback. Cohen's text has no severability and no savings clause, so a court striking the corrupt-purpose sentence has no drafted instruction on what survives.
- **Line-drawing trap (family).** *Cohen: same solution as ours*, and this is genuine convergence. His "up to a third degree relation, of the President, or a spouse thereof" and our "any relative of the President or the President's spouse within the third degree of kinship, or to the spouse of any such relative" reach nearly the same set; ours is more explicit about the spouse's relatives.

### If we adopted their language 100%

**Adopting Cohen's H.J.Res.13 wholesale:**

| Gain | Lose | Need to add back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A self-pardon ban in the text (currently only a gated candidate clause at 61% R) | **Our entire §1** — publication, reasons, no-effect-until-published: the article's lead provision and its strongest polled item (79%, 79R/83D) | All of §1 |
| Exclusion of administration officials and paid campaign employees | **Our entire §2** — individual naming and offense specification; the answer to blanket and preemptive grants | All of §2 |
| The coordination clause (Randolph's "traytors may be his own instruments" pedigree) | **Our §5 savings clause** — the sentence our memo says every spokesperson should memorize | All of §5 |
| A congressional enforcement clause | The associates ban at **39% R** and the congressional items our corpus rejected | Severability for the motive-test provisions |
| Nine years of sponsor persistence and a filed bill number | Nine years of one-caucus coding, no hearing, no Republican cosponsor | An independent, forward-looking polling base |

**Net:** adopting Cohen's text would trade our two best-polling, least-incumbent-coded provisions for provisions our own gate check has already held back or rejected on independent data. It inverts the article's strategic order.

**Adopting Olszewski's H.J.Res.135 wholesale:** not viable. It is a single-mechanism amendment whose mechanism polls at **30% R**, was moved to rejected in our corpus on 2026-08-18, and carries the mercy-destruction trap in its own text. Its bipartisan cosponsorship is a relationship asset, not a language asset.

**Adopting Kentucky's clause (2) as a model for our §1:** partially attractive. "Shall file with each application therefor a statement of the reasons for his or her decision thereon, which application and statement shall always be open to public inspection" is tested, near-unanimously passed, and Republican-sponsored. Two differences argue for keeping ours: Kentucky requires filing reasons on **each application** (including denials), which is a heavier administrative load on a federal pipeline already suffering the clemency drought our memo documents; and Kentucky's rule has no "no effect until published" enforcement, which is the clause that makes ours self-executing. Our §1 is the stronger federal draft. **Kentucky's value to us is evidentiary, not textual** — cite it as proof that clemency transparency passes in a red state, and re-check the November 3, 2026 result.

## Adversaries

**Cato Institute — the most substantive opposition, and it is aimed at the Olszewski text, not ours.** "Can the Pardon Power Be Saved from Its Own Abusers?" by **Mike Fox**, Legal Fellow, published in *The Hill* on **February 22, 2026**. Cato's argument, in its own words: the Pardon Integrity Act "would further politicize the pardon process, putting justice even more out of reach for everyday Americans"; Hamilton in *Federalist* No. 74 argued the authority must remain "as little as possible fettered"; with "more than 5,000 federal criminal statutes and an estimated 400,000 regulatory crimes … the pardon is not an abuse but a vital check on a runaway bureaucracy"; a congressional veto "would paralyze the only mechanism capable of bypassing political gridlock," and "mercy for unpopular figures … would likely perish in a politicized floor vote, discouraging future presidents from helping the powerless"; and finally "a two-thirds supermajority to override a president is a Herculean threshold that few would cross against their own party," so the amendment "would likely not accomplish much."

**Why this is the most important adversary read in the memo.** Note carefully what Cato objects to: a **second decision-maker**, **delay**, and **politicized review**. Our Article 8 has none of the three. Every one of Cato's arguments lands on H.J.Res.135 and glances off our draft — which is exactly the outcome [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) engineered when it wrote that the lead provisions "add no second decision-maker and no delay — they leave the 'sole fiat' intact and constrain only its beneficiaries and its secrecy." Cato's piece is the best available independent confirmation that our design choice was the right one, and it should be read as a **road test of our draft** rather than only as opposition. Cato has not, in this piece, addressed a publication-with-reasons requirement or a family exclusion.

**Bauer and Goldsmith — the scholarly "no amendment needed" position**, already in [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) (Lawfare, Feb. 26, 2020; *After Trump*). Their claim that "Congress has much more room to regulate abuses of the pardon power than is commonly assumed" is the intellectual foundation for the entire statutory-only posture of Protect Democracy, CLC, and CREW. Our memo's answer stands and should be the standard reply to any of the four orgs: statutes can regulate the conduct *around* a pardon but cannot make a pardon *void*, and *Trump v. United States* hardened the "conclusive and preclusive" reading after Bauer and Goldsmith wrote.

**No organized opposition campaign to pardon reform exists.** Ballotpedia located no opposition committee against Kentucky SB 10, and no organization in this landscape is organized *against* pardon limits as such. The opposition is intellectual (Cato, the originalist *Federalist* 74 reading) rather than institutional. That is a materially easier adversary environment than campaign finance or term limits.

## Partnership vs. conflict

**Could we endorse anyone's text?** No, on the evidence. Cohen's text omits our two lead sections and leads with provisions our gate check has held back; Olszewski's mechanism was rejected in our corpus on 2026-08-18. Neither is adoptable without inverting our article's strategic order.

**Could we join a coalition?** With care, and probably not yet.

- **Protect Democracy** is the natural counterpart on substance and the best-resourced org in the field, with real right-of-center relationships (Kori Schake on the board; coalition work with Republicans for the Rule of Law, Stand Up Republic, Niskanen). But their institutional position is that an amendment is not needed. Joining their pardon coalition means joining a *statutory* campaign. The productive relationship is narrower and more honest: their researchers (Tudor, Florence) have already used the "both parties" framing publicly, and they are the obvious external reviewer for our draft, not a co-sponsor of it. Note the concentration risk in leaning on their polling, their framing, and their coalition simultaneously.
- **CLC** conflicts less than it appears: it works the pardon-lobbying and recipient-disclosure territory our memo already assigns to **Inherently statutory**. Their June 2026 recipient-disclosure design is the most developed draft of the "pardon-lobbying disclosure" item in our own list. That is complementary, not competitive, and citing their proposal as the companion legislation to our amendment is a defensible, low-cost use of their work.
- **CREW** would code us. Their pardon analysis is sound and worth citing on the *Garland* constraint; their framing ("lawless era," "countering authoritarianism") is incompatible with our voice rules.
- **Public Citizen** endorses via quotes tied to January 6 framing. Association carries the incumbent-coding risk with no offsetting drafting contribution.

**The relationship worth actually pursuing.** **Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE-2)** is the only sitting Republican on record cosponsoring any pardon amendment, and his stated rationale — "across multiple administrations," "preserves the pardon power," "a narrow, commonsense guardrail" — is our article's own argument in a Republican's voice. He signed onto a mechanism we rejected; our draft avoids every objection Cato raised against that mechanism. That is a specific, checkable pitch. **Sen. Chris McDaniel (R-KY)** is the second such name, with a ballot-tested transparency clause behind him.

**Where reform orgs conflict with each other.** Less than in other articles. No Article V convention organization is active on pardon reform, so the convention-route conflict that splits campaign-finance and term-limits orgs does not arise here. The live split is amendment-versus-statute, and on that question all four national orgs sit on the opposite side from us.
