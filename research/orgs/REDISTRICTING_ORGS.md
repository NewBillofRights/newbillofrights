# Redistricting — Organization Landscape and Language Comparison

*Org-landscape pass for **Article 6 (Redistricting)** of [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md); companion to [REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md](../REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md). Researched 2026-08-19. Every factual claim below was fetched at its source in this session; fetches are recorded in `research/cache/index.jsonl`.*

## Founder direction (2026-08-19, recorded verbatim)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

## The headline finding for this article

**No organization in this space has drafted a federal constitutional amendment on redistricting.** That is the single most important result of this pass, and it distinguishes Article 6 sharply from the campaign-finance and term-limits articles, where American Promise and U.S. Term Limits offer finished, road-tested amendment text to adopt.

Every serious actor here has invested its drafting energy in one of two places:

1. **State constitutional text** — where the investment is deep, the language is operative, and the margins are wide (Colorado 71.37%, Michigan 61.3%, Utah 50.34%). This is the "already-invested language" worth mining, and §4 quotes it verbatim.
2. **Federal statutes** — the Freedom to Vote Act, the Redistricting Reform Act of 2025 (S.2885/H.R.5449), Kiley's H.R.4889. These are Elections Clause bills, not Article V proposals, and two of them contain a mid-decade ban clause that is close kin to our Section 2.

The consequence for the founder's question: there is no org text to adopt wholesale for Article 6. There *is* org text to adopt clause-by-clause, and the strongest candidate is not from an advocacy organization at all — it is §102 of the Padilla–Lofgren bill, which independently arrived at the same state-constitution carve-out our own Section 2 added on 2026-08-17.

## Per-organization detail

### Common Cause

Founded 1970; the c4 (EIN 52-6078441, Washington DC) has been tax-exempt since April 1968 and reported FY2025 revenue $19.6M, expenses $9.98M, total assets $20,243,471, liabilities $2,543,506, net assets $17,699,965. The affiliated c3, Common Cause Education Fund (EIN 31-1705370), tax-exempt since July 2000, reported FY2025 revenue $26.8M, total assets $34,104,797, net assets $32,177,902.

Common Cause is the named respondent in *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) and remains the most litigation-active org in the space. **It has no drafted federal constitutional amendment.** Its stated federal vehicles are the Freedom to Vote Act and the Redistricting Reform Act of 2025 — both statutes.

**The partnership problem is its 2025–26 position.** Its own campaign page states: "We oppose gerrymandering on principle—no matter who does it." But its President and CEO, in a signed statement on the same page, writes: "I'll cut to the chase – Common Cause will not pre‑emptively oppose mid‑decade redistricting in California," and "We won't call for unilateral political disarmament in the face of authoritarianism."

The org then published six **fairness criteria** by which it evaluates mid-decade redraws: (1) Proportionality — "Any mid-decade redistricting should be a targeted response proportional to the threat posed by mid-decade gerrymanders in other states"; (2) Public participation; (3) Racial equity; (4) Federal reform — leaders "must publicly endorse the John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act and the Freedom to Vote Act"; (5) Endorsement of independent redistricting; (6) Time-limited — maps "must expire following the 2030 Census." Applying them, Common Cause found that **California "met six out of six"** while **Texas's and Missouri's plans "fail to meet"** the criteria.

Criterion 1 is a retaliation-permission rule and criterion 4 conditions approval on endorsing two bills that code as one party's platform. Whatever its merits as advocacy strategy, this is the precise asymmetry our Article 6 is designed to eliminate, and it is disqualifying for coalition purposes (§6).

### League of Women Voters

Founded 1920. The most symmetric national position found in this pass. LWVUS CEO Celina Stewart, in an August 6, 2025 statement: "The League of Women Voters does not support a tit-for-tat approach to redistricting. State legislatures redrawing their maps mid-cycle reflects political gamesmanship that erodes public trust in our democracy. Redistricting must be fair, transparent, and rooted in the voices of voters — not partisan retaliation." And: "Voters deserve stable, fair representation — not to be used as pawns in a power grab. When politicians rewrite the rules mid-game to protect their control, democracy loses."

That statement contains no carve-out for retaliation and names no party — it is the only major-org statement located in this pass that would survive our own hard rules unedited. LWV state Leagues are also the lead plaintiffs in the two cases that most matter to our remedial exception: *League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth* (Pa. 2018) and *League of Women Voters of Utah v. Utah State Legislature* (2024). **No drafted federal amendment text.**

### Campaign Legal Center

Founded 2002; 501(c)(3). CLC's own page on citizen-led commissions **contains no proposal for federal constitutional amendment text**, and this appears to be deliberate: its products are design guides — *Designing Independent Redistricting Commissions* (July 2018) and *Independent Redistricting Commissions: Primer and Best Practices* (June 2024) — built on the premise that commissions are "created differently depending on many unique local factors." It cites "At least 60 percent of Democrats, Independents and Republicans support the creation of IRCs" and supports state amendment campaigns in Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and Illinois.

CLC is therefore a **methodology partner, not a text source**. Its commission-design work is squarely in the "inherently statutory" bucket our memo already assigned.

### Princeton Gerrymandering Project / Electoral Innovation Lab

Self-described: "The Gerrymandering Project does **nonpartisan analysis** to understand and eliminate partisan gerrymandering at a state-by-state level," and "We translate math into law, and law into math." It supplies the Redistricting Report Card, the three statistical tests published in the *Stanford Law Review*, and state reports for reformers in Virginia, Michigan, and Ohio. It states its work "is used by legislators and reformers of all communities, without regard to partisan affiliation," and it has publicly criticised a reform bill drawn by one party's lawmakers in New Jersey (December 2018) — the strongest available evidence of genuine methodological symmetry.

**No drafted amendment text**, by design; its stated role is to "help analyze and craft reform language." That makes PGP the natural **verification partner** for Article 6 rather than a source of it — and the natural place to send any anti-favoritism clause we later consider.

### Voters Not Politicians (Michigan) — passed constitutional text

Formed 2016–17 by "a group of political novices"; by 2018 "a grassroots powerhouse of 6500 volunteers" who passed **Michigan Proposal 2 with 61.3%**, creating the Michigan Independent Citizens Redistricting Commission. The org stayed together and is now a general Michigan democracy-reform organization whose current priorities are campaign finance, ethics and transparency, and voting rights — redistricting is no longer its lead program. Its operative constitutional language is quoted in §4.

### Better Boundaries (Utah) — passed constitutional-grade text, then eight years of defense

Founded 2017 by "a bipartisan group of Utahns." **Utah Proposition 4 passed in 2018 with 50.34% (512,218 to 505,274)** — the narrowest win in this file and, because Prop 4 was a *statutory* initiative rather than a constitutional amendment, the one that proves our memo's entrenchment lesson most painfully.

The subsequent timeline is the single best case study available on durability: 2020, the legislature passes SB200 stripping the commission to advisory only; 2022, LWV of Utah and Mormon Women for Ethical Government sue; **July 11, 2024**, the Utah Supreme Court rules unanimously that voter-approved initiatives are constitutionally protected; August 2024, the legislature refers Amendment D, which would have let it repeal any initiative by simple majority; October 2024, Amendment D is struck from the ballot as misleading; **August 2025**, the Third District Court reinstates Prop 4 and orders new maps; **November 2025**, the court finds the legislature's Map C "does not comply with Prop 4 and displayed substantial bias" and adopts the plaintiffs' map for 2026; **April 30, 2026**, a repeal initiative fails to qualify after missing the signature minimum in five senate districts.

Eight years of litigation to defend a measure that won at the ballot. That is the argument for Article V rather than state-by-state reform, stated as a fact pattern rather than a prediction.

### Fair Maps Colorado / Amendments Y & Z — the ceiling case

**Colorado Amendment Y passed with 71.37% (1,711,008 to 686,260)**, referred to the ballot by the legislature. Its constitutional text is the most complete anti-gerrymandering language ever ratified by an American electorate at that margin, and §4 quotes the operative clauses.

### Citizens Not Politicians (Ohio) — the instructive failure

Ohio Issue 1 (November 2024) would have repealed Articles XI and XIX and added Article XX, creating a 15-member Ohio Citizens Redistricting Commission. **It failed 53.71% to 46.29% (2,937,489 to 2,531,900).**

Our memo attributes the loss to hostile ballot summary language, which is well documented. This pass surfaces a second cause the memo does not record, and it bears directly on our own drafting. The Ohio Supreme Court, in the ballot-language case brought by the measure's own sponsors, described the amendment this way: apart from contiguity and federal compliance, "the amendment makes only one other criterion paramount: districts must be drawn to elect Republicans and Democrats in proportion to their statewide vote totals." The operative clause required that "the statewide proportion of districts in each redistricting plan that favors each political party *shall* correspond closely to the statewide partisan preferences of the voters of Ohio," with "correspond closely" defined as deviation "by no more than" 3 percent. Population equality, minority-opportunity protection, and communities of interest were required only "to the extent possible," subordinate to that proportionality mandate.

**Lesson for Article 6:** a reform that mandates a partisan outcome formula gives its opponents a true sentence — "this requires districts drawn to hit a partisan target" — with which to describe it as gerrymandering. Our Sections 1–3 mandate no outcome and no formula; they regulate a calendar and name a lodestar. That difference is why Ohio's failure is not evidence against our design, and it is a reason to keep resisting the anti-favoritism floor.

### Fair Districts PA

Founded 2016 as a coalition of the League of Women Voters of PA, Common Cause PA, the Committee of Seventy, and the Pennsylvania Council of Churches. Supports a PA constitutional amendment establishing an 11-member Independent Citizens' Redistricting Commission (HB 31 / SB 131 in the current session). Because Pennsylvania requires passage in two consecutive sessions followed by referendum, **no text has yet been ratified**; the org's stated goal is passage before 2031. Its own materials cite CLC's commission-design reports as its technical basis.

Fair Districts PA is a **coalition-structure precedent** — a religious body, a business-civic body, and two national reform orgs in one durable coalition — more than a text source.

## The passed-text corpus, quoted verbatim

These are the operative anti-gerrymandering clauses that American electorates have actually ratified. Michigan's and Colorado's are constitutional; Utah's is statutory-initiative text later held constitutionally protected against repeal.

### Colorado — Const. Art. V §44.3(4) (Amendment Y, 2018, 71.37%)

> **NO MAP MAY BE APPROVED BY THE COMMISSION OR GIVEN EFFECT BY THE SUPREME COURT IF IT:**
> **(a) HAS BEEN DRAWN FOR THE PURPOSE OF PROTECTING ONE OR MORE INCUMBENT MEMBERS, OR ONE OR MORE DECLARED CANDIDATES, OF THE UNITED STATES HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES, OR ANY POLITICAL PARTY; OR**
> **(b) HAS BEEN DRAWN FOR THE PURPOSE OF OR RESULTS IN THE DENIAL OR ABRIDGEMENT OF THE RIGHT OF ANY CITIZEN TO VOTE ON ACCOUNT OF THAT PERSON'S RACE OR MEMBERSHIP IN A LANGUAGE MINORITY GROUP, INCLUDING BY DILUTING THE IMPACT OF THAT RACIAL OR LANGUAGE MINORITY GROUP'S ELECTORAL INFLUENCE.**

Declaration clause, Art. V §44(1)(a):

> **THE PRACTICE OF POLITICAL GERRYMANDERING, WHEREBY CONGRESSIONAL DISTRICTS ARE PURPOSEFULLY DRAWN TO FAVOR ONE POLITICAL PARTY OR INCUMBENT POLITICIAN OVER ANOTHER, MUST END.**

Definitional clause, Art. V §44(3)(b)(IV):

> **"COMMUNITY OF INTEREST" DOES NOT INCLUDE RELATIONSHIPS WITH POLITICAL PARTIES, INCUMBENTS, OR POLITICAL CANDIDATES.**

*(Colorado also mandates competitiveness — §44.3(3)(a): "THE COMMISSION SHALL, TO THE EXTENT POSSIBLE, MAXIMIZE THE NUMBER OF POLITICALLY COMPETITIVE DISTRICTS," with "COMPETITIVE" defined as "HAVING A REASONABLE POTENTIAL FOR THE PARTY AFFILIATION OF THE DISTRICT'S REPRESENTATIVE TO CHANGE AT LEAST ONCE BETWEEN FEDERAL DECENNIAL CENSUSES." This is an outcome-shaping mandate of the kind that sank Ohio Issue 1 and is not recommended for federal text.)*

### Michigan — Const. Art. IV §6(13) (Proposal 2, 2018, 61.3%)

Criteria in the constitution's own priority order:

> (a) "Districts shall be of equal population as mandated by the United States constitution, and shall comply with the voting rights act and other federal laws."
> (b) "Districts shall be geographically contiguous."
> (c) "Districts shall reflect the state's diverse population and communities of interest."
> (d) **"Districts shall not provide a disproportionate advantage to any political party. A disproportionate advantage to a political party shall be determined using accepted measures of partisan fairness."**
> (e) **"Districts shall not favor or disfavor an incumbent elected official or a candidate."**
> (f) "Districts shall reflect consideration of county, city, and township boundaries."
> (g) "Districts shall be reasonably compact."

### Utah — Proposition 4 (2018, 50.34%), redistricting standards §(3)–(5)

> (3) **"The Legislature and the Commission may not divide districts in a manner that purposefully or unduly favors or disfavors any incumbent elected official, candidate or prospective candidate for elective office, or any political party."**
> (4) "The Legislature and the Commission shall use judicial standards and the best available data and scientific and statistical methods, including measures of partisan symmetry, to assess whether a proposed redistricting plan abides by and conforms to the redistricting standards contained in this Section, including the restrictions contained in Subsection (3)."
> (5) "Partisan political data and information, such as partisan election results, voting records, political party affiliation information, and residential addresses of incumbent elected officials and candidates or prospective candidates for elective office, may not be considered by the Legislature or by the Commission, except as permitted under Subsection (4)."

### Ohio — Issue 1 (2024), §6(B) — **FAILED, 46.29%**

> "the statewide proportion of districts in each redistricting plan that favors each political party shall correspond closely to the statewide partisan preferences of the voters of Ohio"

with "to correspond closely" defined to mean the proportion "may deviate by no more than" 3 percent unless arithmetically impossible.

### The two federal statutory mid-decade bans

**S.2885 §102(a)** (Redistricting Reform Act of 2025, Padilla; House companion H.R.5449, Lofgren):

> "A State that has been redistricted in accordance with this Act and a State described in section 101(c) or section 101(d) may not be redistricted again until after the next apportionment of Representatives under section 22(a) of the Act entitled 'An Act to provide for the fifteenth and subsequent decennial censuses and to provide for an apportionment of Representatives in Congress', approved June 18, 1929 (2 U.S.C. 2a), unless a court requires the State to conduct such subsequent redistricting to comply with the Constitution of the United States, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (52 U.S.C. 10301 et seq.), the Constitution of the State, or the terms or conditions of this Act."

**H.R.4889 §2** (Kiley; introduced as R-CA, Aug. 5, 2025; a motion to discharge was filed May 12, 2026, Petition No. 119-21), amending 2 U.S.C. 2c:

> "A State which has been redistricted in the manner provided by law after an apportionment under section 22(a) of the Act entitled 'An Act to provide for the fifteenth and subsequent decennial censuses and to provide for an apportionment of Representatives in Congress', approved June 18, 1929 (2 U.S.C. 2a), may not be redistricted again until after the next apportionment of Representatives under such section, unless a court requires the State to conduct such subsequent redistricting to comply with the Constitution or to enforce the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (42 U.S.C. 1973 et seq.)."

H.R.4889 §4 makes the ban apply "with respect to any Congressional redistricting which occurs after the November 2024 election" — retroactive to the entire 2025–26 wave — and §3 disclaims any effect on state and local districts.

## Language comparison against our draft

Our Article 6 has three sections: a voter-primacy declaration (§1), the once-per-census rule with a remedial exception (§2), and a prospective effective date keyed to the 2030 enumeration (§3).

### What their language covers that ours does not

- **An anti-favoritism standard.** Colorado, Michigan, and Utah all forbid drawing maps to favor a party or incumbent. Our draft names the principle in §1 but creates no operative prohibition. This is the deliberate held-back layer in our memo, and the state corpus is the strongest argument for revisiting it: an "intent to favor" standard has now been ratified four times (Florida 2010, Colorado, Michigan, Utah) and *Rucho*'s own majority conceded such text "provide[s] standards and guidance for state courts to apply."
- **Anti-evasion definitions.** Colorado's "COMMUNITY OF INTEREST DOES NOT INCLUDE RELATIONSHIPS WITH POLITICAL PARTIES, INCUMBENTS, OR POLITICAL CANDIDATES" closes a loophole our text does not address, because our text sets no criteria at all.
- **Evidentiary rules.** Utah §(4)–(5) tells the map-drawer what data it may and may not consider. Inherently statutory by our own classification.
- **State and local districts.** H.R.4889 §3 expressly disclaims them; our §2 expressly *includes* state legislative districts.
- **Retroactive reach.** H.R.4889 voids the 2025–26 wave. Our §3 deliberately does not.

### What ours covers that theirs does not

- **Durability against a simple majority.** Every text in §4 is repealable or evadable by the body it binds — SB200 (Utah, 2020), Amendment 3 (Missouri, 2020), the Ohio Ballot Board's summary, the $4.365M Utah repeal drive of 2026. Only Article V's two-thirds/three-fourths threshold escapes that, which is the whole reason this article exists.
- **Nationwide simultaneity.** Every state text disarms one state unilaterally — the failure mode California's Prop 50 demonstrated at a 29-point margin.
- **A mandate, not merely a cap.** Our §2 requires that districts "shall be established once following each decennial enumeration," which forecloses the India-style boundary freeze. Both federal bills are phrased purely as prohibitions ("may not be redistricted again") and neither compels the decennial redraw.
- **An interpretive lodestar.** No org text contains anything like our §1.
- **Effect-denial.** Our §3's "no such district … shall be given effect" gives the rule a self-executing consequence. Colorado has a close analogue in §44.3(4) ("OR GIVEN EFFECT BY THE SUPREME COURT"); the federal bills have none.

### If we adopted their language 100%

Because no org offers federal amendment text, this table is stated for the **closest single clause** — S.2885 §102(a), the Padilla–Lofgren mid-decade ban — substituted for our §2.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Gain** | A state-constitution carve-out independently drafted to the same conclusion we reached on 2026-08-17 — strong corroboration that the fix is right. An explicit tie to the 1929 apportionment statute, which makes the trigger date mechanically unambiguous. A live bill with a sitting senator and representative attached, and a discharge petition on its House cousin. |
| **Lose** | The affirmative decennial mandate (§102 is a cap, so the India trap reopens). Coverage of state legislative districts. The self-executing effect-denial. Our prospective 2030 effective date, replaced by the bill's own act-relative triggers. |
| **Need to add back** | "shall be established once following each decennial enumeration" as a mandate; "and for the election of members of any State legislature"; the "no such district … shall be given effect" consequence; the 2030 effective date; and a rewrite of the VRA citation into our generic "laws of the United States" formulation, because §102 names the VRA by code section and *Callais* has already moved that ground once. |

**Net:** adopting §102 wholesale would cost more than it gains. Adopting its *exception list* — which is what our §2 already independently says — is confirmation, not a change. **Recommendation: no text change to Article 6 from this pass.**

## Adversaries

- **Utahns for Representative Government** — the Utah Republican Party's political issues committee, per the party's own post. It backed the 2026 initiative to eliminate Utah's independent commission, raising $4,365,000 and spending $4,363,206.90 against Protect Utah Voters' $442,414.09 — roughly a 10:1 advantage. **It still failed to qualify for the ballot**, missing the signature minimum in five senate districts after voters withdrew signatures. Reporting by Utah News Dispatch (Jan. 5, 2026) traces more than $4.3M of that money to a nonprofit, Securing American Greatness. Two lessons: organized, well-funded repeal efforts are now a standing feature of this space; and money alone did not carry it.
- **Incumbent-controlled ballot machinery** is the more consistent adversary than any single org — the Ohio Ballot Board's 2024 summary and the Missouri legislature's 2020 ballot title (rewritten by a judge for concealing that its "central purpose … is to effectively repeal Amendment 1") are the documented cases in our memo. An Article V route bypasses state ballot boards entirely, which is a genuine structural advantage worth stating on the page.
- **No national organization opposes a mid-decade ban as such.** The opposition is state-level, ad hoc, and organized around defending a specific map — which is consistent with the 70/71-point Marquette finding and is a meaningful asset for this article.

## Partnership and conflict

**Endorsable now:** none, because there is no text to endorse.

**Coalition-safe:** the **League of Women Voters** is the only national org whose stated position survives our hard rules unedited — no retaliation carve-out, no party named. Its state Leagues are also the plaintiffs whose victories (*LWV v. Commonwealth*, Pa. 2018; *LWV of Utah*, 2024) define the remedial exception our §2 must preserve. **Better Boundaries** is cross-partisan by origin and holds the best-documented durability case study in the country. Both are approachable without coding us.

**Technical partner:** the **Princeton Gerrymandering Project** is the right destination for any future anti-favoritism clause — it is explicitly nonpartisan, it has criticised reform drafted by both parties, and its stated function is to "help analyze and craft reform language." Engaging it costs nothing and buys methodological cover.

**Association risk — Common Cause.** Its stated criteria permit "proportional" retaliation and condition approval on endorsing two named federal bills. Its CEO has said the org "will not pre-emptively oppose mid-decade redistricting in California" while the org sued over Texas's, Missouri's, and Florida's maps. Under our own rule that abuses be paired in the same sentence, a coalition with Common Cause on *this* article would import an asymmetry we exist to remove. This is a judgment about one position on one issue in one cycle, not about the organization's record, and it does not extend to other articles where Common Cause may be a natural ally.

**Conflict among reform orgs:** the visible fault line in 2025–26 is not Article V versus statute — it is whether counter-gerrymandering is legitimate. LWV says no without qualification; Common Cause says yes under conditions. Our Article 6 takes LWV's side by construction, since a calendar rule cannot ask who struck first. That is a feature to state plainly and a reason the two orgs cannot both be lead partners here.

**The Article V question** did not surface as a live conflict in this pass: no redistricting organization located here campaigns for or against a convention, because none of them is working the amendment route at all. That is an open field rather than a contested one.
