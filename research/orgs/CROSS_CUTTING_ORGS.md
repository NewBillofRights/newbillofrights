*Written 2026-08-19 by the org-landscape research pass (cross-cutting agent). Scope: multi-issue democracy-reform organizations, the Article V ecosystem on both sides, and a primary-source verification of the ⚖️ lobbying-definition flags in [COFOUNDER_SEARCH.md](../COFOUNDER_SEARCH.md). Unlike the sibling memos in this directory, this one is **not** about adopting anyone's draft language for one of our nine articles — most orgs here have no amendment text at all. Its output is a partner map, a route-conflict map, and a legal answer.*

## Founder direction (recorded verbatim, 2026-08-19)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

**How that direction lands on this memo.** It mostly doesn't, and that is the finding. Of the sixteen organizations surveyed here, **four have drafted constitutional amendment text** (Keep Nine, U.S. Term Limits, renewdemocracy.org, and — as the reference point — American Promise, covered in the campaign-finance memo). The large multi-issue reform orgs that dominate the field's budget and staff — Issue One, Unite America, RepresentUs, Protect Democracy, the Institute for Responsive Government, Leadership Now, Renew Democracy Initiative, Future Caucus — have **none**. They are statutory and litigation shops. There is nothing here to adopt for our nine articles; what there is here is a coalition map and one legal answer.

## 2. The multi-issue reform orgs — what they actually are

### The pattern that matters

The eight large multi-issue orgs share a structural fact that reframes how this project should think about them: **they are not amendment organizations.** Every one of them works the statutory, litigation, administrative, or norm-building lane. This is not an oversight on their part; it is a strategic choice, and in at least one case an explicit one.

- **RepresentUs** is the sharpest illustration. Its flagship product, the **American Anti-Corruption Act**, is a **model statute** — drafted with Trevor Potter, former FEC chairman — covering lobbying limits, campaign finance, and ethics enforcement. It has no Article V program and no amendment text. Five of our nine categories overlap its issue set, and its instrument in all five is legislation.
- **Leadership Now Project** routes *around* constitutional change deliberately. On term limits — our Target 2 — it advances a **voluntary retirement norm at age 75** and **committee-chair caps**, i.e. party-rule and norm mechanisms, precisely the alternatives to amendment. Its other visible work is rule-of-law amicus filings.
- **Renew Democracy Initiative** (Garry Kasparov, chair) touches **zero** of our nine. Its focus is foreign-policy and anti-authoritarian advocacy. It is on this list only because it is frequently named in the same breath as the others, and the finding is that it is not a fit.
- **Future Caucus** (legal name **Millennial Action Project**) is the field's **strongest balance model** and worth studying on method rather than substance: mandatory **D/R co-chair pairs** and generational eligibility rules, structurally enforced rather than aspirational. That is a governance design directly relevant to our host-committee and founding-board question. Its issue overlap is thin (AI-policy privacy; criminal justice, statutory only).
- **Protect Democracy** runs the standard dual structure — **Protect Democracy Project** (c3) and **Protect Democracy United** (c4) — describes its staff as including "conservatives, moderates and progressives," and operates 100+ staff across 20+ states through litigation, research, and technology (VoteShield, OpenOMB, the Civil Service Archive). Its overlap with us is executive compliance, civil service, and DOJ independence — three of our categories or candidates — but again through courts and statutes.
- **Issue One** (founded 2013) and **Unite America** (founded 2014 as the Centrist Project under Charles Wheelan; pivoted 2019) are the field's institutional anchors. Unite America is the largest single budget in this survey at **$24.0M** (c4, 2024) plus **$20.6M** (c3), and runs a hybrid/Carey PAC — a three-entity structure. Issue One's **ReFormers Caucus** of former members of Congress is the most directly reusable asset in the field for our salon and host-committee plans.
- **Institute for Responsive Government** is the newest (IRS ruling **April 2024**; first filing shows **$2.0M** revenue against only **$148K** expenses — a startup year, not a steady state). Election administration is its lane; the overlap with our nine is minimal.

### Why this is good news, not bad

None of these orgs is a competitor for the amendment lane, and none has language we would adopt. What they have is **people, convening power, and cross-partisan credibility** — which is exactly what COFOUNDER_SEARCH.md asks of them. The absence of amendment programs also means partnering with them carries **low route risk**: they are not committed to a convention strategy we would have to disown.

**One caveat, flagged as unverified-absence.** We found **no public Article V position** for Issue One, Unite America, RepresentUs, Leadership Now, RDI, Future Caucus, Protect Democracy, or the Institute for Responsive Government. That is an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence — none of these orgs appears on the Common Cause coalition letter, but that letter's signer list was last updated in **March 2019** and is not a current census. Any of them could hold an unpublished position. This should be asked directly before any coalition commitment.

## 3. The Article V route conflict — the central strategic finding

This is the part of the memo with operational consequences. **The democracy-reform field is split by the route, not the goal**, and the split does not run left/right. It runs convention/no-convention, and it puts several natural partners on opposite sides.

### 3a. The pro-convention campaigns

**Convention of States Action** (c4, Houston; parent **Citizens for Self-Governance**, c3 27-1657203, $3.28M FY2023) runs the largest application campaign. Its model application is a **single, fixed text that each state must pass identically**; the site is explicit that "Each state must pass this exact application to aggregate towards the 34 states necessary to call a Convention." The operative section, verbatim:

> "**Section 1.** The legislature of the State of ______ hereby applies to Congress, under the provisions of Article V of the Constitution of the United States, for the calling of a convention of the states limited to proposing amendments to the Constitution of the United States that impose fiscal restraints on the federal government, limit the power and jurisdiction of the federal government, and limit the terms of office for its officials and for members of Congress."

Note the three subjects: **fiscal restraints, limiting federal power and jurisdiction, and term limits**. Only the third touches our nine, and it arrives bundled with two that are far outside our full-spectrum test. Section 3 makes the application **continuing** — it does not lapse. Common Cause's tracking memo counts the COS resolution as passed in **19 states**: Georgia, Alaska, Florida, Alabama, Tennessee, Indiana, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Arizona, North Dakota, Texas, Missouri, Arkansas, Utah, Mississippi, West Virginia, Nebraska, South Carolina, and Wisconsin. *(Advocate-adjacent count from an opponent's memo, 2023 update — verify independently before publishing.)*

COS's own framing is not neutral ground for us. Its site describes opponents as "George Soros, 230 statist organizations, and a few radical fringe groups" and its endorsers as Mark Levin, Greg Abbott, Marco Rubio, and Tom Coburn. **Association would code the project immediately.**

**U.S. Term Limits** runs a **single-subject** Term Limits Convention application — the one Article V campaign whose subject matter maps cleanly onto one of our targets. USTL claims **fourteen states**: Florida, Alabama, Missouri, West Virginia, Oklahoma, Wisconsin, Tennessee, Louisiana, North Carolina, South Dakota, Indiana, South Carolina, Kansas, and Arizona — consistent with the count already in [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md). Its entity pair is c4 **U.S. Term Limits** ($7.49M FY2023) plus c3 **U.S. Term Limits Foundation** ($1.03M). Common Cause's memo identifies USTL as "founded and funded by conservative mega-donor Howard Rich" — a right-coded funding origin that matters for our balance test even though congressional term limits themselves poll with the smallest partisan gap of anything we have measured (90% R / 86% D, Pew 2023).

**Wolf Pack** — formerly **Wolf-PAC**, now operating as "Wolf Pack" at **fightcorruption.us** (the wolf-pac.com domain redirects) — is the left-origin convention campaign, founded by Cenk Uygur. Its position is the most interesting for us because **it explicitly declines to draft text**, treating the amendment's content as something the movement decides later: its plan page argues you build power "not just to get the amendment introduced, but to ensure it is the strongest amendment possible." Its financial scale has collapsed — the last Form 990 with data (FY2021) shows **$24,729** in revenue against $66,569 in expenses. Common Cause counts its resolution as passed in five states but **rescinded in two**, leaving **Vermont, California, and Rhode Island** — matching the rescission history already recorded in [CAMPAIGN_FINANCE_RESEARCH.md](../CAMPAIGN_FINANCE_RESEARCH.md). Notably, Wolf Pack's "United Against Corruption" page lists **American Promise** and **RepresentUs** as allies, so the ally graph crosses the route line even where positions don't.

### 3b. The opposition

**Common Cause** is the organizing hub of the anti-convention coalition, joined by the **Center for Media and Democracy** as its principal researcher (CMD publishes at exposedbycmd.org and has surveyed all fifty states' convention-delegate laws). The coalition statement — "Constitutional Rights and Public Interest Groups Oppose Calls for an Article V Constitutional Convention," released April 2017, signer list updated March 2019 — carries roughly 230 signatures including the **Brennan Center for Justice, Campaign Legal Center, League of Women Voters of the United States, Democracy 21, CREW, NAACP, AFL-CIO, AFSCME, SEIU, NEA, and EMILY's List**. Its core argument:

> "There are no rules and guidelines in the U.S. Constitution on how a convention would work, which creates an opportunity for a runaway convention that could rewrite any constitutional right or protection currently available to American citizens."

The statement marshals cross-ideological authority deliberately — Chief Justice Warren Burger ("there is no way to effectively limit or muzzle the actions of a constitutional convention"), Justice Antonin Scalia ("I certainly would not want a constitutional convention. Whoa! Who knows what would come out of it?"), Laurence Tribe, and Georgetown's David Super. **That Burger and Scalia anchor the anti-convention case is the single most useful fact in this section for our purposes**: the runaway-convention fear is not a left-coded position, which is why the convention route cannot be made full-spectrum by better messaging.

### 3c. The formulation that resolves the conflict for us

Common Cause states the distinction we need, in its own words:

> "Common Cause fully supports overturning *Citizens United* and other cases that have bolstered the undue influence of money in politics. We support a constitutional amendment as one path – but oppose an Article V convention as the mechanism, for the reasons that are discussed later in this memo."

**This is the sentence that makes a coalition possible.** The field's split is over *mechanism*, not over amending the Constitution. An organization that pursues the **congressional-proposal route under Article V** — two-thirds of both houses, then three-fourths of the states, the route by which all 27 existing amendments were proposed — is on the *same side* as Common Cause, the Brennan Center, and the League of Women Voters, while still being an amendment organization.

This is already our route. The repo's existing research reaches the same conclusion from the evidence side: [CAMPAIGN_FINANCE_RESEARCH.md](../CAMPAIGN_FINANCE_RESEARCH.md) records that memorials urging Congress to propose an amendment (the American Promise model, 25 states) "travel; convention applications do not, and can be clawed back."

**Recommendation.** State the congressional-proposal route explicitly and publicly, and never sign a convention application. This costs us nothing we want — no convention campaign carries language we would adopt, since COS and Wolf Pack have no amendment text at all — and it keeps roughly 230 organizations, including most of the reform field's institutional weight, available as partners. The one real cost is **U.S. Term Limits**, whose drafted term-limits language the founder direction specifically names for adoption. That cost is manageable: **adopting an organization's amendment text is not the same as joining its convention campaign**, and the two decisions should be made separately. We can take USTL's language on the merits (per the term-limits memo) while pursuing the congressional route, and say so plainly.

## 4. The orgs with actual amendment text

Three orgs outside the sibling memos have drafted text. None is adoptable for our nine, but each matters for a different reason.

### 4a. Keep Nine — relevant to our rejected court-expansion category

**Two different texts circulate under this name, and the difference is not cosmetic.** The coalition's own site states:

> "The Supreme Court of the United States shall be composed of nine Justices."

But the version actually reported out of committee — **H.J.Res.1, 119th Congress**, sponsored by Rep. Andy Biggs [R-AZ-5], reported to the House Calendar July 6, 2026 (H. Rept. 119-732) — reads:

> "The Supreme Court of the United States shall be composed of nine justices consisting of one chief justice and eight associate justices."

The enacted-text version specifies the composition; the site version does not. Anyone quoting "the Keep Nine amendment" should use the joint-resolution wording. H.J.Res.1 also carries a **seven-year ratification deadline** in its resolving clause.

**The partisan finding is decisive and confirms the existing rejection.** As reported, H.J.Res.1 had exactly **three sponsors — Biggs, Allen, and Schmidt, all Republicans**. The site's supporter list is the **Republican National Committee, FreedomWorks, Eagle Forum, and the National Federation of Republican Women** — no Democratic organizations. Its only cross-party support is quotations from Justices Ginsburg and Breyer favoring nine justices, which is not organizational endorsement. **No nonprofit entity for "Keep Nine" appears in IRS data**, and the site is a free Google Sites page with an Anedot donation link — this is a coalition brand, not a funded organization.

This confirms [COURT_EXPANSION_REJECTED.md](../rejected/COURT_EXPANSION_REJECTED.md)'s analysis without amendment. Bearing on our platform: **none**.

### 4b. renewdemocracy.org — the warning flag, resolved

Confirmed: **renewdemocracy.org is a different organization from the Renew Democracy Initiative (rdi.org, Garry Kasparov)**, and they should never be conflated. The former does have drafted amendment text — "The Renew Democracy Amendment Voter Bill of Rights," six provisions plus a preamble — covering campaign contributions restricted to natural-person citizens (¶1), direct popular election (¶2), contribution caps pegged to "an amount reasonably affordable by the average citizen" (¶3), regulation of outside spending as to "volume, timing, and coordination" but explicitly **not** content (¶4), lobbying regulation including former officeholders (¶5), and a corporate-personhood provision making organizational speech rights "secondary and subservient" to individual rights (¶6).

**It is not adoptable, and the reason is entity, not language.** The org states its "nonprofit status is applied for" and that donations "are not tax deductible" — **there is no verified legal entity**. No leadership is named anywhere on the site, no founding date, no location, no funders. The site carries a `noindex, nofollow` robots directive and its banner asset dates to 2018. Provision ¶2's direct-popular-election clause would also import our **rejected** Electoral College category, and ¶3's "reasonably affordable by the average citizen" standard is undefined in a way that would not survive drafting review.

**Disposition: note and set aside.** The flag from the earlier run is resolved — the org is real but unincorporated and unattributed. Do not cite it as a peer organization.

### 4c. National Popular Vote and FairVote — both statutory, both worth knowing

Neither belongs to the amendment lane, and both were listed for a reason that the research resolves cleanly.

**National Popular Vote Inc.** (c4, EIN 20-4329338, Lafayette CA, $2.24M FY2023) pursues an **interstate compact — ordinary state legislation, not an amendment**. Its mechanism: enacting states award their electors to "the candidate receiving the most popular votes in all 50 states and DC," triggered only when enacting states total 270 electoral votes. It reports **19 jurisdictions and 222 electoral votes**, consistent with the figure in [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) (18 states plus DC, 222 EVs as of April 2026). Because this route deliberately avoids Article V, NPV is neither partner nor rival — and the underlying category is already rejected (46% R / 80% D, Pew 2024).

**FairVote** (c3 EIN 54-1635649, $7.67M FY2023; **FairVote Action Fund** c4 EIN 38-3650370, $1.13M; Silver Spring MD; IRS ruling 1992) is the relevant org for our **proportional-representation candidate**, and the finding confirms that memo's blocking problem. FairVote's vehicle is the **Fair Representation Act — a statute**, not an amendment. This is exactly what [PROPORTIONAL_REPRESENTATION_CANDIDATE.md](../candidates/PROPORTIONAL_REPRESENTATION_CANDIDATE.md) identified as the category's fatal §5.3 weakness: 2 U.S.C. §2c is a statute, so the why-an-amendment test has no answer. **The largest, best-funded organization in the space agrees, by revealed preference.** FairVote is a useful research source (its *Monopoly Politics* projections are already cited in our fair-districting memo) and a plausible salon participant, but there is no amendment text to adopt because FairVote does not believe one is needed.

## 5. Verification — does advocating an amendment count as lobbying? ⚖️ RESOLVED

This section answers the ⚖️ flags in [COFOUNDER_SEARCH.md](../COFOUNDER_SEARCH.md) §"Structural note." **The memo's claim is correct.** Below is the primary-source chain.

### 5a. The answer: yes, explicitly, by name

**Treas. Reg. §56.4911-2(d)(1)(i)** defines the term for §4911 purposes, verbatim:

> "**Legislation** —(i) *In general.* 'Legislation' includes action by the Congress, any state legislature, any local council, or similar legislative body, or by the public in a referendum, ballot initiative, constitutional amendment, or similar procedure."

"Constitutional amendment" is named in the regulation itself. The IRS restates it in plain language in its published guidance on lobbying, which describes the covered scope as:

> "action by Congress, any state legislature, any local council, or similar governing body, with respect to acts, bills, resolutions, or similar items (such as legislative confirmation of appointive office), or by the public in referendum, ballot initiative, constitutional amendment, or similar procedure."

And the operative test for whether an organization is lobbying:

> "An organization will be regarded as attempting to influence legislation if it contacts, or urges the public to contact, members or employees of a legislative body for the purpose of proposing, supporting, or opposing legislation, or if the organization advocates the adoption or rejection of legislation."

**So: urging Congress to propose an amendment, and urging state legislatures to ratify one, are both lobbying.** Both halves of the Article V congressional route are covered — the first as ordinary direct lobbying of Congress, the second as direct lobbying of state legislatures.

Two refinements worth having on hand:

- **When an amendment becomes "specific legislation."** Under **§56.4911-2(d)(1)(ii)**, "In the case of a referendum, ballot initiative, constitutional amendment, or other measure that is placed on the ballot by petitions signed by a required number or percentage of voters, an item becomes 'specific legislation' when the petition is first circulated among voters for signature." For an Article V amendment proposed by Congress this is not the operative trigger — a joint resolution is legislation from introduction — but it matters for any state ballot-measure work.
- **The public becomes the legislature for ballot measures.** **§56.4911-2(b)(1)(iii)**: "where a communication refers to and reflects a view on a measure that is the subject of a referendum, ballot initiative or similar procedure, the general public in the State or locality where the vote will take place constitutes the legislative body, and individual members of the general public are, for purposes of this paragraph (b)(1), legislators." The practical effect is counterintuitive and favorable: for ballot measures, public communication counts as **direct** lobbying rather than grass roots lobbying — and the direct-lobbying cap is four times larger.

### 5b. The exception that covers our current phase

**§56.4911-2(c)(1)** excludes "nonpartisan analysis, study, or research" from both lobbying categories, an exception mirrored in **26 U.S.C. §4911(d)(2)**, which excludes "making available the results of nonpartisan analysis, study, or research" and technical assistance provided to a government body in response to a **written** request.

COFOUNDER_SEARCH.md's assessment that "research, publishing, polling, salons, civic education" are "comfortably c3" is **supported** — with one condition worth stating plainly, from **§56.4911-2(c)(1)(iii)**: if an organization "times or channels" part of a series of publications "in a manner designed to influence the general public or the action of a legislative body with respect to a specific legislative proposal," those expenses **become** lobbying expenditures. The exception protects genuine research published on its own schedule; it does not protect research timed to a vote. Publishing what fails — already this project's method — is strong evidence of the nonpartisan character the exception requires.

### 5c. The two measurement regimes, and why the 501(h) election is the right call

**The default — the "substantial part" test.** §501(c)(3) conditions exemption on "no substantial part of the activities of which is carrying on propaganda, or otherwise attempting, to influence legislation." The IRS measures this on "all the pertinent facts and circumstances," weighing "the time devoted (by both compensated and volunteer workers) and the expenditures devoted by the organization to the activity." **There is no numerical safe harbor** — and volunteer time counts, which is a particular hazard for a movement organization. Exceeding it means the org "may lose its tax-exempt status, resulting in all of its income being subject to tax," plus a **§4912 excise tax of 5 percent** of lobbying expenditures, and a separate 5 percent tax on organization managers who knowingly agreed to the expenditures.

**The election — §501(h).** An organization may elect (on **Form 5768**) to be measured by expenditures instead. Under **§4911(c)(2)** the lobbying nontaxable amount is:

| Exempt purpose expenditures | Lobbying nontaxable amount |
| --- | --- |
| Not over $500,000 | 20% of exempt purpose expenditures |
| $500,000–$1,000,000 | $100,000, plus 15% of the excess over $500,000 |
| $1,000,000–$1,500,000 | $175,000, plus 10% of the excess over $1,000,000 |
| Over $1,500,000 | $225,000, plus 5% of the excess over $1,500,000 |

capped at **$1,000,000**. The **grass roots** nontaxable amount is **25 percent** of that figure — so grass roots lobbying (influencing legislation by affecting public opinion) is capped four times tighter than direct lobbying (communicating with legislators). Exceeding a cap in a year triggers a **25 percent excise tax on the excess** under §4911(a); exemption is lost only if the organization "normally" exceeds **150 percent** of the ceiling — the lobbying ceiling amount and grass roots ceiling amount under **§501(h)(2)** — measured on a four-year average.

**Why elect.** For an organization whose entire purpose is amendment advocacy, the election converts an unbounded facts-and-circumstances judgment into arithmetic, stops volunteer hours from counting against the limit, and — critically — replaces "you may lose exemption" with "you pay 25% on the excess." **Recommendation: make the §501(h) election on Form 5768 at formation**, subject to counsel's confirmation.
