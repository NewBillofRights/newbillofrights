## Founder direction (recorded verbatim, 2026-08-19)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

## The headline finding: there is no org to adopt from

**No organization in the United States has drafted constitutional amendment text making judicial judgments binding on the executive.** That is not a gap in this search; it is the finding, and it is the sharpest negative result of the nine-article org pass. For Campaign Finance the founder can weigh American Promise's ratified language against ours. For Term Limits he can weigh U.S. Term Limits'. For Article 7 there is nothing on the table — not from the right, not from the left, not from the cross-ideological drafting projects, not from Congress.

Four independent lines of evidence establish it:

1. **The cross-ideological drafting project that exists produced five amendments, and none is this one.** The National Constitution Center's Constitution Drafting Project convened libertarian, progressive, and conservative teams who then reconvened as a virtual convention on August 29 and 31, 2022 and agreed on five amendments: presidential eligibility (XXVIII), legislative veto (XXIX), impeachment (XXX), appointments and eighteen-year Supreme Court terms (XXXI), and Article V reform (XXXII) ([full text](https://constitutioncenter.org/media/files/The_Proposed_Amendments_AMENDMENTS.pdf)). This is the closest analogue in American public life to what the foundation is doing — scholars across the spectrum drafting real text and finding consensus — and executive compliance with courts did not make their list.
2. **All three team constitutions left the gap exactly where 1787 left it.** Each team was invited to rewrite the Constitution from scratch. All three retained the Supremacy Clause in a form binding "the judges in every State" and no one else, and all three retained an oath clause binding officers to "support this Constitution" rather than to obey judgments. Detail in the near-miss section below.
3. **The pro-compliance advocacy sector is litigation-and-norms, not text.** Protect Democracy, the Society for the Rule of Law, Democracy Defenders Fund, the American Bar Association, and Keep Our Republic are all active on executive defiance in 2025–26. Every one of them works through briefs, trackers, bar complaints, statements of principle, and statutory asks. None proposes an amendment.
4. **Congress has nothing pending.** The memo's prior Congress.gov search found no joint resolution constitutionalizing judicial-compliance or anti-impoundment rules; a fresh scan of 119th-Congress joint resolutions this session surfaced amendments on congressional pay, viewpoint-neutral speech regulation, term limits, voting rights, and the debt — and nothing on this subject. Confirmed, not disturbed.

The practical consequence for the founder's decision: **on Article 7 there is no adoption option, only an origination decision.** The question is not "whose language do we take" but "do we write the one that does not exist." That is a different kind of choice than Articles 1 and 2 present, and it carries a different risk profile — no accumulated drafting history to borrow, and no accumulated opposition research to defend against.

## Per-org detail

### Protect Democracy — the largest player, and it does not propose text

The biggest organization in the pro-compliance space by an order of magnitude. Per [ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/814777062), the Protect Democracy Project (EIN 81-4777062) is a 501(c)(3), tax-exempt since August 2017, headquartered in Washington, DC. FY2024: revenue $55,482,925, expenses $34,368,875, net assets $93,052,687, with $14,817,272 in salaries and wages. Ian Bassin is president and executive director ($377,276 in 2024). Board members named on the 2024 filing include Cecilia Muñoz (Obama Domestic Policy Council) and Kori Schake (AEI) — a deliberate cross-partisan pairing.

On this issue Protect Democracy runs [an explainer on the administration's conflict with the courts](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/the-trump-administrations-conflict-with-the-courts-explained/), which describes the enforcement tools courts already hold — civil contempt, accountability through litigation — and emphasizes that "litigators and district court judges are using the tools at their disposal." Its legislative vehicle is the Protecting Our Democracy Act, a statutory package. **It proposes no constitutional amendment on judicial compliance**, and its own framing treats existing law as adequate in kind if not in force. That is a considered position, not an oversight, and it is the single most important data point in this memo: the best-resourced organization in the field has looked at this problem for a decade and has not concluded that text is the answer.

### Society for the Rule of Law — the right-of-center principle, stated as a duty of citizens

Founded 2018 as Checks & Balances by lawyers who served in Republican administrations; relaunched in 2023 as the Society for the Rule of Law. Its c4 entity files as C & B Group (EIN 83-3837181), a 501(c)(4) with FY2024 revenue of $301,335 and expenses of $149,795 — a small operation whose weight comes from its signatories, not its budget. Officers on the 2024 filing: George T. Conway III (president), Stuart M. Gerson (treasurer), Alan C. Raul (secretary), with J. Michael Luttig and Barbara Comstock on the board and Gregg Nunziata as executive director from August 2024. (A separate c3, the Society for the Rule of Law Institute, files amicus briefs; its EIN was not independently verified this session.)

Its [Statement of Principles](https://societyfortheruleoflaw.org/statement-of-principles/) contains the closest thing in the American landscape to a normative statement of the Article 7 rule from the right. The operative duty reads: *"To accept the legitimacy, respect the authority, and abide by the decisions and judgments of the federal courts interpreting the Constitution and laws of the United States."* A companion duty runs to election losers: *"To respect, support, and defend the right of aggrieved candidates to invoke the judicial process to challenge the results of elections and the corresponding obligation of such candidates to respect the resulting decisions of the courts of law."*

Two things about this matter for our drafting. First, it is the *obey-while-you-object* structure — challenge through the process, respect the outcome — which is precisely what our §2 safe harbor encodes. Second, and decisively, **it is addressed to "American citizens" as a duty, not to officers as a rule, and it is not constitutional text.** It has no tiering by court rank, no stay condition, no answer to departmentalism, and no enforcement. It is a pledge, and it was drafted to be signed rather than ratified.

### Democracy Defenders Fund — the fullest diagnosis, the thinnest prescription

DDF's [essay of January 20, 2026](https://www.democracydefendersfund.org/autocracy-v-democracy/executive-defiance) is the most complete civil-society account of the 2025–26 compliance record. It adopts Litman and Deacon's "legalistic noncompliance" frame, walks the *J.G.G.* and *Abrego Garcia* cases, and draws foreign comparisons (El Salvador, Turkey, Hungary). Its prescription is entirely extra-constitutional: bar complaints (it filed one against the Attorney General with the Florida bar), courts using discovery and daily status reports as pressure, and public mobilization. Its conclusion — *"the administration is most likely to respect judicial decrees when the political and legal cost of violating them is too high"* — is the Staton/Vanberg publicity thesis restated by practitioners. **No amendment; no model statute.**

### The American Bar Association — resolutions, and one telling omission

The ABA House of Delegates has passed resolutions on adjacent questions in both 2025 and 2026. At the [2025 Midyear](https://www.americanbar.org/news/abanews/aba-news-archives/2025/02/aba-house-adopts-policies-judicial-security-ethics/) (Feb. 4, 2025, Phoenix): Resolution 203 (binding Supreme Court ethics code), 201 and 202 (judicial security), 200 (judge-shopping and case assignment). At the [2026 Midyear](https://www.americanbar.org/news/abanews/aba-news-archives/2026/02/midyear-house-of-delegates-results/) (Feb. 9, 2026, San Antonio): Resolution 200 opposes threats and violence against judges and "urges parties who disagree with any court decision to follow the applicable appellate process"; Resolution 510 addresses DOJ independence; 506 addresses domestic military deployment; 509 encourages state prosecutors to pursue federal officials who violate state law while acting outside their federal authority.

Resolution 200's appellate-process language is the same obey-while-you-object principle, again in hortatory form. Across two years of resolutions written in the middle of the compliance crisis, **the ABA never asked for constitutional text** — and the organization with the most drafting capacity in the American legal profession chose Resolution 509's route instead, pushing enforcement down to state prosecutors rather than up into the Constitution.

### Keep Our Republic — bipartisan judges, addressed to lawyers

In January 2026 Keep Our Republic issued [Principles for the Independence of the Legal Profession](https://keepourrepublic.org/what-we-do/initiatives/legal-principles/), authored by more than 30 former federal judges appointed by presidents of both parties. The principles run to the conduct of the bar — freedom from government coercion, pro bono duty, refusal of compromising representations — and the preamble commits to a system "binding on both private actors, entities and the government." Genuinely cross-partisan in authorship, and structurally irrelevant to our draft: it regulates lawyers, not officers.

### The National Constitution Center Drafting Project — the one org with text, and what its text shows

Funded by Jeff Yass; three teams. Libertarian: Ilya Shapiro, Timothy Sandefur, Christina Mulligan. Progressive: Caroline Fredrickson, Jamal Greene, Melissa Murray. Conservative: Robert P. George, Michael McConnell, Colleen A. Sheehan, Ilan Wurman. The five agreed amendments are quoted in the negative-space section below; none addresses this subject. The three individual constitutions are examined next, because their near-misses are the most useful drafting intelligence in this memo.

### The Power of the Purse Coalition — the cross-ideological precedent, on the half we held back

The one place where a genuinely cross-spectrum coalition has organized on any part of Article 7's subject matter is the spending half. The [coalition's letter of February 2, 2021](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/power-of-the-purse-coalition-shares-priorities-with-117th-congress/) was signed by Demand Progress, FreedomWorks, Lincoln Network, National Taxpayers Union, Project on Government Oversight, Protect Democracy, R Street Institute, and Taxpayers for Common Sense — left, right, and libertarian in one document. Its self-description is the model our memo's dual-framing note calls for: *"Our organizations represent many different positions on the ideological spectrum, but we formed this coalition based on a firm belief that the erosion of congressional capacity and authority to the aggrandizement of the executive branch is a nonpartisan problem requiring bipartisan solutions."* And its closing asks Congress to weigh the reforms *"regardless of who sits in the Oval Office"* — the veil-of-ignorance framing, in a coalition's own words.

Its asks are all statutory: OMB apportionment transparency, National Emergencies Act reform (citing Mike Lee and Chip Roy's ARTICLE ONE Act), reasserting the Antideficiency Act and Impoundment Control Act, IG and GAO strengthening, and public Congressional Justifications. **No amendment.** This matters for the held-back Appropriations Fidelity Clause: the coalition proves the dual framing can hold a cross-spectrum roster together, and simultaneously shows that everyone who has organized on it treats the fix as legislative.

### The adversary side — Heritage, and the departmentalist tradition

There is no organization mobilized *against* an executive-compliance amendment, because there is no amendment to oppose. There is a developed intellectual position that such an amendment would collide with. The Heritage Foundation's [*Against Judicial Supremacy: The Founders and the Limits on the Courts*](https://www.heritage.org/courts/report/against-judicial-supremacy-the-founders-and-the-limits-the-courts) (January 25, 2019, Carson Holloway) argues that *The Federalist* "lends no aid to the belief that the Supreme Court is the ultimate interpreter of constitutional meaning, unanswerable for its interpretations to any authority but itself," and lists among its key takeaways that "Congress may rightly discipline the judiciary by impeaching and removing from office judges who abuse the judicial power."

Read carefully, this is an argument about *interpretive* supremacy, not about obedience to judgments in an adjudicated case — the same distinction Lincoln drew in the First Inaugural and the same one our §1 turns on. That is the opening: our draft binds conduct in the case and leaves the interpretive debate untouched. It is also the risk: a campaign that lets "obey the courts" be heard as "the courts are supreme" walks directly into the tradition Heritage has spent decades articulating, and the memo's district-judge-veto trap note is the operational form of that risk.

Cato occupies a middle position — skeptical of judicial supremacy as a theory, and publicly tracking executive defiance as a practice — without proposing text in either direction.

### Academic work — the field's actual center of gravity

The serious drafting energy on this question is in law reviews, not in orgs, and even there it is remedial rather than constitutional.

- **Baude, Bray & Levy, "Remedies for a Constitutional Crisis," 139 Harv. L. Rev. 1747 (published online June 10, 2026)** — the leading treatment. Its stated aim is modest and explicitly not amendment-shaped: *"We will lay out the menu of options that are available to federal judges, highlighting the tradeoffs among them."* The authors write that "the range of remedial options available to courts is broader than it may seem at first — and that the best path is not always obvious," and decline to "crown any one choice as the right one." A menu for judges, not a rule for officers.
- **Deacon & Litman, "Legalistic Noncompliance," 75 Duke L.J. 1437 (2026)** — names the phenomenon our §1 second sentence is aimed at: an executive that "uses the language of the law as cover to claim that it is complying with court orders when in fact it is not." Diagnostic; proposes no text.
- **Schmidt & Metzger, "Some Realism About Constitutional Remedies," 139 Harv. L. Rev. 1834 (2026)** — companion piece in the same symposium.
- **Vermeule**, contra, in the New York Times (July 31, 2025), argues the defiance runs the other way.

The symposium is the field's most concentrated effort on this exact problem in the current cycle, and it produced no constitutional language.

## Language comparison vs. our draft

There is no rival text to compare section by section, so this section does the next most useful thing: it measures the nearest drafted language in existence against our §§1–2 and reports what each near-miss tells us.

Our text, for reference (PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md §7):

> **Section 1.** A judgment or order of the Supreme Court of the United States binds the United States and all its officers, including the President, to whom it applies. A judgment or order of any other court of the United States likewise binds the United States and the officers to whom it applies unless and until it is stayed, reversed, or modified by a court of competent jurisdiction; no officer may suspend, disregard, or refuse to execute such a judgment or order on the ground of the officer's own view of its lawfulness.
>
> **Section 2.** Nothing in this article shall be construed to abridge the right of any officer to appeal, to seek a stay or modification of any judgment or order, or to advocate in good faith for a change in law.

### The near-misses, in order of proximity

**1. The NCC Conservative Constitution — a bindingness clause pointed the wrong way.** Article II, §3 provides that the President may issue executive orders *"which orders shall be binding on all executive branch officers on penalty of removal."* This is the only clause in any of the three constitutions that pairs bindingness with an automatic personal consequence — exactly the architecture our held-back Consequences Clause reaches for. But it runs downward from the President to his subordinates, not from courts to the executive. The same document abolishes sovereign immunity in Article III, §3 (*"Neither the United States nor any State shall enjoy immunity from suit in the courts of the United States"*), making the executive suable — and then says nothing about what happens when it loses. A conservative drafting team wrote binding-on-penalty-of-removal into a constitution and did not point it at court orders.

**2. The NCC Libertarian Constitution — a court order directing executive fiscal conduct, deliberately defanged.** Article I, §11 permits members of Congress to enforce the spending cap by original action in the Supreme Court, giving the treasurer authority over outlays *"when required by a court order enforcing the provisions of this article"* — and then immediately limits it: *"The order of the court shall not specify the particular outlays to be made or reduced,"* with compliance permitted as late as "the end of the third full fiscal year following the court order." This is the mirror image of our held-back Appropriations Fidelity Clause: a court-enforced spending *ceiling* rather than a floor. Its drafters, having written the only judicial-order-against-the-executive mechanism in the project, wrote in both a content limit and a three-year grace period. Anyone drafting a self-executing release clause should read that as a warning about how hard the fiscal version is to make bite.

**3. The NCC Conservative Constitution on appropriations — an auditor with no remedy.** Article I, §10 establishes a Board of Treasury with "authority to judge whether expenditures proposed by the executive department comply with the appropriations laws enacted by Congress." A body that certifies compliance is precisely the *"certification of the Comptroller General"* mechanic our held-back clause borrows — but it reports to Congress, has no judicial hook, and carries no consequence. The same section obliges the President to line-item veto appropriations down to anticipated revenue, which cuts the opposite way from anti-impoundment.

**4. The Society for the Rule of Law's principle** — the obey-while-you-object structure, as a citizens' duty rather than an officers' rule, quoted above.

**5. The ABA's Resolution 200 (2026)** — "urges parties who disagree with any court decision to follow the applicable appellate process," which is our §2 in hortatory voice.

**6. All three constitutions' Supremacy and oath clauses** — retained essentially verbatim from 1787, binding "the judges in every State" and requiring officers to "support this Constitution." Three teams rewrote the document from scratch and none converted the oath into a duty to obey judgments.

### What we would gain, lose, or add back

| If we adopted… | We would gain | We would lose | We would need to add back |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The NCC five amendments wholesale** | A cross-ideological pedigree and drafting credibility on Court structure (XXXI's eighteen-year terms overlap our Article 2) | Everything Article 7 does — none of the five touches judicial compliance | §1 bindingness, §2 safe harbor: the entire article |
| **The Conservative Constitution's Art. II §3 removal sanction** | An automatic, personal, office-linked consequence with cross-ideological authorship | Nothing in §§1–2 (it is additive) | Redirection of the clause from presidential orders to court orders; notice-and-hearing and finality guardrails; a *Trump v. Anderson* answer on who adjudicates. Note this is our **held-back** Consequences Clause and remains unpolled |
| **The Libertarian Constitution's Art. I §11 enforcement route** | A drafted model of congressional standing plus court-ordered fiscal compliance | Nothing in §§1–2 | Reversal of polarity (floor, not ceiling); removal of the "shall not specify the particular outlays" limit and the three-year lag, both of which would gut a release clause |
| **SRL's Statement of Principles as our text** | Signatures from Republican-appointed judges and officials | The tiering by court rank; the stay condition; the anti-departmentalism sentence; any binding force at all | §1 in full — the principle would carry the sentiment but not the rule |
| **The Power of the Purse Coalition's agenda** | An eight-org cross-spectrum roster and a proven dual framing | The constitutional status of every provision | The Appropriations Fidelity Clause, which the coalition's own members treat as statutory |

**On the trap notes.** Our memo flags two traps for §1: the judicial-supremacy trap (an untiered rule hands each district judgeship a national veto) and the departmentalism objection. **No existing org language avoids either, because no existing org language reaches them.** The tiered structure of our §1 and the safe harbor of our §2 have no counterpart anywhere in the surveyed material. If the founder adopts external language on Article 7, he adopts nothing; if he keeps ours, he keeps the only text that addresses the traps the memo identified.

## Partnership vs. conflict

**Endorsable today: nobody's text, because nobody has any.** The partnership question on Article 7 is therefore about coalition and coding, not adoption.

- **Society for the Rule of Law is the highest-value association and the lowest-risk.** Republican-administration lawyers, Luttig and Conway and Comstock, a principle already in writing that matches our §§1–2 in substance. An org whose signatories are overwhelmingly conservative endorsing the compliance principle is the single best answer to the "this is an anti-current-administration measure" framing burden the memo flags. Its budget is small; its signal is not.
- **Keep Our Republic's 30-plus former federal judges appointed by presidents of both parties** are the same asset in a different form, and their principles are addressed to a professional audience rather than a political one — useful for validation, not for coalition.
- **Protect Democracy is the deepest expertise and the heaviest coding risk.** Its analysis of the enforcement circularity is the best available and its board is deliberately cross-partisan, but its public profile in 2025–26 is as the leading litigator against the current administration. Association would code us on an article whose whole strategic value is that the principle polls at 76–80% among Republicans. Take the research; be deliberate about the banner.
- **Democracy Defenders Fund codes hardest of all** — its own framing is "Autocracy vs. Democracy in America" and its work product includes a bar complaint against the sitting Attorney General. Cite its factual record; do not stand with it.
- **The Power of the Purse Coalition is the model to emulate if the Appropriations Fidelity Clause ever returns.** Demand Progress and FreedomWorks and the National Taxpayers Union in one letter is the roster shape that clause needs. Approach would be premature — the clause is held back and unpolled, and the coalition's members have all chosen the statutory route.
- **Heritage is not an adversary to negotiate with but a tradition to draft around.** Its objection is to interpretive supremacy; our §1 binds conduct in the adjudicated case. That distinction is the whole ballgame for the right flank, and it should be load-bearing in any messaging, not a footnote.
- **No Article V convention group is organized on this subject**, so the convention-route conflict that splits reform orgs on campaign finance and term limits does not arise here.
- **The academic route is the realistic partnership.** Baude, Bray, and Levy are the field's center of gravity, they published in June 2026, and they explicitly declined to pick a remedy. A drafted amendment is the thing the symposium did not produce. That is where an origination effort would find its most credible interlocutors — and Baude's presence makes it a conversation that does not code left.
