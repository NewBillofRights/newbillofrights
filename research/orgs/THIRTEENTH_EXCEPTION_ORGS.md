# Organizational landscape — Abolition of the Punishment Exception (Article 9)

Org-landscape pass for **Article 9, Abolition of the Punishment Exception** ([PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) §9), companion to [THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_RESEARCH.md](../THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_RESEARCH.md) and the candidate memo [candidates/THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_CANDIDATE.md](../candidates/THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_CANDIDATE.md). Research conducted 2026-08-19. Every factual claim below was verified at its source in this session; unverifiable items are listed in the return JSON and flagged inline.

## Founder direction (2026-08-19, recorded verbatim)

> Michael prefers to adopt, as close to 100% as the evidence supports, amendment language already proposed by established organizations that have invested deeply in their issue (he named American Promise and U.S. Term Limits), rather than maintaining bespoke drafts — *especially* where an org has deep focus on language relevant to one of our nine amendments. The comparison memos produced by this pass are the input to that decision. Draft-text changes still happen only in research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md with a dated revision note, after Michael decides per article.

## Headline findings

1. **There is exactly one drafted federal amendment text in this space, and it is one sentence long.** The Abolition Amendment — Merkley/Booker in the Senate, Nikema Williams in the House — proposes a single operative sentence: *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude may be imposed as a punishment for a crime."* No savings clause, no enforcement clause, no effective date, and **no restatement of the Thirteenth Amendment's Section 1**. Adopting it 100% would mean deleting three of our four sections.

2. **The Abolition Amendment has not been reintroduced in the 119th Congress.** Merkley has sponsored exactly three joint resolutions in the 119th (student loans, Iran war powers, a CFPB disapproval); Williams has sponsored exactly one (a CFPB disapproval). Neither is the Abolition Amendment. The operative federal text is therefore the 118th Congress version, last introduced 2023-06-14 and never acted on beyond committee referral. This is a live fact with campaign consequences and should be re-checked before publication.

3. **Our memo's central drafting rule — the "Louisiana trap" — is contradicted by Utah's ratified text.** Utah's constitution today carries the *exact* formula our memo says never to write: *"Subsection (1) does not apply to the otherwise lawful administration of the criminal justice system."* Utah passed it at **80.48%**, the highest single-issue result in the entire set. Louisiana ran nearly the same words and lost at 39.15%. The distinguishing variable is not the savings-clause formula; it is the **structure of the deletion** and the **presence of the word "slavery."** This is the single most consequential correction this pass produces, and it is developed in §"The Louisiana trap, re-examined" below.

4. **No organization has drafted anything resembling our Section 2 compulsion sentence or our Section 4 effective date.** On both, we are alone. The compulsion sentence has one piece of external corroboration — a Colorado trial court reached the same operative test by construction — but no org has put it in proposed text. The two-year effective date appears in no federal proposal and no state measure in the set.

5. **The movement is programmatically cross-partisan but institutionally coded left.** Five House Republicans cosponsored the federal resolution and Republican legislatures referred four of the passing measures, but every drafting organization in the space is a criminal-justice-reform nonprofit, ASNN raises through ActBlue, and the campaign's public framing is explicitly racial-justice framing. Endorsement carries coding risk that the ballot record does not.

## Per-organization detail

### 1. The Abolition Amendment — the only drafted federal text

**Identity.** Not an organization but a pair of joint resolutions, reintroduced each Congress since the 116th. Senate sponsor **Sen. Jeff Merkley (D-OR)**, with **Sen. Cory Booker (D-NJ)**; House sponsor **Rep. Nikema Williams (D-GA-5)**.

**Bill numbers, verified on congress.gov:**

| Congress | Senate | House | Introduced | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 117th (2021–22) | S.J.Res.21 | H.J.Res.53 | June 2021 | Referred; 193 House cosponsors reported by sponsor |
| 118th (2023–24) | **S.J.Res.33** | **H.J.Res.72** | **2023-06-14** | Referred to Judiciary in both chambers; no further action |
| 119th (2025–26) | **not introduced** | **not introduced** | — | **No 119th Congress version exists as of 2026-08-19** |

The 119th finding is a negative verified directly against each sponsor's congress.gov sponsored-joint-resolutions listing rather than inferred from a keyword search. Merkley's 119th joint resolutions are S.J.Res.196 (Dept. of Education rule), S.J.Res.163 (Iran hostilities), and S.J.Res.156 (CFPB rule). Williams's sole 119th joint resolution is H.J.Res.171 (CFPB rule). Neither list contains an Abolition Amendment.

**The proposed language, verbatim** (S.J.Res.33, 118th Cong., and identically H.J.Res.72):

> *Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled (two-thirds of each House concurring therein), That the following article is proposed as an amendment to the Constitution of the United States, which shall be valid to all intents and purposes as part of the Constitution when ratified by the legislatures of three-fourths of the several States:*
>
> *"article —*
>
> *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude may be imposed as a punishment for a crime."*

That is the entire operative text. The resolution's substantive content beyond that sentence sits in seven **whereas** clauses, which are not part of the amendment and would not be ratified. Two of them are directly relevant to our Section 2, because they place in the legislative record the very reassurances our Section 2 places in the Constitution:

> *Whereas Congress recognizes the value of high-quality work programs that provide useful skills and stability for people in correctional institutions, and it is not the intent of Congress to interfere with opportunities for incarcerated people to consent to work or to modify any employment protections currently available to people in correctional institutions; and*
>
> *Whereas Congress additionally acknowledges the importance of sentencing alternatives, and it is not the purpose of this amendment to interfere with the discretion of a court to offer community service as an alternative to incarceration: Now, therefore, be it*

**This is the most important structural finding in the memo.** The Abolition Amendment's drafters faced exactly the question our Section 2 answers — do prison work programs survive? do sentencing alternatives survive? — and answered it **in the preamble rather than in the amendment**. Their savings language is non-binding legislative history; ours is constitutional text. A court construing the ratified Abolition Amendment would find nothing in the Constitution preserving work programs, and would have to reach for the whereas clauses of a resolution most readers will never see.

**Track record.** H.J.Res.72 accumulated **129 cosponsors: 124 Democratic, 5 Republican.** The five Republicans, verified individually: **Rep. Nancy Mace (R-SC-1)** (2023-06-30), **Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar (R-FL-27)** (2023-07-12), **Rep. William R. Timmons IV (R-SC-4)** (2023-08-08), **Rep. Chris Stewart (R-UT-2)** (2023-08-08), **Rep. Blake D. Moore (R-UT-1)** (2023-08-18). Both Utah Republicans cosponsored — consistent with Utah's 80.48% ballot result. No hearing, no markup, no floor vote in any Congress. Never has this amendment received a recorded vote in either chamber.

**Partisan coding.** 4% Republican cosponsorship against a 66% Republican polling floor is the widest gap between public and congressional support anywhere in our corpus. The category polls bipartisan and legislates partisan. That gap is an argument *for* our project's framing, not against the category.

## The Louisiana trap, re-examined

Our research memo states the rule: *"never write a criminal-justice-administration carve-out"* — a savings clause phrased around "the lawful administration of criminal justice" reads as preserving the exception, and lost 39.15%. This pass verified the ratified texts of every passing state, and **the rule as stated cannot be right**, because Utah's ratified constitution contains that clause and produced the best number in the set.

**Verified at the primary source** (Utah Legislature, Art. I §21, effective 1/1/2021):

> **Article I, Section 21. [Slavery and involuntary servitude forbidden -- Limitation.]**
> *(1) Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within this State.*
> *(2) Subsection (1) does not apply to the otherwise lawful administration of the criminal justice system.*

**Louisiana Amendment 7, as it would have read (defeated 39.15%):**

> *(B)(1) Slavery and involuntary servitude are prohibited* ~~, except in the latter case as punishment for crime.~~ *(2) Subparagraph (1) of this Paragraph does not apply to the otherwise lawful administration of criminal justice.*

The savings clauses are near-identical. Utah scored **80.48%**; Louisiana scored **39.15%**. The clause is therefore not the variable. Four differences are:

1. **Louisiana's prior text had no "slavery" exception to delete.** Louisiana's existing clause read "except in the latter case as punishment for crime" — *the latter case* being involuntary servitude only. Slavery was already unconditionally prohibited. So Louisiana's amendment deleted an exception to *servitude* while adding a *new* carve-out covering both — it could be read as net-broadening the state's authority. Utah deleted an exception that had applied to both nouns. **Louisiana was, structurally, not a repeal.**
2. **The ballot question.** Utah's question told voters plainly that it would "remove the language that allows slavery and involuntary servitude as punishment for a crime" and separately "clarify" the administration point. Louisiana's ballot title scored **grade level 13** on Flesch-Kincaid with a Flesch Reading Ease of **26** — near-unreadable, in 28 words.
3. **The sponsor urged a no vote.** Rep. Edmond Jordan (D), the amendment's own author: *"Because of the ambiguity of how it was drafted, I'm asking that people vote against it, so that we can go and clean it up with the intent of bringing it back next year and making sure that the language is clear and unambiguous."* No measure survives its author campaigning against it.
4. **Louisiana's savings clause did the work alone.** Utah's subsection (2) sits under an unqualified subsection (1) that a voter reads first and understands completely.

**The corrected drafting rule, which our Section 2 already satisfies:** a savings clause does not cost votes, and even the criminal-justice-administration formula survives contact with an 80% electorate — *provided* the operative prohibition is unqualified, comes first, contains the word "slavery," and the deletion is a genuine repeal rather than a swap. What killed Louisiana was ambiguity about whether anything was actually being repealed, certified by the sponsor himself.

**This should be corrected in [THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_RESEARCH.md](../THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_RESEARCH.md)** at two points: the State Models table describes Utah as *"Silent — bare deletion,"* which is inaccurate; and the trap note as written would forbid a formula that has in fact passed at the highest rate in the set. Our Section 2 is unaffected on the merits — it is visibly a work-programs clause, which is the safer of the two available formulas — but the *reason* it is safer is different from the reason the memo gives, and the memo currently overstates the evidence.

## Language comparison: their text vs. our draft

Our Article 9, verbatim from [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md):

> **Section 1.** Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction.
>
> **Section 2.** Nothing in this article shall prohibit a person confined under lawful sentence from working voluntarily, or in a work program authorized by law, with compensation and conditions as Congress or the States shall by law provide. No person shall be compelled to labor by the infliction or threat of punishment.
>
> **Section 3.** Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.
>
> **Section 4.** This article shall take effect two years after its ratification.

### Section by section

**Section 1 — the strike.** Theirs: *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude may be imposed as a punishment for a crime."* Ours: the Thirteenth's own sentence with the exception removed.

These are **not** the same edit, and the difference is larger than it looks.

- **Ours restates the general prohibition**; theirs states a *narrower rule about punishment*. Their sentence, read alone as a new article, prohibits imposing slavery as a punishment — it does not itself prohibit slavery generally, because the existing Thirteenth Amendment §1 continues to do that. Their amendment operates *on* the exception; ours *replaces* the sentence containing it.
- **Ours keeps the settled words.** "Shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction" carries 160 years of construction, including *Kozminski*'s definition of involuntary servitude and *Butler v. Perry* on civic duties. Their sentence introduces the new phrase *"may be imposed as a punishment for a crime,"* which has no construction at all and invites a fresh question: is labor *incident to* lawful confinement "imposed as a punishment"? A corrections department would argue no.
- **Both keep the word "slavery" in the operative sentence** — the single most robust empirical finding in the file. Neither drafting choice risks the California failure.
- **Their approach leaves the exception clause physically in the Constitution.** Because their article does not restate §1, the printed Thirteenth Amendment would continue to read "except as a punishment for crime, whereof the party shall have been duly convicted," with a later article overriding it. Ours produces a Constitution a citizen can read correctly. Our memo's recommendation against an express repealer was reasoned on ballot grounds; the same reasoning cuts against their structure.

**Verdict on Section 1: ours is better drafted and theirs is better credentialed.** Theirs is what 129 members of Congress and 70-plus organizations have signed. Ours is what a court would construe more predictably.

**Section 2 — the savings clause and the compulsion sentence.** **They have no Section 2.** Their equivalent content lives in two whereas clauses, which are not ratified and bind nobody. Nothing in the ratified Abolition Amendment would preserve prison work programs, and nothing would state the compulsion test.

Against the *state* record our Section 2's first sentence has direct ancestry:

| State | Savings language as ratified | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tennessee** | *"Nothing in this section shall prohibit an inmate from working when the inmate has been duly convicted of a crime."* | **79.53%**, every county yes |
| **Utah** | *"Subsection (1) does not apply to the otherwise lawful administration of the criminal justice system."* | **80.48%** |
| **Oregon** | *"Upon conviction of a crime, an Oregon court or a probation or parole agency may order the convicted person to engage in education, counseling, treatment, community service or other alternatives to incarceration…"* | **55.59%** |
| Colorado, Nebraska, Nevada, Vermont | none — bare deletion | 66.21 / 68.23 / 60.60 / 88.71% |

Our first sentence improves on Tennessee's in the three ways our memo identifies (it says *voluntarily*; it routes compensation and conditions to law; it uses "confined under lawful sentence" rather than "duly convicted"). Oregon's clause (2) covers **court-ordered alternatives to incarceration** — community service, treatment, counseling — which our Section 2 reaches only by implication through "work program authorized by law." Since Oregon is the only state whose measure drew a named institutional opponent and still passed, its explicit protection of sentencing alternatives is worth considering as an addition; **the Abolition Amendment's second whereas clause protects the same ground, confirming that both drafting teams saw the same gap.**

Our second sentence — *"No person shall be compelled to labor by the infliction or threat of punishment"* — **exists in no proposed or ratified text anywhere in this landscape.** It is genuinely ours. Its only external support is judicial: the Colorado ruling in *Mortis v. Polis* reached the same operative test by construing a bare deletion, holding the department in violation for compelling work under threat of punishment while sixteen prison industries continued operating. That is meaningful corroboration that the test is workable and does not shut down prison work — but it arrived through litigation, not text, and it is one trial court.

**Section 3 — enforcement.** **They have no enforcement clause.** This is a real gap in their draft. Because their article is a freestanding new article rather than a replacement of Thirteenth Amendment §1, it is at least arguable that the Thirteenth's existing §2 enforcement power — the authority under which federal anti-trafficking and forced-labor statutes operate — does not extend to it. Our Section 3 carries that power forward verbatim and removes the question. **Adopting their text 100% would create an enforcement ambiguity that our draft does not have.**

**Section 4 — effective date.** **No federal proposal and no state measure in the set contains one.** Every state removal took effect on the ordinary schedule, and Colorado's experience — a court-ordered end to compelled labor with prison industries continuing — is the only operational record available. Our Section 4 stands alone in the landscape. The Oregon sheriffs' objection (*"increased costs to local jail operations"*) is the live evidence that the concession answers a real institutional fear; the counter-argument our memo records — that a delayed effective date on a slavery prohibition is a bad sentence to defend in public — is unaffected by anything found in this pass.

### If we adopted the Abolition Amendment's language 100%

| | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Gain** | The only text with congressional standing: bill numbers to cite, 129 cosponsors including 5 Republicans, 70-plus organizational endorsers, and a coalition that would recognize our draft as theirs. Maximal brevity — one sentence a voter can read aloud. Exact match to what the 66% Republican floor was measured on. Zero risk of a reviewer calling any clause an addition. |
| **Lose** | **Section 2 entirely** — no constitutional protection for prison work programs (demoted to unratified whereas clauses), and no compulsion test, which is the clause that answers the evasion pattern 76% of surveyed incarcerated workers report. **Section 3 entirely** — an arguable enforcement gap, since a freestanding article may not inherit the Thirteenth's §2 power. **Section 4 entirely** — no transition period, against a named law-enforcement objection about jail operating costs. The settled construction of the Thirteenth's own words, traded for the untested phrase "imposed as a punishment for a crime." A Constitution that still *prints* the exception clause. |
| **Need to add back** | Realistically all three of Sections 2, 3, and 4 — i.e. everything except the strike itself. At which point we have our own draft again, with a differently worded Section 1. |

**This is the inverse of the campaign-finance situation.** There, an established org had invested deeply in language and our draft was the newcomer. Here, the established text is a single sentence whose drafters deliberately pushed every hard question into a preamble. Our draft is not competing with a more carefully engineered instrument; it is the more carefully engineered instrument.

### The recommended disposition

**Adopt their Section 1 sentence only if the founder wants coalition-matching, and keep our Sections 2–4 regardless.** Two coherent options:

- **Option A (recommended): keep our draft as written, and cite the Abolition Amendment as the precedent our Section 1 tracks.** Our Section 1 already *is* what the amendment proposes, in the memo's own words — expressed as a restatement rather than as a new rule. We lose nothing and gain a better-constructed instrument. The public page can say plainly that a federal amendment has been introduced in four consecutive Congresses, that ours adopts its object, and that ours adds the work-programs, enforcement, and transition provisions its sponsors put in a preamble.
- **Option B: substitute their exact sentence as our Section 1** and retain Sections 2–4. This maximizes coalition legibility at the cost of the settled Thirteenth Amendment construction and of leaving the exception clause in print. Our memo's own trap analysis — keep the original words, let no fragment of the old authorization survive — argues against it.

**Option C, adopting their text wholesale, is not recommended**, because it would delete three sections that answer objections raised on the record by a state sheriffs' association, by a trial court, and by the Abolition Amendment's own drafters in their preamble.

## Partnership vs. conflict

**Could we endorse their text?** Partly, and carefully. The Abolition Amendment's *object* is identical to ours and its Section 1 is a defensible rendering. But its drafting is thinner than ours in three places, and endorsing it without qualification would mean endorsing an instrument with no savings clause, no enforcement clause, and no transition — which is inconsistent with our own published reasoning.

**Could we join their coalition?** This is the sharpest coding question in the corpus.

- **What association would buy:** the deepest state-campaign expertise in existence on this specific clause; nine ratified texts' worth of drafting experience; organizers in Utah, Tennessee, Nebraska, and South Carolina who have won Republican-legislature referrals; and a white-paper research capacity.
- **What association would cost:** ASNN fundraises through **ActBlue**. Worth Rises's mission statement is "dismantling the prison industry" and it runs corporate-pressure campaigns. The endorser bloc — LatinoJustice, ACRE, JustLeadershipUSA, Vera, the ACLU affiliates — is uniformly progressive, with **no conservative, libertarian, or faith-based counterpart organization identified anywhere in this landscape**. Formal coalition membership would code us on exactly the axis our mission forbids, and would do so on the one category in our corpus with the *narrowest party gap* (19 points) — the category where we have the least need for help and the most to lose from coding.
- **The asymmetry is decisive.** On campaign finance or term limits, an established org's endorsement network is a genuine accelerant. Here, the category already passes at 60–80% in Republican-voting states with legislative referral, and its congressional problem is precisely that it reads as a Democratic bill (124 D / 5 R). **Joining the existing coalition would deepen the exact failure mode the category already has.**

**Recommended posture: cite, do not join.** Use their texts, bill numbers, and state campaign record as sources — all of it is public and citable. Do not sign endorsement letters, do not appear on coalition mastheads, do not use ActBlue-adjacent infrastructure. If a single external endorsement is ever wanted for the page, **Polaris Project** is the least-coded organization in the landscape and its published framing ("doesn't mean abolishing prison labor altogether") already matches our Section 2.

**Where reform orgs conflict.** No conflict of the Article-V-convention kind exists here: **no organization in this space proposes a convention route**, and no state has passed an Article V application on this subject that this pass could identify. Every actor uses the congressional route. The only intra-movement conflict on record is the Louisiana 2022 split, in which the reform side campaigned against its own ballot measure — a conflict about drafting quality, which is precisely the question this memo addresses.

## Corrections this pass recommends to the research memo

Recorded here rather than applied, per the no-edit constraint:

1. **State Models table, Utah row** — currently "Silent — bare deletion." Utah's ratified text contains an express savings clause: *"Subsection (1) does not apply to the otherwise lawful administration of the criminal justice system."*
2. **Louisiana trap note** — as written it forbids a formula that Utah ratified and passed at 80.48%. The operative distinction is repeal-versus-swap, ballot readability, and sponsor support, not the savings-clause formula. See §"The Louisiana trap, re-examined."
3. **Savings-clause count** — the memo states Tennessee is "the only ballot measure in the set whose ratified text contains such a clause." Three do: Tennessee, Utah, and Oregon. The claim that a savings clause does not cost votes is *strengthened* by this, not weakened: the two highest results in the set (Utah 80.48%, Tennessee 79.53%) are both savings-clause texts.
4. **Federal status** — the memo should record that the Abolition Amendment has **not** been reintroduced in the 119th Congress as of 2026-08-19, and that its last introduction was 2023-06-14 (S.J.Res.33 / H.J.Res.72).
5. **Oregon clause (2)** — the memo summarizes it as authorizing "court-ordered programs in lieu of incarceration"; the ratified text is broader (education, counseling, treatment, community service or other alternatives) and is worth quoting if the founder considers extending Section 2.
