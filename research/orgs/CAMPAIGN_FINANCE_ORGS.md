## Headline findings

1. **Four organizations have drafted amendment text in this category, and they are not variations on one theme — they are three incompatible constitutional theories.** American Promise's For Our Freedom Amendment is a *construction rule* ("Nothing in this Constitution shall be construed to forbid…"). Move to Amend's We the People Amendment is a *rights-stripping rule* ("shall have no rights under the Constitution"). Free Speech For People's Free and Fair Elections Amendment is a *self-executing policy schedule* that writes $100 and $1,000 into the Constitution. Our draft is a *grant of enumerated power* with a named purpose. Choosing an org's text is choosing its theory, not borrowing its wording.
2. **The named priority org's text exists in two materially different versions, and the congressional one is the operative one.** American Promise's website posts a three-section text in which the artificial-entity power sits in Section 3 alongside enforcement. [H.J.Res. 191](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/191/text) (Barrett, R-MI, introduced 06/03/2026) moves that power into Section 2 and expands the covered elections to "State or local ballot initiatives or referendums." Any adoption decision must name which version, because they are not interchangeable.
3. **The For Our Freedom Amendment is the only text in the category with a Republican House sponsor, and it is also the only one that omits three things our draft has**: a disclosure right, a foreign-money power, and a press savings clause. That is the entire trade. Its cross-partisan reach is real and is bought partly with brevity.
4. **The "25 states" number is real but does not mean 25 states endorsed this amendment.** Idaho, the 25th, adopted [SJM 109](https://legislature.idaho.gov/sessioninfo/2026/legislation/SJM109) on Tenth Amendment and state-sovereignty grounds, asked Congress to champion an amendment "if necessary," and named no amendment. Both chambers adopted it by **voice vote**, so no roll call exists. The count is a measure of movement reach, not of ratification-ready consensus on a specific text.
5. **The adversary's best brief was written against a text very close to ours, not against American Promise's.** The Institute for Free Speech's [2014 statement on S.J.Res. 19](https://www.ifs.org/expert-analysis/statement-of-david-keating-to-the-senate-judiciary-committee-on-the-constitutional-amendment-proposal/) attacks the purpose-clause preamble, the "political equality" rationale, and the press savings clause by name — the three components our Sections 2 and 6 carry and the For Our Freedom Amendment mostly does not. Adopting American Promise's text would reduce our exposure to that brief. It would also give up the clauses that brief exists to attack.
6. **The doctrinal ground moved during this pass.** In *National Republican Senatorial Committee v. FEC* (June 30, 2026) the Supreme Court struck federal limits on coordinated party expenditures and overruled *FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee* (*Colorado II*, 2001). Both American Promise and the Institute for Free Speech treated it as a landmark. The memo's core claim — that the target is the whole *Buckley* line and not *Citizens United* alone — is strengthened by it.

## 1. American Promise — the For Our Freedom Amendment

**Identity.** Cross-partisan organization headquartered in Concord, Massachusetts, operating as a paired 501(c)(4) and 501(c)(3) education fund. Cofounder and CEO Jeff Clements, who argued the case for this amendment in the *Harvard Law & Policy Review* in [July 2019](https://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/wp-content/uploads/sites/89/2019/07/Clements.pdf) and submitted a [working paper to the ABA Task Force for American Democracy](https://americanpromise.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/American-Promise-ABA-Task-Force-Working-Paper-w-logo.pdf) (updated April 2024). The organization runs state chapters, a candidate pledge program, and a research arm that has published state-specific spending reports (Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Maine) and a recurring foreign-money report.

### The text — and the version problem

**This is the single most important drafting finding in this memo.** American Promise's own website and the joint resolution it endorses carry *different* texts. Both are quoted verbatim below.

**Version A — as introduced, [H.J.Res. 191](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/191/text), 119th Congress, Rep. Tom Barrett (R-MI-7), introduced June 3, 2026, referred to House Judiciary:**

> "**section 1.** We the People have compelling sovereign interests in the freedom of speech, representative self-government, federalism, the integrity of the electoral process, and the political equality of natural persons.
>
> "**section 2.** Nothing in this Constitution shall be construed to forbid Congress or the States, within their respective jurisdictions, from reasonably regulating and limiting contributions to and spending in campaigns for elections for public office, elections for public office, and State or local ballot initiatives or referendums, including by distinguishing between natural persons and artificial entities and prohibiting artificial entities from raising and spending money in such campaigns, elections, and State or local ballot initiatives or referendums.
>
> "**section 3.** Congress and the States shall have the power to implement and enforce this article by appropriate legislation."

**Version B — as posted at [americanpromise.net](https://americanpromise.net/our-work/for-our-freedom-amendment/):** Section 1 is identical. Section 2 reads "…from reasonably regulating and limiting contributions and spending in campaigns, elections, or ballot measures" — with **no** artificial-entity clause. Section 3 carries the artificial-entity power instead: "Congress and the States shall have the power to implement and enforce this article by appropriate legislation **and may distinguish between natural persons and artificial entities, including by prohibiting artificial entities from raising and spending money in campaigns, elections, or ballot measures**."

The difference is not cosmetic. In Version A the artificial-entity power is grammatically subordinate to the "reasonably regulating and limiting" standard — the word *reasonably* governs it. In Version B the artificial-entity power sits in the enforcement section, unmodified by any reasonableness standard. Version B also says "ballot measures" without limitation, where Version A says "**State or local** ballot initiatives or referendums." **Any adoption decision must name a version.** For every comparison in this memo, Version A (the joint resolution) is treated as the operative text.

### Route and track record

- **Congress-proposes, not Article V convention.** American Promise pursues state *memorials* urging Congress to act, which — unlike convention applications — are not subject to rescission campaigns and travel in red states.
- **25 states.** Idaho became the 25th on March 30, 2026, per American Promise's [April 8 release](https://americanpromise.net/half-of-u-s-states-now-on-record-urging-congress-to-propose-amendment-on-election-spending/); Oklahoma was the 24th, Utah passed in 2025. Verified independently at the Idaho Legislature (below), with the qualifications noted.
- **First Republican-introduced version in the category's history.** Barrett framed it in his own [press release](https://barrett.house.gov/media/press-releases/barrett-launches-fifth-pillar-blueprint-better-america-reform-campaign-finance) as the fifth pillar of a "Blueprint for a Better America," saying the amendment "guarantees Congress and the states the authority to give the power in elections back to the people," and that "our elections should not be determined by elite megadonors who have never been to Michigan." He simultaneously cosponsored two statutory bills, H.R. 8720 (contribution verification and reporting) and H.R. 8721 (foreign-national political spending).
- **Litigation posture is deliberately neutral.** In *NRSC v. FEC* American Promise filed an [amicus brief in support of neither party](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/24/24-621/373157/20250828181254622_24-621%20ac%20American%20Promise.pdf) — arguing about *who decides* rather than the policy outcome. Clements's [statement](https://americanpromise.net/american-promise-on-nrsc-v-fec-americans-lose-ground-as-courts-retain-control-over-money-in-politics-rules/) on the ruling: "Whatever one thinks of the policy result in this case, the American people lost today because once again, federal judges, not the people or their chosen representatives, have decided the rules for money in our elections." This is a discipline worth noting: it is the same structural framing our own material uses, and it is what makes red-state recruitment possible.

### Verifying the 25-state claim: what Idaho actually passed

Idaho [SJM 109](https://legislature.idaho.gov/sessioninfo/2026/legislation/SJM109) (2026), by the Senate State Affairs Committee. Its operative clause asks Idaho's delegation

> "to actively uphold the principles of federalism and to be proponents of appropriate action, including, if necessary, championing a constitutional amendment that affirms the primary role and the authority of states to govern their election processes and establishes the ability of the states to enact policies regarding campaign finance, election security and transparency, and outside influence in a manner that is consistent with established constitutional principles and that prioritizes state sovereignty."

Three qualifications follow from the primary source, and they matter for how we may cite this number:

1. **It is a Tenth Amendment argument, not a political-equality argument.** The recitals invoke the Tenth Amendment, states as "laboratories of a republic," freedom from "disproportionate federal influence," and "state sovereignty." It asks for *state* authority; it does not ask for Congress's authority to limit spending, and it never uses the equality rationale our Section 2 declares.
2. **It endorses no text and hedges the ask** — "including, *if necessary*, championing a constitutional amendment."
3. **There is no roll call.** The legislative history records "ADOPTED – voice vote" in both chambers. The recorded "Ayes 66 Nays 0" in the House is a vote to *suspend the rules*, not to adopt the memorial. Any public claim of a near-unanimous Idaho vote must be phrased carefully, and our existing memo's wording ("passed with near-unanimous support") should be revisited against this record.

**This does not diminish the achievement** — half the states going on record in any form is the strongest structural signal in the category. It does mean the honest formulation is "25 states have asked Congress to act on money in elections," not "25 states have endorsed this amendment."

### Polling American Promise commissions

[Ipsos, January 9–11 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/few-see-unlimited-campaign-spending-form-free-speech), KnowledgePanel, n=1,036, MoE ±3.2 (Democrats n=330 ±5.6, Republicans n=279 ±6.0, Independents n=297 ±6.0). **Advocacy-commissioned — American Promise is the client** — but released with full methodology by the pollster, which is the better-than-usual case. From the Ipsos release:

- **81% are concerned about the influence of money in American politics — 78% R / 90% D / 82% I.** This is the strongest cross-partisan number in the release and the one worth carrying.
- **76% agree large donors "make it harder for ordinary Americans to have their voice heard" — 76% R / 86% D / 81% I.**
- 15% say spending unlimited amounts on campaigns is First Amendment-protected speech; 50% say it is not; 32% unsure.
- 22% were aware that the Court has so ruled.

⚠️ **Two numbers on American Promise's own [polling page](https://americanpromise.net/january-2026-ipsos-survey/) are framed more favorably than the Ipsos topline supports, and we should not repeat the site's framing.** On the free-speech item, the Ipsos release states that majorities of Democrats (61%) and Independents (53%) say unlimited spending is *not* free speech, but that only "a plurality of Republicans (44%)" agree. On who should set the rules, the release reports a **plurality** — 41% voters and elected officials versus 12% the Supreme Court, with 14% "neither" and 31% unsure. Neither is a majority finding. Cite the 81% concern figure and the 76% large-donor figure; leave the rest.

## 2. Free Speech For People — the Free and Fair Elections Amendment

**Identity.** Founded 2010 in Massachusetts, a 501(c)(3) that combines amendment advocacy with litigation. Its program extends well beyond money in politics: the site's navigation carries "Impeach Trump. Again.," "Abolishing ICE," and a Supreme Court reform campaign. **This is a coalition-coding fact more than a drafting fact, and it is decisive for us** — see Partnership and conflict.

**The text.** FSFP drafted the Free and Fair Elections Amendment, carried as [H.J.Res. 119](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/119/text) (Rep. James P. McGovern, D-MA-2, introduced September 10, 2025, **no cosponsors listed**; predecessor H.J.Res. 78 in the 118th). Eight sections. The load-bearing ones, verbatim:

> "**section 1.** To advance democratic self-government and political equality, and to protect the integrity of government and the electoral process, the right of citizens of the United States to vote in elections in which campaign contributions and spending are subject to enforceable limits as set forth in this article, shall not be abridged by the United States.
>
> "**section 2.** In any calendar year, no person may spend or contribute more than one hundred dollars for the purpose of influencing any other person's election for the office of Representative, Senator, President, or Vice President; nor spend for the purpose of influencing elections, including such person's own election… or contribute to candidates for such offices, their campaigns, or political parties, more than an aggregate total of one thousand dollars; provided that Congress may by law prescribe lower limits and may periodically increase the amounts set forth in this section, but only to the extent required by changes in the value of money.
>
> "**section 3.** No corporation or other entity created by law shall contribute or spend any amount for the purpose of influencing any election… but a political party or candidate's campaign may spend the amounts prescribed by law."

Section 4 commands Congress to legislate within sixty days, including public funding "equaling at least eighty percent of the amount that may be spent." Section 5 withholds congressional salaries until it does. Section 6 creates a direct cause of action. Section 7 preserves state power. Section 8 is the press savings clause.

⚠️ **Section 2 is a direct collision with our memo's incumbent-protection trap note**, which says in terms: "Drafters should resist any urge to constitutionalize dollar figures — frozen numbers are how a protective clause becomes an incumbency machine." FSFP writes $100 and $1,000 into the Constitution and permits Congress to raise them "only to the extent required by changes in the value of money" — that is, an inflation ratchet and nothing more, with Congress free to go *lower* but never to restore real-terms headroom by judgment. A challenger's ability to buy name recognition is capped at a fixed real value forever, by the text, with no judicial safety valve of the kind *Bowman* supplied when it struck the UK's £5 cap as a "total barrier." FSFP itself frames this as the advantage — its text "will establish, upon enactment, key contribution and spending limits" rather than leaving them to politics. That is a coherent theory. It is the opposite of our memo's strategic read, which is to constitutionalize authority and purpose, not policy.

FSFP also endorses the Democracy For All and We the People amendments, while stating its own is "preferable."

## 3. Move to Amend — the We the People Amendment

**The text.** [H.J.Res. 54](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/54/text) (Rep. Pramila Jayapal, D-WA-7, introduced February 12, 2025, 28 original cosponsors; classified by congress.gov under "Civil Rights and Liberties," not "Government Operations and Politics" — a tell about what it actually does). Verbatim, Section 1:

> "The rights and privileges protected and extended by the Constitution of the United States are the rights and privileges of natural persons only. An artificial entity, such as a corporation, limited liability company, or other entity, established by the laws of any State, the United States, or any foreign state shall have no rights under the Constitution and are subject to regulation by the People, through Federal, State, or local law."

Section 2 commands that governments "shall regulate, limit, or prohibit contributions and expenditures," requires disclosure of all permissible contributions and expenditures, and provides that "The judiciary shall not construe the spending of money to influence elections to be speech under the First Amendment." Section 3 is a press savings clause.

⚠️ **This is our memo's corporate-personhood trap in its purest form**, and the memo already rejected it on the merits: text stripping *all* constitutional rights from *all* artificial entities reaches nonprofits, churches, unions, and every incorporated newspaper, and removes due-process and takings protection from businesses. Note also that Section 3 protects only "the freedom of the press" — while Section 1 has just declared that the corporation publishing the newspaper has no constitutional rights at all. **We should not adopt any part of this text.** Its one genuinely useful contribution is Section 2's disclosure mandate, and our Section 3 already does that job better by framing disclosure as a right of the people and keeping thresholds statutory.

## 4. Wolf-PAC (now "Wolf Pack") — the Article V route

Wolf-PAC has rebranded and now operates at [fightcorruption.us](https://fightcorruption.us/); the old wolf-pac.com resolution page returns 404. **It publishes no amendment text** — its petition asks support for "a 28th Amendment to end corruption" in the abstract. Its stated strategy is "focusing on organizing and building a movement to achieve a campaign finance reform amendment, as well as advocating for an Article V convention."

The Article V convention route is the structural fault line in this category. Our existing memo already records that Wolf-PAC won five state applications in 2014–2016 and none since, and that New Jersey and Illinois later adopted blanket rescissions of all prior Article V applications over runaway-convention concerns on both left and right. **Nothing found in this pass changes that assessment.** Wolf Pack's own ally page lists American Promise, RepresentUs, Stamp Stampede, and Take Back Our Republic — so the movement is not formally split, but the routes are incompatible in practice: convention applications can be clawed back, congressional memorials cannot.

## 5. Issue One — cross-partisan, and deliberately not an amendment org

Issue One's [money-in-politics program](https://issueone.org/issues/money-in-politics/) contains **no constitutional amendment at all**. Its work is statutory and administrative: disclosure, dark money and super PACs, FEC repair, leadership PACs, bundler disclosure, and megadonor research. Named wins include the 2018 Senate Campaign Disclosure Parity Act. Its "Outsized Influence" report found 12 megadonors responsible for $1 of every $13 in federal elections since *Citizens United*.

**Its structure is the most genuinely cross-partisan in the category and is worth studying as a model.** Its ReFormers Caucus filed an amicus in the First Circuit on October 29, 2025 signed by two dozen former elected officials — **exactly 12 Democrats and 12 Republicans**. The brief pairs both parties' abuses in a single sentence, citing money flowing through super PACs to benefit Sen. Robert Menendez (D-NJ), Ohio Speaker Larry Householder (R-OH), and Puerto Rico Gov. Wanda Vázquez Garced. That is the same discipline our own mission requires, executed by a Washington reform group at scale.

Live litigation worth tracking: *Dinner Table Action v. Schneider*, argued in the First Circuit July 29, 2026, challenging the Maine ballot measure passed by **75% of Maine voters** in November 2024 capping super PAC contributions at $5,000/year. Issue One reports companion efforts in Hawaii and Montana. An Issue One poll released in fall 2025 found 79% said large independent expenditures by wealthy donors and corporations give rise to corruption or its appearance. *(Party breakdown for that 79% was not published on the page fetched; do not cite it without one.)*

## 6. People For the American Way, and the Democracy For All lineage

PFAW drafts no text of its own; it is an endorser within the Democracy For All coalition. The lineage matters more than the org. The Democracy For All chassis — the descendant of S.J.Res. 19, the one text in this category ever to receive a Senate floor vote — is now carried as [H.J.Res. 122](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/122/text) (Rep. Joe Neguse, D-CO-2, introduced September 17, 2025, with 35 original cosponsors, all Democrats). Verbatim:

> "**section 1.** Congress and the States may regulate and impose reasonable viewpoint-neutral limitations on the raising and spending of money by candidates and others to influence elections.
>
> "**section 2.** Congress and the States may regulate and enact systems of public campaign financing, including those designed to restrict the influence of private wealth by offsetting the raising and spending of money by candidates and others to influence elections with increased public funding.
>
> "**section 3.** Congress and the States shall have power to implement and enforce this article by appropriate legislation, and may distinguish between natural persons and corporations or other artificial entities created by law, including by prohibiting such entities from spending money to influence elections.
>
> "**section 4.** Nothing in this article shall be construed to grant Congress or the States the power to abridge the freedom of the press."

**H.J.Res. 122 Section 1 is the closest single sentence in Congress to our Section 1**, including the words "reasonable" and "viewpoint-neutral" that our memo specifically defends as the answer to the ACLU objection. Its Section 2 constitutionalizes a *public financing* power, which our memo deliberately held back as the roughly 50%-support weak remedy. Its Section 4 press clause is the bare 2014 formulation our Section 6 was written to improve on.

## Language comparison — our Article 1 against the For Our Freedom Amendment

Section-by-section against [H.J.Res. 191](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/191/text) (Version A), the strongest adoption candidate.

| Our draft | What H.J.Res. 191 does with it |
|---|---|
| **§1** — power to "regulate and set reasonable, viewpoint-neutral limits on the raising and spending of money to influence elections, including contributions to candidates and expenditures made in their support or opposition" | **Covered, differently.** §2 covers contributions and spending in campaigns, elections, and State or local ballot initiatives. **Two of our words are missing.** It has no "viewpoint-neutral" — the express answer to the ACLU objection. And it never names *independent expenditures* "in their support or opposition," which is the phrase our memo says "closes the *SpeechNow* super-PAC channel." "Spending in campaigns for elections" is likely broad enough, but ours says it and theirs infers it. |
| **§2** — political equality and independence from concentrated wealth "hereby declared to be compelling interests" | **Weaker.** Their §1 recites that "We the People have compelling sovereign interests in… the political equality of natural persons" — but as a recital of pre-existing interests, not an operative declaration, and with no "in furtherance of which laws may be enacted under this article" tether. Ours is drafted in operative register on the model of the 21st Amendment's "is hereby repealed." Theirs also bundles "freedom of speech" and "federalism" into the same list of compelling interests, which cuts both ways in court. |
| **§3** — the people's right to know the source of substantial contributions and expenditures | **Absent entirely.** H.J.Res. 191 has no disclosure provision. This is the 85% R / 88% D provision — the highest-polling item in the category and the one red-state legislatures pass most readily. Adopting their text 100% means dropping it. |
| **§4** — power to distinguish natural persons from artificial entities in election spending | **Covered, and stronger in one respect.** Their §2 permits "distinguishing between natural persons and artificial entities and prohibiting artificial entities from raising and spending money" — the prohibition is express where ours is implied by the power to distinguish. Ours is scoped to "the regulation of contributions and expenditures to influence elections"; theirs is scoped by the same election list. Both avoid the corporate-personhood trap. Note the Version A / Version B divergence: only in Version A is this power governed by "reasonably." |
| **§5** — power to exclude foreign governments, foreign nationals, and entities they own or control, including on ballot measures | **Absent entirely.** No foreign-money provision. This is the category's highest bipartisan floor per our memo — PPC 2021 at 77% R / 84% D, Maine Question 2 at 86%, H.R. 3535 by unanimous House voice vote. Adopting their text 100% means dropping it. (Barrett himself cosponsored a *statutory* foreign-money bill, H.R. 8721, on the same day — evidence that he sees this as legislation's job, not the amendment's.) |
| **§6** — press and speech savings clause protecting "news reporting, commentary, and editorial content, regardless of the speaker's form of organization"; no revival of laws held invalid before ratification; enforcement power | **Half-covered.** Their §3 is enforcement only. **There is no press savings clause and no speech savings clause anywhere in H.J.Res. 191**, and no anti-revival clause. Their §1 lists "the freedom of speech" among compelling interests, which is a recital, not a savings clause. Our memo calls a press clause "Non-optional for ratification politics." |

| | |
|---|---|
| **We would gain** | A Republican House sponsor and the only cross-partisan sponsorship in the category. A named ally with 25 state memorials, state chapters, a candidate pledge program, and a research shop. Brevity — three sections against our six, and the memo's own note that the adversary's first attack on S.J.Res. 19 was that it was "over four times longer than the First Amendment it seeks to amend." | **We would need to add back** | At minimum §3, §5, and §6. That is half our article, and adding them back means we are no longer adopting their text — we are adopting their §§1–2 as a replacement for our §§1, 2 and 4, and keeping the rest. |

**Reading:** our draft is the only one of the four that clears every trap its own memo identified. The For Our Freedom Amendment is the second-best and fails on one — the press clause — plus two absences (disclosure, foreign money) that are omissions rather than traps.

## Adversaries

### Institute for Free Speech

Founded 2005 as the **Center for Competitive Politics**, renamed Institute for Free Speech; a 501(c)(3) at 1150 Connecticut Avenue NW, Washington DC. Founder and Chairman **Bradley A. Smith is a former Chairman of the Federal Election Commission** — the single most credentialed opponent in the category. It litigates rather than lobbies, and it is winning: it co-authored amicus briefs in *NRSC v. FEC* (decided June 30, 2026), where the Court struck limits on coordinated party expenditures and **overruled *Colorado II*** (2001). Smith's framing of the trajectory: "Ever since the 2007 decision in *Wisconsin Right to Life v. FEC*, the Court has been slowly but steadily returning campaign-finance law to its First Amendment roots. Today's decision is a logical next step in that project."

**Their standing brief against a text like ours.** David Keating's [June 3, 2014 statement](https://www.ifs.org/expert-analysis/statement-of-david-keating-to-the-senate-judiciary-committee-on-the-constitutional-amendment-proposal/) to the Senate Judiciary Committee on S.J.Res. 19 remains the most complete opposition document, and it is aimed with unusual precision at the components our draft carries:

- **On length:** "The constitutional amendment being considered by the Committee today is over four times longer than the First Amendment it seeks to amend." (45 words against 200.) Our Article 1 is longer than S.J.Res. 19 was. This attack lands harder on us than on American Promise.
- **On the purpose clause:** "This section begins with a constitutional rarity: a preamble," and a footnote arguing that the only comparable preamble is the Second Amendment's, and that the *Heller* litigation "ought to be sufficient to discourage Sen. Udall's style of constitutional draftsmanship." Our §2 is a purpose declaration — this is aimed at us.
- **On the equality rationale:** that replacing the quid-pro-quo corruption standard with "integrity" or "political equality" "invites Congress to discriminate amongst entities that have differing levels of 'influence,'" citing *Arizona Free Enterprise* and *Buckley* for the proposition that equalization "might serve not to equalize the opportunities of all candidates, but to handicap a candidate who lack[s] substantial name recognition."
- **On the press clause:** that its "scope is unclear," that the Court has "consistently rejected the proposition that the institutional press has any constitutional privilege beyond that of other speakers," that the FEC's media exemption is circular — "in order to qualify as a media entity, a speaker must first be a media entity" — and that Justice Stevens, asked what counted as press under his own proposed amendment, could not answer. **Our §6's functional framing — protecting news reporting, commentary and editorial content "regardless of the speaker's form of organization" — is a direct answer to this specific objection**, and is better drafted for it than any competitor text. It is worth recognizing that we drafted the reply before reading the brief.
- **On incumbency:** quoting Cato's Trevor Burrus, "[g]iving elected representatives the power to regulate the process by which they get elected is a terrifying proposition."
- **On bipartisan opposition:** the statement's most effective move is to cite critics from the *pro-regulation* side — Obama White House Counsel Bob Bauer, and the Campaign Legal Center's Paul S. Ryan saying "it's entirely impossible to predict the impact of this amendment, even if ratified."

The ACLU's own opposition to S.J.Res. 19, already recorded in our memo, is the companion problem from the left. Together they are why "viewpoint-neutral" is in our §1 and why the press clause is written functionally.

## Partnership and conflict

- **American Promise is the only org in this category we could associate with without coding ourselves.** It is cross-partisan in substance and not just in branding: a Republican House sponsor, red-state memorials, an amicus supporting neither party, and a *who decides* frame rather than a *which side wins* frame. It is also the only one whose method — recruiting in-state legislator champions — is the method our own sequencing note recommends.
- **Free Speech For People cannot be a coalition partner for us on any terms, and the reason is not its amendment.** Its site runs "Impeach Trump. Again." and "Abolishing ICE" from the same navigation bar as its money-in-politics work. Association would code us instantly and irreversibly, against the mission's first constraint. Its amendment text can be cited as a drafting comparison; the organization cannot be a partner.
- **Move to Amend is a drafting adversary within the reform movement.** Its text is the one our memo says "converts a 79%-consensus issue into a radical restructuring fight." We should be able to say plainly, without disparaging it, that we chose the enabling model over the rights-stripping model and why.
- **Wolf Pack's Article V route is the movement's live internal conflict.** Endorsing a convention application would put us against the majority of the category's orgs and would import the runaway-convention objection from both left and right. Our memo's position — congressional memorials travel, convention applications get rescinded — holds.
- **Issue One is the best structural model and the safest citation.** No amendment to conflict with, a 12 D / 12 R caucus, and research we can cite without an advocacy discount. Its bipartisan-pairing discipline in the ReFormers amicus is worth emulating directly.
- **Take Back Our Republic** is the conservative reform org that would balance a coalition, but [takebackaction.org](https://www.takebackaction.org/) did not resolve in DNS this session. Its status needs checking before it is named anywhere public.

## Recommendation

**Do not adopt the For Our Freedom Amendment as a wholesale replacement. Adopt from it selectively, and say so publicly.**

The founder's instruction is to adopt established language as close to 100% as the evidence supports. Here the evidence supports adoption of the *strategy* and part of the *language*, but not the whole text — because H.J.Res. 191 is silent on three provisions our own research identifies as the highest-polling and most ratification-critical in the category (disclosure at 85 R / 88 D; foreign money at 77 R / 84 D; the press clause our memo calls non-optional). Adopting it 100% would mean dropping the best-polling clause we have in exchange for brevity.

Two specific borrowings are worth making, and one question is worth putting to the founder:

1. **Borrow "State or local ballot initiatives or referendums"** from their §2 in place of our §5's "including elections on ballot measures," and consider extending the ballot-measure coverage to §1. Their formulation is more precise about which ballot measures, and the precision is a federalism reassurance.
2. **Consider borrowing the express prohibition power** — "and prohibiting artificial entities from raising and spending money" — into our §4. Ours grants the power to distinguish and leaves the prohibition implied; theirs says it. Our memo's scoping analysis supports the express version, since the point was always to restore *Austin* as a permissible legislative choice.
3. **Put the length question to the founder.** The adversary's opening attack is length, our article is longer than the one that attack was written against, and brevity is a real ratification asset. The trade is that every clause we would cut is a clause our own research says is load-bearing.

**On the alliance, separately from the text:** American Promise is worth approaching regardless of the drafting decision, and the approach is stronger if we are candid that our draft adds a disclosure right, a foreign-money power, and a press clause to their chassis — all three of which are additions in the direction of their own cross-partisan strategy, not against it.
