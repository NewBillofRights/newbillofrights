# Term Limits Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

This document supports **Target Category 2 (Term Limits — for both Supreme Court / Federal Judiciary and Congress)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md) and is a companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). Term limits are the foundation's strongest full-spectrum candidate: congressional term limits poll at [87% (90% R / 86% D — Pew, 2023)](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/congress-and-the-presidency/) — the smallest partisan gap of any structural reform we have measured — and Supreme Court fixed terms poll at [79% (73% R / 87% D — Marquette Law School, 2026)](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/). Uniquely among our categories, term limits also come with a complete legal history: the Supreme Court has already ruled that only an Article V amendment can do this for Congress ([*U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/779/)), and the country has already passed a term-limits amendment once ([the 22nd, ratified 1951](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/amendments/amendment-xxii)). The task is not inventing the idea; it is drafting it to survive the traps that killed it in 1995–97 and that hollow it out in the states.

**The headline findings:**

1. **The amendment route is legally mandatory for Congress and probably for the Court.** *Thornton* (5–4) closed the state-law path for congressional limits, and the [Congressional Research Service reports that "most commentators agree" Congress cannot impose Supreme Court term limits by statute](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12343) — despite serious contrary scholarship. An amendment moots the fight.
2. **This has almost passed before.** The House voted 227–204 for a term-limits amendment on March 29, 1995 (63 short of two-thirds) and [217–211 on February 12, 1997](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/199721) — the closest any modern structural amendment has come to proposal.
3. **The public has already picked the numbers.** In the [University of Maryland Program for Public Consultation's deliberative survey (2023)](https://publicconsultation.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Term-Limits-2023-Report-V2.pdf), 83% backed an amendment (86% R / 80% D) after hearing both sides' arguments, and majorities converged on roughly **12 years per chamber** (60% chose ≤4 House terms; 63% chose ≤2 Senate terms).
4. **The US Supreme Court is a global outlier.** [No other major democracy gives life tenure with no age limit to its constitutional-court judges](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/life-tenure-us-supreme-court-justices-global-oddity-clear-costs) — Germany (12 years), France (9), Italy (9), South Africa (12/age 70), UK (retire at 70/75), Canada (retire at 75). The "fixed 18-year terms" framing is both internationally standard and the wording that wins Republicans (Marquette finds "fixed terms" outpolls "term limits" on the right).
5. **The strongest counterevidence is real and must be designed around.** Two decades of political-science research on California and Michigan finds legislative term limits [shift power to lobbyists, staff, and the executive branch](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/state-politics-and-policy-quarterly/article/abs/on-the-outside-looking-in-lobbyists-perspectives-on-the-effects-of-state-legislative-term-limits/E2F38BFA10481E5C44B3CDF153A34B68) and drain institutional expertise. The lesson is longer limits (12 years, not 6–8), not no limits.
6. **Term limits die by evasion, not repeal.** The comparative literature ([Versteeg et al., *Columbia Law Review* 2020](https://www.columbialawreview.org/content/the-law-and-politics-of-presidential-term-limit-evasion/); [Ginsburg, Melton & Elkins 2011](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/wmlr/vol52/iss6/3/)) shows roughly a third of term-limited executives worldwide attempt to stay past their limit, usually via "legal" amendment or friendly-court reinterpretation. An American amendment needs an anti-evasion clause, not just a limit.

## Amendment Framework — What a Term Limits Amendment Might Cover

### Core provisions

A full-spectrum term-limits amendment plausibly bundles two (possibly three) elements:

1. **Congressional limits.** Cap lifetime service at **two Senate terms (12 years) and six House terms (12 years)** — or adopt the current legislative vehicle's tighter 2-Senate/3-House formula. The deliberative-polling evidence points to 12/12: the [UMD PPC survey](https://publicconsultation.org/united-states/congressional-term-limits/) found majorities in both parties choosing ~4 House terms and 2 Senate terms once trade-offs were explained, and the 12-year symmetry ("twelve years in either chamber") is easy to communicate. Longer caps also directly answer the expertise-drain literature.
2. **Judicial fixed terms.** A **single, nonrenewable 18-year term for Supreme Court justices**, staggered so a seat opens every two years — one appointment per Congress, two per presidential term. This is the design analyzed at length by the [Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court (2021)](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/pcscotus/final-report/), which found "considerable, bipartisan support" for it, and it mirrors the Khanna–Beyer and Whitehouse–Booker bills. Optionally extend a service cap or retirement age to all Article III judges.
3. **Anti-evasion machinery.** No change to the limits may benefit sitting officeholders; limits apply prospectively from ratification. This is the single clearest lesson of the international literature (see Abstraction Lessons below).

### Challenges in drafting and adoption

- **The self-interest problem.** An amendment proposed by Congress asks members to limit themselves. The 1995 vote showed exactly how this fails: members voted for the *least* restrictive version, and rival versions split the pro-limits coalition (four competing proposals were floored on March 29, 1995, so members could tell constituents they voted for "a" term limit while none passed). Grandfathering sitting members — as the 22nd Amendment grandfathered Truman and as every serious congressional proposal since has done — is the price of the necessary two-thirds.
- **The convention wildcard.** [U.S. Term Limits](https://termlimits.com/progress/) *(advocacy source — verify all counts independently)* claims **14 states** have passed its single-subject Article V application (FL, AL, MO, WV, OK, WI, TN, LA, NC, SD, IN, SC, KS, AZ as of 2025, with [Arizona passing in February 2025](https://azcapitoltimes.com/news/2025/02/20/house-passes-article-v-measure-to-limit-congressional-terms/) and [Indiana advancing the same month](https://indianacapitalchronicle.com/2025/02/27/indiana-call-to-amend-congressional-term-limits-into-constitution-gains-ground/)); 34 are needed. The campaign's real function so far is leverage — a credible convention threat is what historically moves Congress to propose amendments itself (the 17th Amendment pattern).
- **Numbers fights fracture coalitions.** 6 vs. 12 House years split Republicans in 1995 (U.S. Term Limits famously scored the 12-year version as a betrayal). The drafting choice must be made once, early, and defended with the deliberative-polling data.
- **The judicial-term add-on changes the partisan valence.** Congressional limits poll evenly; SCOTUS terms currently poll *better with Democrats* (87% D / 73% R, Marquette). Bundling them makes the package genuinely cross-cutting — each wing gets its priority — but wording matters: **"fixed 18-year terms" rather than "term limits for justices"** preserves Republican support.

### Strategic read

Term limits are the foundation's ice-breaker amendment: maximal name recognition, minimal partisan coding, an existing 87% floor, and a live state-legislature campaign to draft behind. The credible criticisms (lobbyist empowerment, lost expertise) argue for *our* version — longer 12-year congressional caps plus long 18-year judicial terms — against the 6-year populist version, which gives the foundation a distinctive, defensible position rather than a me-too endorsement. The realistic near-term goal is not ratification; it is making a specific, well-drafted text the default answer to a question 87% of Americans already say yes to.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

**The doctrinal wall.** In [*U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/779/), the Court (5–4, Stevens writing) struck down Arkansas's Amendment 73 and with it the congressional term limits adopted in [23 states](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/96-152.html), holding that the Qualifications Clauses fix an exclusive list that neither states nor Congress may supplement — changing it "must come not by legislation adopted either by Congress or by an individual State, but rather... through the amendment procedures set forth in Article V." Justice Thomas's dissent (joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, and Scalia) argued the Constitution is [simply silent, leaving the power with the people of each state](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-1456.ZD.html). Practical upshot: the reserved-powers path is dead; Article V is the only road, and the case's 5–4 closeness is itself a talking point with conservatives who agree with Thomas.

**The successful precedent — the 22nd Amendment.** After FDR broke the two-term tradition in 1940 and 1944, the Republican 80th Congress proposed [H.J.Res. 27 on March 21, 1947](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt22-1/ALDE_00001008/), largely on party lines; ratification took nearly four years, completing [February 27, 1951 with 36 of 48 states](https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/running-for-office/larger-image.php?image=44.1). Design details that mattered: it limits *election* ("No person shall be elected... more than twice"), handles partial terms (the 10-year rule), and **grandfathered the sitting incumbent** (Truman). It proves a term-limits amendment can be proposed and ratified in the modern era — and that grandfathering is how you buy the votes.

### Prior Amendment Proposals — the 1995–97 floor fights

- **March 29, 1995 (104th Congress).** The Contract with America's term-limits plank reached the floor as multiple competing versions. The most permissive — 6 House terms / 2 Senate terms, prospective only — got the top vote: [**227–204**, 63 votes short of the required 290](https://www.baltimoresun.com/news/bs-xpm-1995-03-30-1995089005-story.html). Republicans split 189–40 in favor; Democrats 38–163 against. Stricter versions (including a 3-term House limit and a version letting states set tighter limits) all drew fewer votes. It was the Contract's first major floor defeat — and remains [the closest Congress has ever come](https://termlimits.com/frequently-asked-questions/) *(advocacy source)* to proposing term limits.
- **1996 Senate.** The companion ([S.J.Res. 21, S. Rept. 104-158](https://www.congress.gov/committee-report/104th-congress/senate-report/158/1)) died when a cloture motion failed in April 1996 — the Senate has never held an up-or-down vote.
- **February 12, 1997 (105th Congress).** [H.J.Res. 2](https://www.congress.gov/index.php/bill/105th-congress/house-joint-resolution/2) (2 Senate / 6 House terms) failed [**217–211**](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/199721) — a majority, but 69 short of two-thirds. Rival state-option substitutes again splintered supporters ([H. Rept. 105-2](https://www.congress.gov/committee-report/105th-congress/house-report/2/1)).

**Current congressional vehicle.** The Cruz–Norman amendment — [S.J.Res. 1 / H.J.Res. 12 (119th Congress)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/12): **two Senate terms, three House terms, prospective only** (terms before ratification don't count) — has been reintroduced each Congress since 2017 ([Cruz press release](https://www.cruz.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/sen-cruz-rep-norman-colleagues-introduce-constitutional-amendment-to-impose-term-limits-for-congress)). Its predecessor [H.J.Res. 11 (118th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-joint-resolution/11/all-info) got a rare House Judiciary markup in 2024 and was [killed in committee when four Republicans joined Democrats against it](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/will-congress-ever-have-term-limits-four-republicans-sided-democrats-kill-latest-proposal.amp) — a data point on how thin elite support is beneath the 87% public number.

**SCOTUS term-limit vehicles — the statutory-vs-amendment fight.**

- **House:** the [Khanna–Beyer Supreme Court Term Limits and Regular Appointments Act](https://khanna.house.gov/media/press-releases/reps-khanna-and-beyer-reintroduce-legislation-set-supreme-court-term-limits) — 18-year terms, appointments every odd year (two per presidential term), current justices exempt, term-expired justices rotate to "senior" duty.
- **Senate:** the [Whitehouse–Booker–Blumenthal–Padilla Supreme Court Biennial Appointments and Term Limits Act (S. 3096, 118th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/3096/text), [reintroduced in July 2026](https://www.whitehouse.senate.gov/news/release/whitehouse-booker-blumenthal-padilla-reintroduce-supreme-court-term-limits-bill-to-restore-americans-trust-in-the-court/) — same architecture: a new justice every two years; after 18 years a justice hears only the Court's small original-jurisdiction docket.
- **The constitutionality question:** Both bills are statutes, defended on the theory that a term-limited justice retains the *office* (title, salary, life tenure in the judicial branch) and merely rotates duties — analogous to the long-accepted **senior status** system. The [Brennan Center concludes statutory limits are constitutional](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/supreme-court-term-limits-are-constitutional); [Fix the Court agrees](https://fixthecourt.com/2021/12/supreme-court-term-limits-statute-constitutional-yes/) *(advocacy source)*. But [CRS reports most commentators believe an amendment is required](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12343) given Article III's "good Behaviour" clause and the "one supreme Court" objection (rotating panels arguably create two tiers of justices), and the [Biden Commission's final report (Dec. 2021)](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/pcscotus/final-report/) — which took no position on any reform — was [explicitly divided on the statute-vs-amendment question even while noting "considerable, bipartisan support" for 18-year terms](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/courts_law/supreme-court-commission-term-limits/2021/12/07/eb0ef982-5767-11ec-9a18-a506cf3aa31d_story.html). **The foundation's position writes itself:** any statute would be reviewed by the very justices it limits; an amendment ends the argument. This is the foundation's comparative advantage over Fix the Court/Brennan-style statutory advocacy.

## State Models

**Legislative term limits — the surviving laboratory.** After the 1990–94 initiative wave (14 states in 1992, 8 more in 1994, [average initiative support over 64%](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-104srpt158/html/CRPT-104srpt158.htm)), *Thornton* voided the congressional limits but left state-legislative limits standing. Today [**16 states** limit their legislators](https://www.kttc.com/2026/02/19/16-states-have-term-limits-their-legislatures-minnesota-does-not/): Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Florida, Louisiana, Maine, Michigan, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Dakota. Six are **lifetime** limits (CA, MI, MO, NV, ND, OK); the rest are **consecutive** (sit out, then return). Caps range from 6 to 12 years per chamber. Six states repealed or judicially voided limits (ID, MA, OR, UT, WA, WY) — always via legislature or courts, never by popular vote. [North Dakota is the newest (2022, ~65% approval, 8-year caps)](https://ballotpedia.org/North_Dakota_State_Legislative_Term_Limits_Amendment_(2026)) — and its legislature has already [referred a 2026 measure to loosen the limits](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/04/07/north-dakota-legislature-refers-amendment-to-2026-ballot-amending-term-limits-for-state-legislators-approved-by-voters-in-2022/), a live domestic example of incumbent evasion pressure.

**What the political science found — the strongest counterarguments.** This literature is the honest opposition brief and must be engaged, not dodged:

- **Lobbyist and staff empowerment.** [PPIC's California studies](https://www.ppic.org/wp-content/uploads/content/pubs/report/R_1104BCR.pdf) found term-limited members relied heavily on lobbyists and staff for bill drafting and expertise; [Sarbaugh-Thompson et al.'s 12-year Wayne State study of Michigan](https://www.canr.msu.edu/michiganpolicyguide/a-changing-political-environment) found limits dissolved checks, empowered career lobbyists and unelected staff, and eroded cross-party relationships. [Surveys of lobbyists themselves](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/state-politics-and-policy-quarterly/article/abs/on-the-outside-looking-in-lobbyists-perspectives-on-the-effects-of-state-legislative-term-limits/E2F38BFA10481E5C44B3CDF153A34B68) report power shifting from the legislature to the governor, agencies, and interest groups.
- **The dose-response point (our design answer):** the worst findings cluster in **6–8-year states** (CA pre-2012, MI pre-2022). Both California (Prop 28, 2012) and Michigan (Prop 1, 2022) responded not by repealing limits but by **consolidating to 12 years in one chamber** — converging, from experience, on exactly the 12-year figure the UMD deliberative survey produced. That convergence is the foundation's core rebuttal.

**Governors.** [36–37 states limit their governors](https://ballotpedia.org/States_with_gubernatorial_term_limits) (the count varies with how informal arrangements are treated); the dominant model is **two consecutive 4-year terms**, with 9 states imposing lifetime limits ([U.S. Term Limits tracker](https://termlimits.com/governor_termlimits/) *(advocacy source)*). Executive term limits are wholly uncontroversial in American state practice — Virginia even limits governors to a single consecutive term.

**State judiciaries.** [33 states set maximum ages for judges](https://ballotpedia.org/Mandatory_retirement) — 70 is the most common, others use 72–75 (Vermont, an outlier, 90). Per the [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/life-tenure-rarity-state-supreme-courts), **339 of 344 state supreme court justices** serve limited terms, face mandatory retirement, or both; only Rhode Island's five justices hold true life tenure. Life tenure without limit is thus an anomaly even *within* the United States — a fact that reframes SCOTUS terms as normalization, not radicalism.

## International Models

### Germany — Federal Constitutional Court

Justices of the Bundesverfassungsgericht serve a [single, nonrenewable **12-year** term, with mandatory retirement at 68](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Federal-Constitutional-Court), elected by two-thirds supermajorities of the Bundestag or Bundesrat — a rule that forces cross-party consensus candidates. Crucially for our anti-evasion analysis: in **December 2024**, Germany amended the Basic Law to move the court's structure — including the 12-year term, the 16-judge bench, and retirement age — from ordinary statute *into the constitution itself*, precisely so a bare parliamentary majority could not "capture the court by simple legislation" ([ConstitutionNet, "Resilience lite"](https://constitutionnet.org/news/voices/resilience-lite-german-federal-constitutional-court); [Library of Congress FALQs](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2026/02/falqs-the-german-federal-constitutional-court-turns-75/)). Germany looked at the American court fight and *entrenched*.

### France — Conseil constitutionnel

Nine members serve [**nine-year, nonrenewable** terms, one-third renewed every three years](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr/en/council-organisation) — the cleanest working model of staggered replacement (each of three appointing authorities names one member per triennium). The only exception to nonrenewability is a member appointed to finish a deceased predecessor's term, who may then serve one full term of their own — a ready-made answer to the early-vacancy drafting problem.

### Italy — Corte costituzionale

Fifteen judges serve [**nine years** "beginning in each case from the day of their swearing in," and "cannot be re-appointed"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Italy) (Constitution art. 135). Italy is also a cautionary tale: the original 12-year term was cut to 9 by constitutional amendment in 1967, and because terms run individually from each swearing-in rather than on a fixed calendar, appointment delays have produced gaps — an argument for calendar-fixed seats rather than officeholder-fixed terms.

### South Africa — Constitutional Court

Section 176 of the 1996 Constitution: a Constitutional Court judge "holds office for a **non-renewable term of 12 years, or until he or she attains the age of 70**, whichever occurs first" (extendable by Act of Parliament — later legislation lets some serve to 75 or 15 years total). A dual limit — term *and* age — from the democracy whose court is most often compared to the US model ([FJC, Judiciaries Worldwide](https://judiciariesworldwide.fjc.gov/judicial-tenure)).

### United Kingdom — Supreme Court

No fixed term, but mandatory retirement: 70 for judges appointed after 1995, [raised to **75** by the Public Service Pensions and Judicial Offices Act 2022](https://judiciariesworldwide.fjc.gov/judicial-tenure). The UK abandoned unbounded life tenure without controversy — as did Australia (1977 referendum, age 70) and New Zealand.

### Canada — Supreme Court

Constitution Act, 1867, s. 99(2): superior-court judges hold office during good behaviour "until the age of **seventy-five years**." Canada thus proves the exact textual point: a "good behaviour" clause and a mandatory retirement age coexist in the same sentence — directly rebutting the claim that "good Behaviour" *necessarily* means unbounded life tenure ([Brennan Center comparative analysis](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/life-tenure-us-supreme-court-justices-global-oddity-clear-costs)).

### Mexico — the no-reelection experiment and its reversal

"*Sufragio efectivo, no reelección*" — the [rallying cry of the 1910 Revolution against Porfirio Díaz](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/effective-suffrage-no-reelection) — became a constitutional absolute in 1917: no reelection to *any* office, ever. The result over 80 years: a rotating cast of single-term legislators wholly dependent on party bosses for their next job, i.e., the expertise-drain and party-machine pathologies at maximum dose. The [2014 political reform](https://fairvote.org/mexico_s_election_policy_revolution/) partially reversed course, allowing consecutive legislative reelection (up to 12 years) beginning with the [2021 elections](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0185-16162023000200125), while keeping the president's absolute single six-year term. **Lesson: the tightest possible limit is the worst-performing limit; Mexico landed, from the opposite direction, on the same ~12-year legislative figure.**

### The evasion file — Venezuela, Russia, and the courts that said yes

- [Versteeg, Horley, Meng, Guim & Guirguis, "The Law and Politics of Presidential Term Limit Evasion," 120 *Colum. L. Rev.* 173 (2020)](https://www.columbialawreview.org/content/the-law-and-politics-of-presidential-term-limit-evasion/): surveying 234 incumbents in 106 countries since 2000, **about one-third of presidents who reached their limit tried to stay** — rising to about half outside consolidated democracies — and virtually all did so through *legal forms*: amendment, friendly-court reinterpretation, a puppet successor, or delayed elections.
- [Ginsburg, Melton & Elkins, "On the Evasion of Executive Term Limits" (2011)](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/wmlr/vol52/iss6/3/): of 89 documented overstay attempts, 71 succeeded, 56 of them via formal amendment or constitutional replacement.
- **Venezuela (2009):** Chávez abolished term limits by referendum after a first attempt failed; the captured Supreme Tribunal raised no obstacle. **Russia (2020):** the constitutional amendments "zeroed" Putin's prior terms, and the [Constitutional Court blessed the maneuver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Term_limits_in_Russia). **Honduras (2015)** went further — the Supreme Court struck down the term limit *itself* as a rights violation. **Bolivia (2017)** declared reelection a human right.
- The successful defense: **Colombia (2010)**, where the Constitutional Court invoked the *substitution of the constitution* doctrine (Judgment C-141/10) to block a referendum enabling Uribe's third term — and the [Venice Commission's 2018 term-limits report](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD(2018)010-e) treats limits on evasion, including eternity-clause-style entrenchment, as the core design problem. Scholars now ask directly [whether unamendable term limits can prevent presidents-for-life](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13510347.2025.2534695).
- For scale: presidential term limits appear in the large majority of the world's presidential constitutions — [over 75% of those in force by 2006, and 87 of 92 presidential and semi-presidential systems studied 1992–2006](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13510347.2021.1997991). Term limits on executives are as close to a global constitutional default as anything in comparative law.

**The US-as-outlier point, stated precisely:** the [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/life-tenure-us-supreme-court-justices-global-oddity-clear-costs) — "Today no other major democracy gives lifetime seats to judges who sit on their constitutional courts," common-law (Ireland, New Zealand, South Africa) and civil-law (France, Germany, Spain) alike, and every democracy that once had unbounded tenure (Australia, Canada, UK) has abandoned it. The [Federal Judicial Center's comparative survey](https://judiciariesworldwide.fjc.gov/judicial-tenure) confirms constitutional-court judges worldwide typically serve a single 7–12-year term or face a retirement age of 70–75. Average SCOTUS tenure since 1993: ~28 years — [more than double peer countries](https://foreignpolicy.com/2022/05/04/supreme-court-international/). *Cite the FJC (a US-courts institution) for this in public materials, not only Brennan, to keep the sourcing cross-partisan.*

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Limits must live in the constitution, not statute.** Germany entrenched its court's 12-year terms into the Basic Law in 2024 explicitly to put them beyond a simple majority; Honduras and Bolivia show what friendly courts do to sub-constitutional (or even constitutional) limits. Maps onto the US statutory-vs-amendment fight: a statute reviewed by the limited justices is structurally fragile.
2. **Nonrenewability is the independence mechanism.** Germany, France, Italy, and South Africa all bar reappointment, so no judge ever rules with an eye toward the appointing power's favor. A renewable term would be *worse* than life tenure; any US draft must say "nonrenewable" in the text.
3. **Staggering is what actually depoliticizes appointments.** France's one-third-every-three-years and the 18-year/biennial US design turn each vacancy from a generational jackpot into a routine event, lowering the stakes of both confirmations and (indirectly) presidential elections.
4. **Fix the calendar, not the officeholder.** Italy's terms run from each judge's swearing-in, so delays compound into bench gaps. Terms should attach to numbered *seats* on a fixed schedule, with partial-term replacements finishing the seat's clock (the French rule).
5. **Anti-evasion beats prohibition.** A third of the world's term-limited executives try to overstay, almost always through legal forms. The robust designs make limit-loosening *non-self-dealing*: any relaxation applies only to persons elected or appointed after it takes effect (the 22nd Amendment's Truman clause, generalized and made permanent).
6. **The tightest limit is the worst limit.** Mexico's absolute single terms produced boss-controlled amateur legislatures; California's and Michigan's 6-year chambers produced the lobbyist-empowerment literature; both jurisdictions independently reformed to ~12 years. Twelve years is the empirically convergent number.
7. **Age limits and term limits are complements, not substitutes.** South Africa (12 years *or* 70), Germany (12 years *and* 68) use dual limits; the UK/Canada retirement-age model alone still allows 40-year tenures for young appointees. An 18-year term does most of the work; an age ceiling backstops the incapacity problem.
8. **Supermajority or bipartisan appointment rules pair naturally with fixed terms.** Germany's two-thirds election rule is what converts regular vacancies into consensus judges. (For the US package this is probably a statutory companion, not amendment text — but the pairing explains *why* fixed terms lower court temperature abroad.)

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. The three below clear it — congressional limits at 87% (90R/86D), fixed SCOTUS terms at 79% (73R/87D), and the anti-evasion clause as structural protection for both. One candidate is held back at the end.*

1. **The congressional service cap.**
   - Proposed formulation: *"No person shall be elected to the Senate more than twice, nor to the House of Representatives more than six times; and no term to which a person was elected before the ratification of this article shall be counted."*
   - Tracks the 22nd Amendment's proven "No person shall be elected" structure; 12/12 years matches the UMD deliberative majorities, the California/Michigan reform endpoint, and Mexico's corrected figure. (The Cruz–Norman 2/3 formula is the tighter alternative; adopting it would buy an existing congressional coalition at the cost of the expertise evidence.)
   - ⚠️ **The expertise-drain trap:** at 6 House years, the [state-level evidence](https://www.ppic.org/wp-content/uploads/content/pubs/report/R_1104BCR.pdf) predicts power flowing to lobbyists, staff, and the executive. Defend 12 years per chamber on the record and resist pressure to out-populist U.S. Term Limits with shorter caps.
   - ⚠️ **The transition/grandfathering trap:** counting pre-ratification terms would force sitting members to vote themselves out — the surest way to lose a two-thirds vote (every near-miss 1995–97 version was prospective; the 22nd grandfathered Truman). Prospectivity also buys ~12 years of implementation runway. Accept the "hypocrisy" attack; it is the price of proposal.
   - ⚠️ **The partial-term trap:** appointed and special-election partial Senate terms need the 22nd Amendment's device (a partial term over half served counts as a full term), or governors can mint limit-exempt seat-warmers.

2. **Fixed eighteen-year Supreme Court terms with biennial seats.**
   - Proposed formulation: *"Justices of the Supreme Court shall be appointed as heretofore, and shall hold their offices during good behaviour, but shall exercise the judicial power of the Supreme Court for a single term of eighteen years, one such term commencing in each odd-numbered year; thereafter they may continue in judicial office as Congress shall by law provide."*
   - Keeps the "good Behaviour" words (the office remains life-tenured; only *Supreme Court duty* is fixed), which both answers the independence objection and lets the senior-status machinery live in statute. "Fixed term," not "term limit," is the tested cross-partisan wording ([Marquette 2026](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/): 79%, 73% R / 87% D).
   - ⚠️ **The staggering trap:** 18-year terms yield exactly two appointments per presidential term *only if* the calendar holds. The amendment must attach terms to nine numbered seats on a fixed biennial schedule; a justice who dies or retires early is replaced only **for the remainder of that seat's term** (the French rule), or the seat sits with the Court hearing cases as constituted — otherwise strategic retirements and deaths re-randomize the Court and recreate today's lottery. Also decide the *confirmation-refusal* default (a Garland scenario): specify that the Court sits with fewer justices until confirmation, so obstruction gains nothing permanent.
   - ⚠️ **The statutory-shortcut trap:** endorsing the Khanna/Whitehouse statutes as a "faster path" imports the [good-Behaviour/"one supreme Court" litigation risk that CRS flags](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12343) — adjudicated by the affected justices themselves. The foundation's amendment-only posture is the differentiator; treat the statutes as evidence of demand, not as the vehicle.
   - ⚠️ **The transition trap (judicial edition):** applying terms to sitting justices reads as targeting particular incumbents and flips the reform's partisan valence overnight. Both current bills exempt sitting justices; the amendment should too, phasing in as seats naturally turn over.

3. **The anti-evasion clause.**
   - Proposed formulation: *"No amendment to, or law affecting, the limitations of this article shall apply to any person holding office at the time of its adoption, nor to any person elected or appointed within [four] years thereafter."*
   - Directly generalizes the 22nd Amendment's grandfather device into a permanent non-self-dealing rule — the answer the comparative literature ([Versteeg 2020](https://www.columbialawreview.org/content/the-law-and-politics-of-presidential-term-limit-evasion/); [Venice Commission 2018](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD(2018)010-e)) gives to Russia's "zeroing," Venezuela's referendum, and, domestically, North Dakota's 2026 loosening measure.
   - ⚠️ **The eternity-clause trap:** a flatly unamendable clause is alien to US doctrine and would draw legitimate Article V objections from all sides. Delay-and-disqualify (changes valid, but never for current beneficiaries) achieves the entrenchment effect without unamendability. Don't overreach into a true eternity clause.

**Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar:**

- **Uniformity and the Thornton settlement** (*"This article establishes the sole limitation on the number of terms of service in the offices to which it applies, and shall not be construed to deny the several States any power they otherwise retain over the manner of holding elections."*) — unpolled and purely construction-guiding, and its state-election-power language risks being read against the redistricting category. Uniformity follows from the operative text anyway; revisit only if ratification lawyers demand it.

**The package skeleton.** Section 1: congressional limits (two Senate / six House terms, prospective, partial-term rule). Section 2: eighteen-year fixed Supreme Court terms on nine biennially-staggered seats, sitting justices exempt, remainder-of-term replacements, post-term judicial office preserved. Section 3: anti-evasion — no change to this article benefits incumbents or near-term appointees. Section 4: Congress's enforcement/implementation power for scheduling and senior-service details. Section 5: effective-date and transition mechanics. (The held-back uniformity clause is omitted; it can be reintroduced during formal drafting if needed.)

**Inherently statutory (leave out of the amendment):**

- The senior-status regime for term-expired justices (duties, circuit-riding, original-jurisdiction service) — Congress already runs senior status by statute.
- Confirmation-process scheduling details (hearing deadlines, floor-vote timetables) beyond the constitutional default for an unfilled seat.
- Lower-federal-court retirement ages or service terms — desirable, but bundling all Article III judges multiplies opposition; the amendment can permit it via the enforcement clause.
- Pensions, salary protection mechanics, and post-service employment/lobbying restrictions (that's the Lobbying category's territory).
- State legislative and gubernatorial limits — already state-constitutional matters, working as a laboratory; nationalizing them would trade federalism objections for no polling gain.
- Ballot-notation or "Scarlet Letter" devices (voided in *Cook v. Gralike* (2001) anyway).
- Appointment-supermajority or bipartisan-commission rules for judicial nominations — internationally attractive (Germany) but a separate, far more contested reform.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)

### Extends: US Legal & Legislative Landscape

- **A sitting president has now formally endorsed 18-year SCOTUS terms — but as a statute, not an amendment.** On July 29, 2024, President Biden's [White House fact sheet](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/fact-sheet-president-biden-announces-bold-plan-reform-the-supreme-court-and-ensure-no) called for a system in which "the President would appoint a Justice every two years to spend eighteen years in active service on the Supreme Court," alongside a binding ethics code and a separate "No One Is Above the Law" *amendment* on presidential immunity. Notably, the term-limits plank was framed for Congress to legislate, and [Forbes reported he "didn't specify whether he wants legislation or a constitutional amendment"](https://www.forbes.com/sites/alisondurkee/2024/07/29/biden-endorses-supreme-court-term-limits-how-it-would-work-and-why-its-still-unlikely/); Speaker Johnson called court reform "dead on arrival." Relevance to the memo's partisan-valence warning: the reform is now attached to a Democratic president's name, which makes the "fixed terms, by amendment, sitting justices exempt" framing — and cross-partisan sourcing — more important, not less.
- **Amendment-form SCOTUS term-limit vehicles now exist alongside the statutes.** On December 7, 2024, Sens. Peter Welch (D-VT) and Joe Manchin (I-WV) [filed a joint resolution](https://www.welch.senate.gov/welch-manchin-lead-constitutional-amendment-to-establish-supreme-court-term-limits) proposing a constitutional amendment: nonrenewable 18-year terms for new justices, a new term beginning every two years, sitting justices unaffected, the Court kept at nine, and — the French rule — a justice who leaves early is replaced only for the remainder of that term, with terms commencing on the biennial calendar regardless of when incumbents depart. In the House, [H.J.Res. 174 (119th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hjres/BILLSTATUS-119hjres174.xml) (Olszewski, D-MD, introduced May 4, 2026, six cosponsors) proposes the same in amendment form. Both are all-Democratic sponsorship so far; but they show the amendment path — the foundation's posture — is already on the table in Congress, and the Manchin–Welch text is the closest existing draft to the memo's Principle 2 (seat-fixed calendar, remainder-of-term replacement, incumbents exempt).
- **Domestic evasion pressure on the 22nd Amendment runs in both directions — cite it paired.** Rep. José Serrano (D-NY) introduced a resolution to *repeal the 22nd Amendment outright* in Congress after Congress (e.g., [H.J.Res. 15, 113th, Jan. 4, 2013](https://www.congress.gov/bill/113th-congress/house-joint-resolution/15); earlier versions in the 108th, 111th, and 112th), during presidencies of both parties. On January 23, 2025, Rep. Andy Ogles (R-TN) [introduced a joint resolution](https://ogles.house.gov/media/press-releases/rep-ogles-proposes-amending-22nd-amendment-allow-trump-serve-third-term) to rewrite it so that "No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than three times, nor be elected to any additional term after being elected to two consecutive terms" — language his release states was designed to allow President Trump a third term. Neither has advanced, but together they are the US instance of the pattern the Versteeg/Ginsburg literature describes: limit-loosening proposed by the incumbent's own side, through legal forms. Both bear directly on Principle 3 (the anti-evasion clause): a rule that no change to a limit may benefit sitting or near-term officeholders would have neutralized each proposal's motive without touching Article V.

### Extends: Prior Amendment Proposals / Current congressional vehicle

- **The 2023 committee kill, precisely.** The House Judiciary Committee markup that defeated H.J.Res. 11 (118th) took place on **September 28, 2023**, failing 17–19; the four Republicans voting no were Darrell Issa (CA), Tom McClintock (CA), Scott Fitzgerald (WI), and Harriet Hageman (WY) — Hageman arguing term limits "would deny voters a choice," Fitzgerald that biennial elections already serve as term limits ([Fox News, Feb. 2, 2024](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/will-congress-ever-have-term-limits-four-republicans-sided-democrats-kill-latest-proposal); [U.S. Term Limits](https://termlimits.com/time-to-clean-house/) *(advocacy source)*). The current Cruz–Norman H.J.Res. 12 (119th) had [113 House cosponsors — 105 Republicans and 8 Democrats — as of May 2026](https://www.newsweek.com/term-limits-congress-popular-whats-stopping-them-11925287): a real but lopsided elite coalition beneath an evenly split public.

### Extends: Polling (headline findings / Amendment Framework)

- **NPR/PBS News/Marist (fieldwork April 27–30, 2026; n=1,322 adults; ±3.1):** [83% support term limits for members of Congress](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/07/nx-s1-5806294/congress-age-caps-term-limits-npr-poll) — **78% D / 89% R / 84% I** — and 80% support a maximum age for congressional candidates (78% D / 83% R / 79% I). The Democratic figure is the bipartisan floor and is a few points below Pew 2023's 86%; still comfortably above the bar. Support is flat across generations (Gen Z lowest at 72%). Age caps poll nearly as well as term limits and are worth tracking as a possible companion, though the memo's design leaves age limits to the judicial section only.
- **PRRI American Values Survey (fieldwork Aug. 15–Sept. 8, 2025; n=5,543):** [75% agree there should be term limits for U.S. Supreme Court justices — 85% D / 76% I / 67% R](https://prri.org/research/trumps-unprecedented-actions-deepen-asymmetric-divides/); 22% disagree. The 67% Republican floor is lower than Marquette's 73% for "fixed terms" — consistent with the memo's finding that generic "term limits" wording costs Republican support relative to "fixed 18-year terms."
- **Fox News (Beacon Research/Shaw & Co.; fieldwork July 7–10, 2024; n=1,210 RV):** [78% favor limiting justices to an 18-year term (up from 66% in July 2022) and 81% favor a mandatory retirement age (up from 71%)](https://www.foxnews.com/official-polls/fox-news-poll-supreme-court-approval-rating-drops-record-low); the article reports the largest gains among Republicans (+17 on term limits, +15 on retirement age) but does not publish party-level percentages. Useful as a Republican-branded confirmation of the ~78–79% baseline for conservative audiences.

### Extends: State Models

- **North Dakota, 2026 — the anti-evasion clause worked, and it worked without an eternity clause.** The 2022 voter-approved [Article XV](https://ndlegis.gov/constit/a15.pdf) contains a section the memo should quote: "*Notwithstanding the legislative assembly's authority to propose amendments to this constitution under article IV, section 16 thereof, the legislative assembly shall not have authority to propose an amendment to this constitution to alter or repeal the term limitations established in section 1 of this article. The authority to propose an amendment... is reserved to initiative petition of the people.*" On **June 25, 2026**, the North Dakota Supreme Court [unanimously held](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2026/06/25/north-dakota-supreme-court-tosses-ballot-measure-finding-term-limits-proposal-unconstitutional/) (McEvers, C.J.) that the legislature's SCR 4008 — the 2026 measure to loosen the limits to 16 years in either chamber and exclude partial terms — violated that clause, declared it void ab initio, and ordered the Secretary of State to keep "Constitutional Measure 1" off the November 2026 ballot. The challenge was brought by two members of the 2022 sponsoring committee (one a former Republican senator) and funded by U.S. Term Limits. This is the best domestic evidence for Principle 3: the clause did not make the limits unamendable — it only removed the self-interested body's power to propose — and a court enforced it. It also updates the memo's State Models paragraph (the referred measure will *not* be voted on in 2026).
- **Term limits and polarization — a further strand of the opposition brief.** [Olson & Rogowski, "Legislative Term Limits and Polarization," *Journal of Politics* 82(2) (2020)](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/citations/legislative-term-limits-and-polarization/), using state roll-call data 1993–2016, find term limits "produced systematically higher levels of polarization" by widening the R–D gap in voting records, with larger effects in more professional legislatures and increased party-committee contributions to candidates — i.e., limits shift leverage from members to parties. The earlier 50-state survey ([Carey, Niemi, Powell & Moncrief, *Legislative Studies Quarterly* 31:1 (2006)](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/polsci_facpubs/82/)) found limits change *who is elected* almost not at all but do produce a "Burkean shift" away from district constituents and strengthen governors and bureaucracies at the expense of legislative leaders. Both reinforce the memo's dose-response answer (12 years, not 6) and add a specific counter to the populist claim that limits reduce partisanship.

### Extends: International Models — Germany

- **The entrenchment vote, precisely.** The Bundestag adopted the Basic Law amendment on **December 19, 2024, by 600–69** (of 733), and the Bundesrat approved it without change on December 20; the package constitutionalized the Court's status as an autonomous federal court, its two senates of eight judges, the **12-year non-renewable term ending no later than age 68**, the binding force of its rulings, the two-thirds election rule split between Bundestag and Bundesrat, and a *replacement-election mechanism* letting the other chamber elect if one is blocked for three months — adopted explicitly with Poland's court capture in view and before a blocking minority could form after the February 2025 election ([Birmingham Law School analysis, Jan. 2025](https://blog.bham.ac.uk/lawresearch/2025/01/strengthening-the-resilience-of-the-german-federal-constitutional-court/)). The replacement mechanism is Germany's answer to the "confirmation-refusal default" the memo flags under Principle 2.

### Extends: International Models — Mexico

- **Mexico has reversed course again — from the memo's "12-year" endpoint back to no consecutive reelection.** A constitutional decree published in the *Diario Oficial* on **April 1, 2025** (in force April 2) rewrote Article 59 so that "senators and deputies... may not be reelected for the period immediately following their mandate," and directed states to bar consecutive reelection of state deputies, mayors and councils; the ban applies from the **2030** electoral processes (President Sheinbaum had proposed 2027; the Senate pushed it to 2030 to hold the two-thirds coalition), so members serving in 2030 may not seek reelection ([Cámara de Diputados / DOF text](https://comunicacionsocial.diputados.gob.mx/index.php/notilegis/dof-publica-reformas-que-prohiben-reeleccion-consecutiva-y-nepotismo-electoral); [Mexico News Daily, Feb. 26, 2025](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/politics/mexico-nepotism/)). The April 2026 electoral-reform package, ratified by 20 of 32 states, [reiterated the consecutive-reelection ban](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/politics/electoral-reform-approved-april-10-mananera/). The 2014–2025 window of consecutive reelection thus lasted a single cycle. The memo's abstraction lesson ("the tightest limit is the worst limit") still stands on the 1917–2014 record, but the claim that Mexico "landed" on ~12 years must be retired — see Flagged corrections.

### Extends: The evasion file / International Abstraction Lessons

- **International human-rights law now rejects the "reelection is a human right" evasion.** In [Advisory Opinion OC-28/21 (June 7, 2021)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-legal-materials/article/advisory-opinion-oc2821-on-presidential-reelection-without-term-limits-interam-ct-hr/A438E71742DF0669EFB1CF06F4940D44), requested by Colombia, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights held that presidential reelection without term limits is *not* an autonomous right under the American Convention; that prohibiting indefinite reelection is compatible with the Convention as a protection of representative democracy and pluralism; and that *permitting* indefinite reelection is contrary to states' Convention obligations (Judge Zaffaroni dissenting). This is the direct doctrinal answer to the Bolivia (2017) and Honduras (2015) court rulings the memo cites, and it supplies a regional-court precedent that term limits are democracy-protecting rather than rights-restricting — useful in the anti-evasion argument for Principle 3.

The markup and 17–19 vote occurred on **September 28, 2023**; the memo's Fox News source is dated February 2, 2024, which is likely the source of the slip ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/will-congress-ever-have-term-limits-four-republicans-sided-democrats-kill-latest-proposal); [U.S. Term Limits](https://termlimits.com/time-to-clean-house/)).

As of the April 1, 2025 constitutional decree, consecutive reelection of federal and state legislators is again prohibited beginning with the 2030 elections ([DOF text via Cámara de Diputados](https://comunicacionsocial.diputados.gob.mx/index.php/notilegis/dof-publica-reformas-que-prohiben-reeleccion-consecutiva-y-nepotismo-electoral)). ## Implementation: the law today and the day after (2026-08-18)

*Amendment-specific companion to [RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md](RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md) (supremacy, self-execution vs. enabling legislation, trigger laws, effective dates). This section applies that general law to the three-section draft in [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) §2; it does not restate it. Every new URL below was opened and checked on 2026-08-18.*

### 1. Federal law today

- **No federal statute limits congressional or judicial service, and none forbids limits.** The only federal term limit in force is the Twenty-second Amendment (President), which also supplies the two devices the draft borrows or omits: the "No person shall be elected" form, the exemption for the officeholder at proposal, and the partial-term rule (more than two years of another person's term counts as a full election) — [National Archives, Amendments 11–27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27); [National Constitution Center, Amendment XXII](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/amendments/amendment-xxii).
- **Congress.** The Qualifications Clauses (Art. I §2 cl. 2 and §3 cl. 3: age, citizenship, inhabitancy) are the whole list — [LII, Article I](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei). *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), holds that under Art. I §5 each House "is limited to the standing qualifications prescribed in the Constitution" when it judges its members' qualifications — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/395/486). *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995), holds neither states nor Congress may add to the list; change "must come... through the amendment procedures set forth in Article V" — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/779/). *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510 (2001), struck Missouri's ballot labels for candidates who declined to support a term-limits amendment as outside the Elections Clause ("It does not regulate the time of elections; it does not regulate the place of elections; nor... the manner of elections") — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-929.ZO.html). Ballot access for congressional candidates is otherwise state law under Art. I §4 ("The times, places and manner of holding elections... shall be prescribed in each state... but the Congress may at any time by law make or alter such regulations") — [LII, Article I](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei).
- **Supreme Court.** Art. III §1: "one supreme Court"; judges "shall hold their offices during good behaviour" — [LII, Article III](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articleiii). Appointment is by the President "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate" (Art. II §2 cl. 2) — [LII, Article II](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articleii). By statute the Court "shall consist of a Chief Justice of the United States and eight associate justices, any six of whom shall constitute a quorum" — [28 U.S.C. § 1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1). Senior status: a justice or judge meeting the age-and-service "rule of 80" may "retain the office but retire from regular active service" at full salary, subject to annual certification of continued work — [28 U.S.C. § 371](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/371); a retired Chief Justice or associate justice "may be designated and assigned by the Chief Justice of the United States to perform such judicial duties in any circuit, including those of a circuit justice, as he is willing to undertake," and "no retired justice or judge shall perform judicial duties except when designated and assigned" — [28 U.S.C. § 294](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/294). No statute fixes a term of Supreme Court duty; the CRS view is that "most commentators agree" Congress cannot do so by statute — [CRS IF12343](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12343).
- **Pending federal vehicles.** Statutory: the Whitehouse–Booker [Supreme Court Biennial Appointments and Term Limits Act, S. 3096 (118th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-118s3096is/html/BILLS-118s3096is.htm) — the President appoints one justice "within the first 120 days of the first and third years" of a term; "only the 9 most recently appointed justices... shall preside over appellate jurisdiction cases" while "all justices... shall preside over original jurisdiction cases"; the first appointment after a Chief Justice vacancy is to that office; effective with "the first full term of a President" after enactment (reintroduced July 2026 — [Whitehouse release](https://www.whitehouse.senate.gov/news/release/whitehouse-booker-blumenthal-padilla-reintroduce-supreme-court-term-limits-bill-to-restore-americans-trust-in-the-court/)); and the [Khanna–Beyer bill](https://khanna.house.gov/media/press-releases/reps-khanna-and-beyer-reintroduce-legislation-set-supreme-court-term-limits) (same architecture, current justices exempt). Amendment-form: the [Welch–Manchin joint resolution (Dec. 2024)](https://www.welch.senate.gov/welch-manchin-lead-constitutional-amendment-to-establish-supreme-court-term-limits) and [H.J.Res. 174 (119th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hjres/BILLSTATUS-119hjres174.xml) (18-year nonrenewable terms, new term every two years, sitting justices unaffected, remainder-of-term replacement); congressional: [S.J.Res. 1 / H.J.Res. 12 (119th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/12) (2 Senate / 3 House, prospective).

### 2. State law today

- **State legislators:** 16 states limit them (AZ, AR, CA, CO, FL, LA, ME, MI, MO, MT, NE, NV, ND, OH, OK, SD), a mix of consecutive and lifetime caps of 6–12 years per chamber — [NCSL, "The Term-Limited States"](https://www.ncsl.org/about-state-legislatures/the-term-limited-states); [KTTC (Feb. 2026)](https://www.kttc.com/2026/02/19/16-states-have-term-limits-their-legislatures-minnesota-does-not/). Untouched by the draft, which reaches only the Senate, the House, and the Supreme Court.
- **State judges:** 33 states set mandatory retirement ages — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Mandatory_retirement); 339 of 344 state supreme court justices serve limited terms or face retirement — [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/life-tenure-rarity-state-supreme-courts). Untouched.
- **State limits on members of Congress:** 23 states adopted them 1990–94 — [CRS 96-152](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/96-152.html); all became unenforceable under *Thornton*. Some remain printed in state constitutions. Example: Missouri Const. Art. III §45(a) still reads "No United States Senator from Missouri shall serve more than two terms in the United States Senate, and no United States Representative from Missouri shall serve more than four terms," and adds that voters intend federal officials "to observe the wishes of the people as stated in this section in the event any provision thereof is held invalid" — [Missouri Revisor of Statutes](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=III++++45%28a%29&bid=31795&constit=y). Arkansas Amendment 73 §3 (three House / two Senate terms) is the provision *Thornton* itself struck — [Encyclopedia of Arkansas](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/term-limits-8365/). Because the draft sets its own numbers and *Thornton*'s rule that states may not add qualifications is not disturbed, these stricter state provisions stay unenforceable after ratification; the draft's held-back uniformity clause would have said so expressly.
- **Article V applications:** 14 states have passed a term-limits-only convention application per the campaign's own count — [U.S. Term Limits](https://termlimits.com/progress/) *(advocacy source)*. Congressional proposal and ratification would render them moot.
- **A ratification-contingent state provision already existed on this subject.** Missouri's 1996 Article VIII (the "instruct and label" scheme struck in *Cook v. Gralike*) provided, in its §20, for "automatic repeal of the Article should the specified Congressional Term Limits Amendment be ratified" (*Cook*, n. 5) — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-929.ZO.html). It is the one verified instance of a state law keyed to ratification of a congressional term-limits amendment; it was itself voided in 2001.

### 3. The day after ratification

**(a) Becomes unenforceable or moot.** No federal statute falls: nothing in the U.S. Code sets or forbids these limits. What changes is doctrine and pending business. *Thornton*'s holding that congressional term limits require an amendment is satisfied rather than overruled; *Powell*'s limit on exclusion now includes a new "standing qualification," so a House or Senate could refuse to seat a person elected past the cap under Art. I §5. The statutory Supreme Court bills (S. 3096, Khanna–Beyer) are superseded as to the term itself — the "good Behaviour"/"one supreme Court" litigation risk CRS flags attaches to a statute, not to text in the Constitution — though their senior-service and calendar mechanics become the natural template for legislation under Section 2. State congressional-limit provisions such as Missouri Art. III §45(a) remain unenforceable (they set different numbers and remain state-added qualifications); repeal is state housekeeping, not required. State convention applications are moot.

**(b) Stands.** The Twenty-second Amendment; 28 U.S.C. § 1 (nine justices, six for a quorum) — the draft's "one such term commencing in each odd-numbered year" produces nine 18-year seats at steady state without saying "nine"; 28 U.S.C. §§ 371 and 294 (senior status and assignment of retired justices) — but note § 371's age-and-service thresholds would not reach a term-expired justice who is younger than 65, so § 371 alone cannot carry Section 2's post-term service; state legislative limits and state judicial retirement ages; the Seventeenth Amendment's temporary Senate appointments (an appointee is not "elected"); Article II appointment ("appointed as heretofore").

**(c) The draft's own transition and enforcement clauses.** Section 1: "no term to which a person was elected before the ratification of this article shall be counted" — prospective; the first election a sitting member could lose to the cap is the seventh House election or third Senate election after ratification, i.e., 12+ years out. Section 2: "as heretofore" preserves the appointment process; "as Congress shall by law provide" delegates the post-term judicial office; there is no express clause on justices in office at ratification and no seat-calendar or early-vacancy rule. Section 3: "No amendment to, or law affecting, the limitations of this article shall apply to any person holding office at the time of its adoption, nor to any person elected or appointed within four years thereafter" — self-executing rule of construction. There is no effective-date section; the article applies on certification. There is no enforcement clause.

**(d) Enabling legislation.** Required by the text: a statute under Section 2 defining what a justice does after the 18-year term (senior status, circuit duty, original-jurisdiction sittings, salary continuation) — the closest existing drafts are S. 3096's "9 most recently appointed justices"/original-jurisdiction split and the Khanna–Beyer senior-duty rotation, and the existing template is §§ 371/294. Until Congress acts, a term-expired justice holds a life-tenured office with no defined duties. Likely though not textually required: (i) a statute under Art. I §4 directing states to keep term-barred candidates off congressional ballots (Congress "may at any time by law make or alter such regulations"), or Congress may leave it to state ballot-access law plus Art. I §5 exclusion; (ii) an amendment to 28 U.S.C. § 1 aligning the Court's size, quorum, and Chief Justice succession with the biennial calendar; (iii) a seat-numbering and vacancy statute (see (f)).

**(e) State conforming or trigger legislation.** States administer congressional ballot access; each would need to amend its filing statutes so the chief election officer may reject a declaration of candidacy from a person already elected six times to the House or twice to the Senate (counting only post-ratification terms), and to define the records used to prove prior elections. States could enact these now, effective on the Archivist's certification — the ordinary contingent-statute mechanism. The only verified historical instance of state law keyed to ratification of a congressional term-limits amendment is Missouri Article VIII §20 (1996), a self-repeal clause in a scheme later voided (*Cook v. Gralike*, n. 5); no state has a pending trigger law for this amendment that we could confirm.

**(f) Litigation questions the wording leaves open.**
1. *Sitting justices.* Section 2 does not say whether justices in office at ratification are counted, exempt, or start an 18-year clock at ratification. Section 3's "person holding office at the time of its adoption" governs later amendments and laws, not the article itself. The foundation's stated design (memo skeleton; page "What we left out") is that sitting justices are exempt; the canonical text does not say so. This is the largest gap.
2. *Early vacancies and the calendar.* "One such term commencing in each odd-numbered year" with "a single term of eighteen years" does not say whether a successor to a justice who dies or resigns mid-term serves the remainder or a fresh 18 years, or whether the term runs from the calendar date if the Senate has not confirmed anyone. Welch–Manchin and H.J.Res. 174 answer this in text; the draft does not. Congress could try to answer it under the necessary-and-proper power as it structures the Court in 28 U.S.C. § 1, but whether such a statute is a "law affecting the limitations of this article" under Section 3 — and so inapplicable to anyone then in office or appointed within four years — is itself a question.
3. *The Chief Justice.* Whether elevation of a sitting associate justice is a new "appointment" beginning a new "single term," and whether the Chief's 18 years runs from first appointment. S. 3096 handles this by statute; the draft is silent.
4. *Partial and special-election terms (Section 1).* The draft counts elections, not years, and omits the Twenty-second Amendment's partial-term device. An appointed senator's service is not "elected" and does not count; a special-election winner's brief term does count as one of two (Senate) or six (House). Both consequences follow the text.
5. *Section 3 against a later amendment.* Whether one amendment can bind the effect of a later amendment that expressly says otherwise (the general rule is that later text controls) — the same debate the memo flags under the eternity-clause trap. Against statutes it is straightforward; against amendments it will be argued.
6. *State enforcement scope.* Whether a state may adopt stricter congressional limits after ratification (the *Thornton* rule says no; the draft's held-back uniformity clause would have said so expressly), and whether state ballot bars mirroring the article are "manner" regulations under *Cook v. Gralike* (enforcing a constitutional qualification is unlike Missouri's labels, but the question will be litigated).
7. *"Judicial power of the Supreme Court."* Whether a term-expired justice may still vote on certiorari, sit by designation on the Court in a recusal-driven shortage, or perform in-chambers work — all "exercise of the judicial power of the Supreme Court" — is left to the Section 2 statute and, ultimately, the Court.
