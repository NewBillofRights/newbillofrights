# Expanding the House of Representatives — Rejected Category Memo

*Candidate 2026-08-06 to 2026-08-18; rejected 2026-08-18 on the founder's decision after the candidate memo. Memo written 2026-08-18 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md); every figure below was verified against the linked source in the candidate memo ([candidates/HOUSE_EXPANSION_CANDIDATE.md](../candidates/HOUSE_EXPANSION_CANDIDATE.md)), which is retained for the record.*

**Verdict in one line:** 23% (Republicans) on increasing the size of the House, Pew July 2023 — **Weak**, and the why-an-amendment test (§5.3) is weak because 435 has been a statutory number since 1929 — path back is an independent poll of the Wyoming-Rule or cube-root framing clearing a 45% Republican floor, after a statutory enlargement has been tried.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

**The cap is a statute, not a constitutional rule.** The Constitution sets only a ceiling on district size and a floor on state delegations — "The Number of Representatives shall not exceed one for every thirty Thousand, but each State shall have at Least one Representative." The House grew after nearly every census until the 1910 census fixed it at 435. After the 1920 census the House failed to reapportion itself — the only time in its history — and the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 ended the deadlock by capping membership at the 1910 level and automating reapportionment after each census (House passed June 11, 1929; signed June 18, 1929 — [House Historian](https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1901-1950/The-Permanent-Apportionment-Act-of-1929/)). Under the 2020 census the apportionment population was 331,108,434 across 435 seats — **761,169 people per representative** ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2023/decennial/c2020br-01.html)).

**The main variants:**

- **Repeal the cap outright / fix a ratio** — roughly one representative per 300,000 in the version that entered our pipeline. Statutory as to the cap; constitutional only if a ratio is entrenched.
- **The Wyoming Rule** — divide the national apportionment population by the population of the least-populous state, so the average district approximates the smallest state's. On 2020 numbers the American Academy of Arts and Sciences models this at **574 seats, averaging 572,000 constituents** per member ([*The Case for Enlarging the House*, §5](https://www.amacad.org/ourcommonpurpose/enlarging-the-house/section/5)).
- **The cube-root rule** — set chamber size at the cube root of national population, which the same report models at **692 seats** on 2020 data, noting the United States "has one of the largest gaps between chamber size and the cube root of its population" among democracies (§5).
- **"Article the First"** — the unratified first of the twelve articles sent to the states on September 25, 1789. Eleven states ratified, the last Kentucky on June 27, 1792, one short of the twelve then required; it carries no ratification deadline, remains pending, and would today need 38 ([ratification record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congressional_Apportionment_Amendment)). Two cautions belong with the story: a last-minute change of "less" to "more" in the final clause left the formula mathematically inoperable across part of its range, and read literally against a 331-million population its ratio would require **more than 6,000 representatives**. It is a pedigree argument, not a usable text. Its only live legislative vehicle runs the other way: **H.J.Res. 186**, introduced May 14, 2026 and referred to the Judiciary Committee, would make the amendment valid "only if it is ratified … not later than December 31, 2026," with any later ratification "not … valid" ([full text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres186ih/html/BILLS-119hjres186ih.htm)) — extinguishing Article the First rather than enacting it. No pending bill would revive it.

**The leading institutional proposal is statutory.** The American Academy of Arts and Sciences' *Our Common Purpose* Recommendation 1.1 is to "substantially enlarge the House of Representatives through federal legislation," and its FAQ states the reason plainly: "The size of the House is set by statute, so a vote of Congress is all that is needed." The companion report proposes adding 150 seats, for a House of 585, with regular expansion thereafter ([American Academy, Enlarging the House](https://www.amacad.org/ourcommonpurpose/initiative/enlarging-house-representatives)).

Logged in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) and [REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md); added to [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) as a candidate on 2026-08-12 with the gate "new polling of Wyoming Rule / cube-root framings." ## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Pew Research Center, American Trends Panel Wave 130, July 10–16, 2023 (n=8,480 adults; ±1.5 points).** Asked whether the size of the House should be increased, decreased, or kept the same: **29% increase / 53% stay the same / 15% decrease.** By party, **23% of Republicans and Republican leaners** favor increasing it against **35% of Democrats and Democratic leaners** — "larger shares in both parties favor keeping the House at its current size." Republican-leaning independents (30%) run ahead of Republican partisans (19%); adults under 30 favor an increase far more than those 65 and older (44% vs. 17%) — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/); [methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/views-of-politics-methodology/). **This is the floor number and the only modern national reading with a party split.** For scale, the same survey found congressional term limits at 87%, an age limit for elected officials at 79%, and an age limit for justices at 74% — the House-size item is not depressed by a general reluctance to change institutions; it is specifically unpopular.
- **Pew Research Center, American Trends Panel, January 29–February 13, 2018 (n=4,656; ±2.4 points).** A split-form experiment. Asked cold: **28% increase / 51% same / 18% decrease** — **Republicans 21% / 55% / 22%; Democrats 34% / 49% / 14%.** Asked with historical context — that the first Congress had 65 members to today's 435, and that each member then represented roughly 60,000 people against roughly 700,000 now — the increase share rises to **34%** overall (44% same, 21% decrease), but the movement is one-sided: **Democrats rise to 44%** while Pew reports **no differences among Republicans** — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/5-the-electoral-college-congress-and-representation/); [methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/methodology-91/). The strongest argument for expansion, delivered directly, moved one party and not the other.
- **Knowledge Networks probability panel, September 13–19, 2006 (n=1,020 of 1,425 invited; 71.6% completion), reported in Brian Frederick, "The People's Perspective on the Size of the People's House," *PS: Political Science & Politics* 41(2), April 2008 ([open-access full text with party crosstabs](https://vc.bridgew.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1013&context=polisci_fac)).** Three framings, each with a party breakdown:
  - **Costs named.** The stem recites that the House began with 65 members at roughly 30,000 people per district and now has 435 at roughly 640,000, then gives *both* arguments — closer representation for an increase; greater cost and a less efficient legislative process against it: **18.9% increase / 61.9% keep at the current size / 19.3% decrease**, with **Republicans 11.3%**, Democrats 23.7%, independents and others 22.7%. Every subgroup reported in the study gave majority support to keeping the House at its present size.
  - **Enlarging to keep states from losing seats after reapportionment** — **33.6% support / 66.4% oppose**, with **Republicans 26.2%**, Democrats 39.2%, independents and others 33.4%.
  - **Enlarging to create more opportunities for women and racial minorities to be elected** — the highest-scoring framing at **48.6% supportive** (15.5% very, 33.1% somewhat) against 51.4% opposed, and the most divided: **Republicans 33.9% supportive** against Democrats 59.7%. The study's own conclusion is that a proposal built on that rationale "would not garner a bipartisan consensus."

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **Verasight for Fix Our House and New America, June 30–July 7, 2026 (n=1,000; ±3.2 points).** **54% support / 27% oppose** "expanding the House of Representatives" with 150 new members — the only majority reading in the record, and 25 points above Pew's 29% three years earlier. The verbatim item is: *"The U.S. House of Representatives has had 435 members since 1911. Since then, the U.S. population has more than tripled. Would you support or oppose adding 150 new members to the House so that each representative serves a smaller number of constituents?"* — 18.3% strongly support, 36.0% somewhat support, 15.3% somewhat oppose, 11.8% strongly oppose, **18.6% not sure**. Two features matter: the stem carries the founding-era growth argument *inside the question*, which is the same information treatment Pew's 2018 split form found moved Democrats and not Republicans; and nearly one in five respondents is unsure, so the majority rests on a soft base — [Fix Our House](https://www.fixourhouse.org/new-poll-shows-growing-support-for-proportional-representation); [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/proportional-representation-poll/); [Newsweek, Aug. 10, 2026](https://www.newsweek.com/voters-back-adding-150-new-lawmakers-congress-12304081). **No party breakdown is published for the House-expansion item**, and no crosstabs are posted. Both sponsors advocate the reform. The same survey reports 50% support / 24% oppose for proportional representation, with the sponsors stating that the 2:1 ratio "holds true for Republicans, Democrats, and Independents alike" — a claim made for that item only, not for House expansion.

**Legislative and ballot record**

- **No roll-call vote and no ballot measure exists.** The cap has stood since 1929 ([House Historian](https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1901-1950/The-Permanent-Apportionment-Act-of-1929/)), and House size is not a question any state can put to its voters. The last statutory enlargement bill — the Restoring Equal and Accountable Legislators in the House (REAL House) Act, [H.R. 622, 118th Congress](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/622), introduced January 30, 2023 — would have raised the House from 435 to 585 members beginning with the apportionment after the 2030 census, with an automatic mechanism thereafter. It drew two cosponsors, both from one party ([GovTrack cosponsor list](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/118/hr622/cosponsors)), received no committee action or floor vote, and expired with the 118th Congress ([Democracy Docket, Feb. 1, 2023](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/u-s-rep-earl-blumenauer-introduces-bill-to-expand-house-to-585-members/); [American Redistricting Project summary](https://thearp.org/blog/apportionment/restoring-equal-and-accountable-legislators-house-act/)). The two §4.2 evidence tiers below polling — ballot results and cross-party roll calls — are empty here.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The named framings have never been polled.** No survey found asks about the Wyoming Rule, the cube-root rule, or a constitutional ratio by name or description. The gate CATEGORIES.md set on 2026-08-12 was never met, and the rejection does not claim those framings were measured and failed.
- **The one majority reading is the one that cannot carry a verdict.** The 54% is advocacy-commissioned, has no party split, and describes a seat count (150) rather than a principle. Under §4.2 it can serve only as corroboration, and here it points the opposite way from every independent measure.
- **Question format is doing work.** Pew asks increase/decrease/same, offering a status-quo option; Verasight asks support/oppose, which does not. A three-way question with a "keep it the same" answer reads lower than a two-way form on the same underlying opinion, so part of the 29%→54% gap is likely format rather than movement. The two numbers are not comparable, and no independent pollster has run the support/oppose form.
- **Not regime-coded.** Nothing ties either party's position to who holds the chamber. The record does show that the modest Democratic edge is information-sensitive (2018: 34%→44% on context) while the Republican number did not move under the same treatment.
- **Salience is very low.** No independent-tier pollster — Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, UMD PPC — has asked about House size in the 2024–2026 window, and Cato/Morning Consult's June 2026 amendment battery of eight ideas ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/how-americans-would-change-the-constitution-new-poll-12152648)) does not include it. Low salience cuts both ways: opinion is unformed and therefore movable, but the 23% is a soft number rather than hardened opposition.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **23% (Republicans)** on increasing the size of the House, Pew July 2023 → **Weak** per §4.1 (< 45%). The corroborating independent readings are 21% R (Pew 2018), unchanged after an information treatment, and 11.3% R when the costs are named (Knowledge Networks, 2006). The Democratic figure — 35% in 2023, 34–44% in 2018, 23.7% in 2006 — is itself below the Moderate band, so this is not a high-topline idea with a partisan floor. **Neither party has ever recorded majority support in an independent poll.** That is a worse pattern than every category rejected on polling: the Electoral College (46R/80D), the self-pardon ban (43R/81D), and qualified immunity (42R/79D) each had one party well above 60%.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *Weak floor* — **the first failing test.** Three independent surveys twenty, eight, and three years apart place the Republican number between 11% and 23%, and no independent reading of either party has reached a majority. There is no high topline hiding the split; the 29% overall is itself the finding.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **tested once, and it failed.** The 2006 Knowledge Networks survey is the only measurement in the record that names the costs — greater expense and a less efficient legislative process — and support fell to **18.9% overall with a Republican floor of 11.3%**, the lowest reading in the file. It is twenty years old, and no survey has named the costs since, so the depth flank on the 54% advocacy reading remains untested.
  - *Framing dependency* — **unresolved, and not resolvable on this record.** The 29%→54% gap between Pew's three-way form and Verasight's support/oppose form is confounded with sponsorship, format, and question content. Until an independent pollster runs the support/oppose form, no framing can be said to clear the bar.
  - *Staleness* — **at risk.** The party-split data end in 2023; the newest reading of any kind is a 2026 advocacy poll with no crosstabs.
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — **does not apply.** There is no high topline to conceal a split.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **cleared.** No evidence that either party's position depends on who holds the chamber.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **not applicable in the usual form** (no "abuse" both parties condemn), but the structural analogue is present: the diagnosis — districts are too big, gerrymandering too easy — has never been measured as a public grievance. Expansion is a remedy for a problem the public has not been asked whether it has.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **open.** The ratio change is real and documented: 65 members at roughly 60,000 constituents each in the first Congress against 435 at 761,169 today ([Pew 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/5-the-electoral-college-congress-and-representation/); [Census 2020](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2023/decennial/c2020br-01.html)), and the American Academy places the United States among the widest gaps between chamber size and the cube root of population in the democratic world (§5). Whether that threatens the country's future or is a good-government improvement is the judgment §5.1 reserves — and the public's own answer, 53% "keep it the same," is not the answer of a country that experiences this as a crisis. Stated, not decided.
  - *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes, and this is the category's strongest result.** The American Academy ran 10,000 simulations at each House size from 435 to 700 seats on 2020 results, applying national and state-level vote shocks, and found minimal partisan effect in either direction: Republicans gain roughly 2–3 points in probability of control between 436 and 524 seats; Democrats' odds rise above 525 seats but "never by more than roughly 2.7 percent"; and "changing the size of the House would not generate a strong partisan shift in either control of the House or the outcome of the Electoral College," with Electoral College effects favoring larger states but showing "no partisan impact" ([American Academy, §6](https://www.amacad.org/ourcommonpurpose/enlarging-the-house/section/6) — a source that advocates enlargement; method and figures published). A rule that shifts control probability two to three points in *both* directions depending on where the number lands is close to the definition of a rule written behind the veil. The category was not closed on symmetry.
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **weak, and the second failing test.** House size has been set by statute since 1911 and 1929; Congress can enlarge the House tomorrow by ordinary legislation, which is what the leading institutional proposal recommends ("a vote of Congress is all that is needed"), and what a bill in the last Congress attempted without a vote. None of the four accepted answers applies: no court has struck an enlargement statute, the Court has not exempted itself, Congress is not structurally barred from acting (unlike term limits, where *Thornton* forces Article V), and the text is not silent — Article I fixes the 30,000 ceiling and the one-per-state floor and leaves the rest to Congress. The entrenchment argument reads §7 backwards: the sequencing rule locks in agreement the country has *already* reached. Here the statute has never been passed, so there is nothing yet to lock in.

**Reading the table.** No framing clears the bar. The plain question has been measured twice by an independent pollster five years apart, and both times it lands at 21–23% among Republicans; the three 2006 framings run from 11.3% to 33.9% among Republicans, and the highest of them is also the most divided. The one majority result is advocacy-commissioned, format-incomparable, and publishes no floor. The abuse-framing-versus-remedy-framing gap that rescues other categories has no analogue here, because the unrepresentative ratio has never been polled as a grievance in its own right; the nearest thing is Pew's 2018 context treatment — the argument in its most favorable form — which moved Republicans by zero.

One framing distinction changes what the amendment *is*: constitutionalizing a *ratio* (Article the First's approach, which produces a chamber of thousands) is not the same as constitutionalizing a *floor or formula* (Wyoming Rule, cube root). Any poll commissioned in future must test the formula, not the slogan.

## 5. Path back

Two conditions, in order. First, a statutory enlargement must be tried: passage of a bill enlarging the House, or a serious cross-party floor vote on one, would supply the §4.2 evidence the file lacks and satisfy the sequencing rule that the why-an-amendment test failed on. Second, an independent national pollster (Pew, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Monmouth, or UMD PPC's deliberative format), with published party crosstabs, must test the mechanism rather than the direction — for example: *"The number of House seats has been fixed at 435 by law since 1929, while the average district has grown to about 760,000 people. Would you favor or oppose setting the size of the House by a fixed formula tied to population — for instance, so that the average district is about the size of the smallest state — which would add roughly 140 seats?"* — asked alongside a costs-named follow-up naming the added members and their salaries and offices, which is the test the 2006 survey failed. The threshold is a **Republican floor of 45% or better**, which would move the category from Weak to Moderate and reopen it; **60% or better in both parties** would make it a target on the polling. Short of a statutory enlargement having been tried, a strong poll alone would argue for the statute, not for the amendment.
