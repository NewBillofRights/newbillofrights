# Statehood for DC and Puerto Rico — Rejected Category Memo

*Candidate 2026-08-06 to 2026-08-18; rejected 2026-08-18 on the founder's decision after the candidate memo. Memo written 2026-08-18 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md); every figure below was verified against the linked source in the candidate memo ([candidates/STATEHOOD_CANDIDATE.md](../candidates/STATEHOOD_CANDIDATE.md)), which is retained for the record.*

**Verdict in one line:** District of Columbia 13% (Republicans) on "making Washington, D.C., a separate state," Yahoo News/YouGov March 2021 — Weak, with only 39% of Democrats in Gallup's 2019 reading — fails the bipartisan floor and the symmetry test; Puerto Rico 41–45% (Republicans) — Weak — fails the why-an-amendment test, because admission is a statute under Article IV, Section 3, and the statutory route stays on the record; the path back is DC representation without statehood, which no independent pollster has ever asked.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The category bundles two jurisdictions that share a grievance — residents who are taxed and drafted but send no voting member to either chamber of Congress — and little else. They differ in law, in evidence, and in what an amendment would have to say.

**Puerto Rico.** Admission is an ordinary statute. [Article IV, Section 3, Clause 1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articleiv) provides that "New states may be admitted by the Congress into this union; but no new states shall be formed or erected within the jurisdiction of any other state." Thirty-seven states have entered under that clause without an amendment. The vehicles are correspondingly statutory: the **Puerto Rico Status Act, H.R. 8393 (117th Congress)**, which would have held a binding plebiscite among statehood, independence, and sovereignty in free association, passed the House **233–191 on December 15, 2022 — Republicans 16–191, Democrats 217–0** ([Clerk, Roll 529](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2022/roll529.xml)). In the current Congress the vehicle is the **Puerto Rico Democratic Self Determination Act, H.R. 9246**, introduced June 10, 2026 by the Resident Commissioner, with **8 cosponsors, all Democrats** ([GovTrack](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hr9246)).

**The District of Columbia.** Here the amendment question is genuine, because two constitutional provisions sit in the way of the statutory route. [Article I, Section 8, Clause 17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei) gives Congress power "to exercise exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever, over such District (not exceeding ten miles square) as may, by cession of particular states, and the acceptance of Congress, become the seat of the government of the United States." The [Twenty-third Amendment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/amendmentxxiii) grants electors to "the District constituting the seat of government," capped at the least populous state's total. The statutory workaround every DC admission bill uses is to shrink the federal district to a capital core containing the Capitol, White House, and principal federal buildings, and admit the remainder as a state — which would leave the Twenty-third Amendment granting up to three electoral votes to a residual district with a very small resident population. That is why DC admission bills pair themselves with an expedited repeal process, and why serious versions of the idea are drafted as an amendment: **repeal the Twenty-third Amendment and provide for the admission of the District's residential territory, or, in the alternative, provide congressional representation for the District's residents directly, without statehood.** The vehicles: **H.R. 51 (117th Congress)** passed the House **216–208 on April 22, 2021, with zero Republican yeas — Republicans 0–208, Democrats 216–0** ([Clerk, Roll 132](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2021/roll132.xml)); its predecessor passed **232–180 on June 26, 2020, also with zero Republican yeas — Republicans 0–178, Democrats 232–1** ([Clerk, Roll 122](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2020/roll122.xml)). In the 119th Congress, [H.R. 51](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hr51) was reintroduced January 3, 2025 and carries **208 cosponsors, all 208 of them Democrats**; the Senate companion, [S. 51](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/s51), was introduced January 9, 2025. Neither has moved.

**Pipeline entry and disposition.** "Statehood for DC and Puerto Rico?" is a founder seed entry (Michael Hamilton) in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) and [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md), independently surfaced in the public-thread mining by u/AstroSardine, u/alabasterskim (whose variant was that territories must be given statehood votes within fifteen years), and u/Kursed_Valeth in [REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md). The category was placed on the candidate list on 2026-08-06 and rejected on 2026-08-18 after the candidate memo. Puerto Rico's statutory route is logged in AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md rather than dropped.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — District of Columbia**

- **Yahoo News/YouGov, fieldwork March 23–25, 2021 (n=1,556 adults).** "Making Washington, D.C., a separate state": **13% R support / 64% R oppose; 64% D support / 13% D oppose; 32% I support / 37% I oppose.** The same article records the movement since July 2020: Democratic support rose 49→64, Republican support fell 23→13, Republican opposition rose 51→64 — [Yahoo News/YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/35011-democrats-support-washington-dc-statehood). This is the floor of record.
- **Gallup, fieldwork June 19–30, 2019 (n=1,018 adults).** "Would you favor or oppose making Washington, D.C., a separate state?" — **29% favor / 64% oppose / 8% no opinion — 15% R / 30% I / 39% D** — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/260129/americans-reject-statehood.aspx). Gallup's trend: 20% favor in 1992 (Yankelovich), 31% in 1989 (Washington Post). The Democratic figure is the decisive detail in the DC file: **the proposal does not reach majority support in the party that sponsors it.**
- **Washington Examiner/YouGov, released November 3, 2020 (n=1,200 registered voters).** DC statehood **46% support / 40% oppose — 71% D / 45% I / 13% R** (78% of Republicans opposed) — [Washington Examiner/YouGov](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/news/2626144/more-support-statehood-for-dc-and-puerto-rico-washington-examiner-yougov-poll-finds/). An independent replication of the 13% Republican figure at a different moment and with a different sample frame.
- **Economist/YouGov, fieldwork April 25–27, 2021 (n=1,500 adult citizens).** DC statehood **36% favor / 37% oppose / 27% unsure** — [Economist/YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/35628-what-americans-think-about-dc-statehood).
- **Morning Consult/Politico, fieldwork April 16–19, 2021 (n=1,992 registered voters).** DC statehood **40% support / 32% oppose — 62% D support; 59% R oppose.** The same survey asked opponents why: **74% said Congress should not grant statehood because "D.C. was never meant to be a state," against 17% citing partisan concerns** (no party breakdown published for that follow-up item) — [Morning Consult/Politico](https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/articles/dc-statehood-puerto-rico-polling).
- **YouGov, fieldwork August 21–22, 2019 (n=1,251 adults).** DC statehood **34% support / 30% oppose / 36% don't know** — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/25128-puerto-rico-washington-dc-statehood).

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — Puerto Rico**

- **YouGov, fieldwork October 29, 2024 (n=7,200 U.S. adults).** "If the residents of Puerto Rico voted in favor of it, would you support or oppose making Puerto Rico a U.S. state?" — **59% support / 16% oppose / 25% not sure; 79% D / 56% I / 41% R** (Republicans: 41 support, 29 oppose, 31 not sure) — [YouGov daily result](https://yougov.com/en-us/daily-results/20241029-e7ac2-2). The newest independent national reading with a party split.
- **Gallup, fieldwork June 19–30, 2019 (n=1,018 adults).** Puerto Rico statehood **66% favor / 27% oppose / 7% no opinion — 45% R / 69% I / 83% D**; Gallup notes the result is "consistent with the 59% to 65% range of public support Gallup has recorded for Puerto Rico statehood since 1962" — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/260744/americans-continue-support-puerto-rico-statehood.aspx). The highest Republican reading in the independent record.
- **Economist/YouGov, fieldwork April 25–27, 2021 (n=1,500 adult citizens).** **43% favor / 31% oppose — 63% D / 45% I / 21% R** (58% of Republicans opposed) — [Economist/YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/35628-what-americans-think-about-dc-statehood).
- **YouGov, fieldwork August 21–22, 2019 (n=1,251 adults).** **57% completely or somewhat support / 18% oppose — 77% D / 56% I / 43% R.** In the same survey, support for Puerto Rico *voting for President* drew **38% R / 47% I / 77% D** — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/25128-puerto-rico-washington-dc-statehood).
- **Washington Examiner/YouGov, released November 3, 2020 (n=1,200 registered voters).** **61% support / 27% oppose — 86% D / 61% I / 29% R** (61% of Republicans opposed) — [Washington Examiner/YouGov](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/news/2626144/more-support-statehood-for-dc-and-puerto-rico-washington-examiner-yougov-poll-finds/).
- **Morning Consult/Politico, fieldwork April 16–19, 2021 (n=1,992 registered voters).** **45% support / 52% oppose — 26% R support / 52% R oppose** — [Morning Consult/Politico](https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/articles/dc-statehood-puerto-rico-polling). The only independent reading in the record where Puerto Rico statehood draws net national opposition.

**The newest national reading, covering both**

- **Strength In Numbers/Verasight, fieldwork February 18–20, 2026 (n=1,566 U.S. adults, ±2.5).** A five-item structural-reform battery. **DC statehood 32% support / 36% oppose / 32% not sure** — the only item in the battery where opposition exceeded support, and the only one net-negative among independents. **Puerto Rico statehood 51% support / 24% oppose.** Party figures are published as **net support (support minus oppose), not raw support**, and therefore set no floor: **DC net −25 R / −6 I / +15 D; Puerto Rico net −3 R / +21 I / +55 D** — [Strength In Numbers](https://www.gelliottmorris.com/p/two-thirds-of-americans-want-term); [topline release and methodology](https://www.gelliottmorris.com/p/ahead-of-state-of-the-union-address). The pollster attributes the 19-point gap between the two jurisdictions to "the perceived electoral consequences of each new state."

**Ballot results**

- **Puerto Rico status plebiscite, November 5, 2024** (certified by the Comisión Estatal de Elecciones January 17, 2025): **statehood 620,782 (58.61%), sovereignty in free association 313,259 (29.57%), independence 125,171 (11.82%)** of 1,059,212 valid votes; **181,200 ballots (14.34%) blank** and 23,141 invalid, of 1,263,553 cast and 1,987,317 registered — measured against all ballots cast rather than valid votes, statehood drew closer to 49% — [Ballotpedia, citing the CEE](https://ballotpedia.org/Puerto_Rico_Statehood,_Independence,_or_Free_Association_Referendum_(2024)); [Wikipedia summary of the CEE certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Puerto_Rican_status_referendum); the official [CEE certification PDF](https://ww2.ceepur.org/sites/ComisionEE/es-pr/Certificaciones/Certification%20of%20final%20results%20-%20Plebiscite%202024.pdf) confirms the certification text, signatories and date, but its vote-count table is a scanned image and was not text-verified, so the counts rest on the transcriptions above.
- **Puerto Rico, November 3, 2020.** "Should Puerto Rico be admitted immediately into the Union as a State?" — **Yes 655,505 (52.52%) / No 592,671 (47.48%)**, turnout 54.72% of 2,355,894 registered voters — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Puerto_Rican_status_referendum). Non-binding; the U.S. Department of Justice declined to approve it, in part because the ballot offered no option to continue as a territory.
- **District of Columbia advisory referendum, November 8, 2016.** **Yes 244,134 (85.69%) / No 40,779 (14.31%)** of 284,913 valid votes, on turnout of 64.98% — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Washington,_D.C.,_statehood_referendum). Advisory, and a vote of the affected residents only: it measures local demand, not national consent, and is not evidence under §4.2 for a national floor.

**Legislative votes.** The roll calls in §1 are the cleanest cross-party evidence available, and they point in opposite directions: **16 Republican yeas on the Puerto Rico process bill; zero Republican yeas on either DC admission bill**, across two Congresses, with 208 of 208 current DC cosponsors from one party.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The polls measure different things.** Every high Puerto Rico number is conditional — "if the residents voted in favor of it" (YouGov 2024, 2019). The unconditional Gallup ask is 66% overall / 45% R. The DC questions are unconditional and blunt: "making Washington, D.C., a separate state."
- **Staleness is largely relieved, and the direction holds.** The DC floor of record is 2021, but the February 2026 reading is five years newer and consistent with it — DC net −25 among Republicans, net −6 among independents, and only +15 among Democrats — while reporting net rather than raw support, so it confirms the file without setting a floor.
- **Regime coding is visible in the trend.** Yahoo/YouGov's own year-over-year comparison shows both parties moving in opposite directions by double digits (D 49→64, R 23→13) across the eight months spanning a change in Senate control and an active House vote. The numbers track the expected seat result, not a stated principle about representation.
- **The 2026 evidence beyond the national reading is not national.** McLaughlin & Associates, June 5–16, 2026, commissioned by the Puerto Rico Statehood Council (advocacy, flagged), surveyed 1,000 Latino likely voters nationally plus a Puerto Rican oversample: **68% of Hispanic voters and 71% of Puerto Rican respondents** support statehood, rising to 75% and 79% after pro-statehood messaging — [The Latino Newsletter](https://thelatinonewsletter.org/p/new-poll-about-statehood-for-puerto-rico). No party breakdown of the national electorate; it cannot carry a verdict.
- **What has never been asked.** No independent national poll found asks either jurisdiction's question in amendment form, in forward-looking both-parties wording, or against the alternative remedies — congressional representation without statehood, or retrocession to Maryland for the District. No poll found asks about repealing the Twenty-third Amendment.

## 3. The gate result

Two jurisdictions, two gates, two different failing tests. They are taken separately.

- **Bipartisan floor — District of Columbia:** **13% (Republicans)** on "making Washington, D.C., a separate state," Yahoo News/YouGov March 2021 → **Weak** per §4.1, and the weakest floor anywhere in the corpus. Independently replicated at **13% R** (Washington Examiner/YouGov, November 2020) and bracketed at **15% R** (Gallup, June 2019). Gallup's survey adds the finding that closes the question: **39% of Democrats** favored it — the proposal does not command a majority in the party that sponsors it, so there is no bipartisan floor left to measure.
- **Bipartisan floor — Puerto Rico:** **41% (Republicans)** on statehood conditional on residents voting for it, YouGov October 2024 → **Weak** per §4.1, four points below the Moderate band. The best Republican reading ever recorded by an independent pollster is **45%** (Gallup, June 2019) — still Weak. Across six independent readings the range is **21%–45% R** against **63%–86% D**; gaps run 34 to 57 points.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — the defining polling failure for Puerto Rico: 59% and 66% toplines conceal 41% R and 45% R.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — the Yahoo/YouGov movement from July 2020 to March 2021 (D +15, R −10) tracks the expected seat result across a change in Senate control and an active House vote; the February 2026 pollster attributes the DC/PR gap to "the perceived electoral consequences of each new state."
  - *Framing dependency* — real but does not rescue either jurisdiction; see §4.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — untested. No independent poll found names the costs (permanent alteration of Senate composition; the residual federal district) and re-asks.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — does not apply cleanly. The category has no abuse pole; it has a grievance whose remedy is contested. The closest analogue is Morning Consult's finding that 74% of DC-statehood opponents object that "D.C. was never meant to be a state" — an objection to the remedy's form rather than to representation as such.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Symmetry (5.2) — **fails for the District**, and this is the second named test in the rejection.* The rule cannot be written behind a veil of ignorance, because admission is a one-time act with a known partisan result rather than a rule binding future actors equally. The method's symmetry devices — prospective effective dates, grandfathering, paired examples — have no purchase on a single act of admission. The roll-call record shows the same shape: zero Republican yeas across two House passages.
  - *Why-an-amendment (5.3) — **fails for Puerto Rico**, and this is the named test in the rejection.* None of the four accepted answers applies: no court struck a statute, the Court exempted nothing, Congress can and does self-bind here, and the text is not silent — Article IV, Section 3 says exactly how a state is admitted, and thirty-seven states have used it. Under §5.3 an idea that is genuinely statutory is flagged and parked rather than drafted, so the statutory route stays on the record in AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md. For the District the answer runs the other way and is real — the Twenty-third Amendment can be repealed only by amendment — which is why the District is rejected on the floor and the symmetry test rather than on this one.
  - *Existential-problem (5.1) — open, and stronger for the District than for Puerto Rico.* Roughly 700,000 U.S. citizens taxed without voting representation in either chamber is a democracy claim rather than a policy preference, and it is the strongest thing in the file; it is what the path back in §5 is built on. It is weaker for Puerto Rico only in that residents can resolve their own status question among statehood, independence, and free association, and have not converged: 58.61% of valid votes but roughly 49% of ballots cast in 2024, on a ballot 14.34% of voters left blank.

**No framing clears the bar for either jurisdiction.** The best-case framings show where the ceiling sits. Data for Progress — an advocacy pollster, testing its own best arguments on likely voters, in the highest-salience week the issue has had — reached **42% among Republicans** with the taxation-without-representation frame, and its own memo names that as the highest Republican share it found ([Data for Progress / Democracy for All 2021 Action, fieldwork Feb. 19–22, 2021, n=1,526 likely voters, web panel, ±3](https://www.filesforprogress.org/datasets/2021/3/DFA-DFP-DC-Statehood.pdf)). The same PDF's crosstab appendix gives the plain topline: on "granting statehood to the more than 714,000 people who live in Washington D.C., so they can elect voting Senators and Representatives," **54% support / 35% oppose / 11% don't know — Republicans 34% support (17 strongly, 17 somewhat) against 56% opposed, independents 51%, Democrats 74% (45 strongly)**, with Republican opposition concentrated at the intense end (38% strongly opposed, against 6% of Democrats). Those figures were separately reported by [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/washington-dc-statehood-national-support-congress/). An advocacy poll's best framing landing eighteen points below the Strong threshold is a ceiling finding, not a floor finding.

For Puerto Rico the framing gap runs the wrong way for the category. The conditional wording ("if the residents voted in favor of it") is what produces 41% R and 79% D; the same YouGov survey found that 69% of Americans, including 61% of Republicans, correctly identified Puerto Ricans as U.S. citizens, with 20% of Republicans saying they are not — a knowledge gap adjacent to the status question, not evidence for the mechanism. Within a single 2019 survey, statehood drew 43% of Republicans while Puerto Rico *voting for President* drew 38%: when the question makes the federal-power consequence explicit, the Republican number falls.

The framing that has never been tested is the one that decides whether the category can exist as an amendment at all: **representation without statehood.** No independent poll found asks whether the District's residents should have voting members of Congress without becoming a state, or whether Puerto Rico's status should be resolved by a binding federal process. Those are the two questions on which a bipartisan number is conceivable, and both are unpolled.

## 5. Path back

**District of Columbia.** The category reopens if an independent national pollster (Pew, Gallup, Marquette, AP-NORC, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac) with 2026-or-later fieldwork asks, in forward-looking both-parties wording, whether the roughly 700,000 U.S. citizens who live in the District should have voting representation in the House and Senate — with a follow-up naming the mechanism (statehood; representation by constitutional amendment without statehood; or retrocession to Maryland) — and the **remedy**, not merely the grievance, clears **60% among Republicans**. A cross-party roll call in either chamber on any DC representation measure, or a red- or purple-state legislature memorializing Congress for DC representation, would count as §4.2 evidence on the same question. The route is narrow but specific: the strongest finding in the file is that opponents mostly object to statehood's form rather than to representation itself (74% "D.C. was never meant to be a state" against 17% citing partisan concerns, Morning Consult 2021), and "representation without statehood" has never been polled.

**Puerto Rico.** No poll reopens Puerto Rico as an amendment category. The failing test is §5.3, not §4.1: Article IV, Section 3 already supplies the mechanism, so a stronger floor would move the idea up the statutory list rather than onto the platform. The category returns as an amendment only if the statutory route is foreclosed — a holding that Congress cannot admit Puerto Rico by ordinary law would be such a change, and none exists.
