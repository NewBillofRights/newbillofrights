# Presidential Self-Pardon Ban — Rejected Category Memo

*Reopened 2026-08-18 as a candidate clause — see [candidates/clauses/PARDON_SELF_PARDON_CLAUSE.md](../candidates/clauses/PARDON_SELF_PARDON_CLAUSE.md); this memo is retained as the 2026-08-06 rejection record.*

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 3 corrections applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Verdict in one line:** 43% R (YouGov, Aug 2024, "prohibiting a president from pardoning themself") — Weak floor; fails high-topline/low-floor, incumbent/beneficiary coding, and abuse-vs-remedy gap (§4.3) — path back is a forward-looking, both-parties re-poll clearing ~60% R, the first poll the foundation intends to commission on the pardon package.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The idea is a single sentence added to Article II: *the President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to himself or herself.* It is the narrowest of the pardon-limiting proposals and the only one whose legal answer may already be "unconstitutional" without any amendment. The Office of Legal Counsel's memorandum of August 5, 1974 (Mary C. Lawton, Acting Assistant Attorney General) concluded that "under the fundamental rule that no one may be a judge in his own case, the President cannot pardon himself," while noting the Twenty-Fifth Amendment route by which an Acting President could pardon a temporarily disabled President ([DOJ OLC, 1974](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president); [PDF](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/olc/opinions/1974/08/31/op-olc-supp-v001-p0370_0.pdf)). An OLC opinion binds no court, no president of either party has attempted a self-pardon, and no court has ruled. An amendment would settle a question the text does not expressly address — which is the foundation's fourth accepted answer to the why-an-amendment test (§5.3).

Variants in circulation:

- **Standalone ban** — the sentence above. This is what the polls test.
- **Bundled ban** — the modern legislative standard is Rep. Steve Cohen's serial joint resolution, introduced in every Congress since 2017. The current text, [H.J.Res.13 (119th Cong., introduced Jan. 9, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hjres13/BILLS-119hjres13ih.htm), bars pardons of "the President's self," relatives to the third degree, current or former administration members, paid campaign staff, offenses motivated by a "direct and significant personal or pecuniary interest" of those persons, offenses "at the direction of, or in coordination with, the President," and voids any pardon "issued for a corrupt purpose." Sponsors: Cohen, Johnson (GA), Levin — all one party. Cohen's own announcement of the original [H.J.Res.120 (2018 press release)](https://cohen.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/congressman-cohen-will-reintroduce-constitutional-amendment-limit) frames it around a specific administration, which is the packaging the foundation exists to avoid.
- **Consequence rule rather than ban** — leave the power's scope alone but treat a self-pardon as an impeachable act. This is what the 2018 pollsters actually asked about (below); no one has drafted it as text.

**How it entered our pipeline.** The category surfaced in the 2026-08-06 polling gap search ([AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md), "Rejected Ideas — Presidential Self-Pardon Ban"). Separately, **Michael Hamilton**'s "Restricting Self-Serving Pardons" proposal in the same file — amend Article II to ban self-pardons and clemency for immediate family, co-defendants, campaign associates, and subjects of investigations touching the president — included the self-pardon ban as one component. The family, sale, naming, and public-justification components became Target Category 8 (Limited Pardon Reform); the self-pardon component alone was rejected. It survives partially inside the target's "shielding the President from legal liability" clause and is listed as held back in [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md).

## 2. The evidence

Best evidence first. Note that only one independent national poll has ever asked about *prohibiting* self-pardons with a party breakdown; everything else measures the norm ("appropriate?"), the consequence ("impeach?"), or a named individual.

- **YouGov (independent), Aug. 15–19, 2024, n=1,130 U.S. adult citizens.** "Would you support or oppose… prohibiting a president from pardoning themself?" Support **64%** (50% strongly, 14% somewhat; YouGov's write-up rounds to 65%), oppose 18%, not sure 17%. By party: **Democrats 81%** (71 strongly / 10 somewhat; write-up rounds to 82%), **Independents 67%**, **Republicans 43%** (24 strongly / 19 somewhat), with 29% of Republicans opposed and **27% not sure** — [crosstabs PDF, table 7B](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results.pdf); [write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50378-presidential-pardons-which-ones-do-americans-approve-of). **This is the floor number.** Fieldwork was under a Democratic president, three months before the election, while the Republican nominee faced pending federal indictments — the question read as being about one specific potential beneficiary.
- **Same YouGov survey, the norm question.** "Do you think it is appropriate or inappropriate for a president to pardon… themself?" **Inappropriate 77%** overall — **Democrats 88% / Independents 77% / Republicans 63%**; appropriate 11% (Republicans 18%) — [table 5A, same PDF](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results.pdf). Twenty points separate Republicans' view of the *act* (63% inappropriate) from their support for the *ban* (43%).
- **Same YouGov survey, the knowledge question.** "Does the president have the power to pardon… themselves for a federal conviction?" Yes 26% / No 38% / Not sure 37% (Republicans 23 / 35 / 42) — [table 1A](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results.pdf). Most Americans do not know the answer, which is what an amendment would supply.
- **AP-NORC (independent), June 13–18, 2018, n=1,109 adults, AmeriSpeak panel.** "Do you think it would be acceptable or unacceptable for presidents to pardon themselves if charged with a crime?" **Unacceptable 85%**, acceptable 13%; and 76% said Congress "should take steps to remove the president from office" if a president self-pardons — [AP-NORC topline PDF, CORR2–CORR3](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/June-2018-AP-NORC-Poll-Topline_Release2.pdf); [AP-NORC project page](https://apnorc.org/projects/american-attitudes-toward-international-relations/). The topline publishes no party split; the AP write-up reports **about 75% of Republicans** saying presidents should not self-pardon if charged and **56% of Republicans** favoring removal, with Democrats above 90% on both — [AP via PBS NewsHour, June 22, 2018](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/most-americans-oppose-presidential-self-pardons-poll-finds) (news write-up used because the pollster's topline omits the breakdown). Fieldwork followed the sitting president's public assertion of an "absolute right" to self-pardon; no charge was pending. This is a norm/consequence measure, not a ban question.
- **YouGov/Economist write-up dated July 23, 2018 (independent).** Americans reject the president's power to pardon himself "by nearly three to one"; "more than a quarter of Republicans say the President does have that power, though more disagree"; on impeachment for a self-pardon, "a quarter of Republicans agree, a third disagree and the rest aren't sure" — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/21224-most-think-president-cant-pardon-himself) (no full party table published in the write-up).
- **YouGov/Economist, Dec. 19–22, 2020, n=1,500 (independent).** Power to pardon himself: No 51% / Yes 19%; Republicans 34% yes / 30% no — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/33523-americans-are-divided-whether-president-can-pardon). Knowledge, not preference.
- **Named-individual polls (regime-coded by construction; no party breakdown published in either release):** ABC News/Washington Post, Jan. 10–13, 2021, n=1,002: "Would you support or oppose Trump issuing a presidential pardon to himself…?" **Oppose 68% / support 28%** — [Langer Research PDF, Q16](https://www.langerresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/1219a2TransitiontoBiden.pdf); Yahoo News/YouGov, June 16–20, 2023, n=1,626: **55% would disapprove / 28% approve** of a self-pardon by the then-former president if convicted and re-elected — [Yahoo News write-up](https://www.yahoo.com/news/can-trump-pardon-himself-if-he-wins-in-2024-202946662.html) (news write-up; crosstabs not located). These measure attitudes toward one person, not toward a rule, and are excluded from the floor.
- **Generic amendment wrapper (independent).** "Do you think the Constitution should be amended to limit the president's ability to grant pardons?" **Yes 56% / No 25%** — **Democrats 76% / Independents 58% / Republicans 34%** — [Economist/YouGov, Dec. 5–8, 2025, n=1,530, table 46](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_qLhWxpK.pdf); [write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53702-majorities-americans-say-donald-trump-gives-too-many-pardons-want-to-limit-pardon-power-december-5-8-2025-economist-yougov-poll). **This poll contains no self-pardon item** (see Nuances).
- **Legislative record.** No self-pardon amendment has received a hearing or markup in any Congress; every sponsor of the current vehicle is from one party ([H.J.Res.13 text](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hjres13/BILLS-119hjres13ih.htm)). No ballot measure exists; the pardon power is federal.
- **Advocacy-commissioned (flagged).** YouGov for Protect Democracy, May–June 2024, n=1,200 validated voters in 43 battleground House districts (not a national sample). Respondents disagreeing that "presidents should pardon themselves": **78%** overall — **Republicans 59% / Independents 76% / Democrats 96%** — [Protect Democracy](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/survey-jan-6-pardon-opposition/). This is a norm statement, not a ban proposal; it is the highest Republican number in the record and cannot carry a verdict alone (§4.2).

**Nuances & caveats**

- **Corpus attribution error, corrected here.** The 82% D / 43% R figure that appears in CATEGORIES.md, POLLING_RESEARCH.md, PARDON_RESEARCH.md, and the pardon-reform page footnote is attributed to Economist/YouGov Dec. 5–8, 2025. The Dec. 2025 crosstabs contain no self-pardon question; the figure comes from YouGov's standalone Aug. 15–19, 2024 pardons survey (table 7B, rounded from 81/43). The floor is unchanged (43% R, Weak), but the corpus narrative that Republican support "fell from 59% (2024) to 43% (Dec. 2025)" across an administration change is not supported: both measurements are mid-2024 fieldwork, and the 16-point gap is a wording-and-sample difference (norm statement among battleground voters vs. ban proposal among national adults), not movement over time.
- **A second attribution error.** The "~75% of Republicans, 2018" figure is labeled PBS/NPR/Marist in POLLING_RESEARCH.md; the PBS page is an AP story on the AP-NORC June 2018 poll, and the party split appears only in the AP write-up, not in the AP-NORC topline.
- **Regime and beneficiary coding, stated precisely.** Republican numbers on the same underlying norm ran ~75% (AP-NORC 2018, no charge pending, self-pardon hypothetical) → 63% inappropriate / 43% for a ban (YouGov Aug. 2024, indictments pending against the party's nominee). The number moved with *who the plausible beneficiary was*, not simply with who held the White House. A ban polled today would be read the same way; a ban polled after 2028 might not. That is the definition of an unstable floor (§4.3, incumbent/regime coding; §11, "same principle, different incumbent").
- **Soft opposition.** In the floor poll, Republican opposition to a ban is 29%, with 27% unsure — the "not sure" share is the largest of any party. The 43% is a Weak floor with headroom, not a hard ceiling.
- **Two-year gap.** No independent poll since Aug. 2024 has asked about a self-pardon ban; the 2025 waves (YouGov Jan.–Feb. 2025 acceptability battery; Protect Democracy Jan. 2025; Economist/YouGov Dec. 2025) omitted it. The norm has been polled since 2018; the *ban* has one data point.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **43% (Republicans)** on "prohibiting a president from pardoning themself," YouGov Aug. 15–19, 2024 → **Weak** per §4.1. Topline 64%; Democrats 81%; independents 67%.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — 64% overall, 81 D / 43 R: a 38-point party gap on the only independent ban question.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding — here, beneficiary coding* — Republican support for the norm ran ~75% in 2018 with no charge pending and 63%/43% in 2024 with the party's nominee under indictment; the Republican floor tracks the identity of the likely beneficiary.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — 63% of Republicans call a self-pardon inappropriate; 43% back the prohibition (same survey, same week). Both parties oppose the act; only one supports the fix.
  - *Framing dependency* — the norm ("presidents should not pardon themselves") reaches 59% R in advocacy battleground polling and ~75% R in 2018 independent polling; the remedy ("prohibit") reaches 43% R. The framing that clears is not the text we would draft.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):** The category **passes** all three. *Existential-problem:* a rule that no official may be judge in his own case is the core of the mission's Rule-of-Law pillar. *Symmetry:* a self-pardon ban is symmetric by construction — no president of either party has attempted one, and the text binds all future presidents identically. *Why-an-amendment:* the constitutional text does not expressly address a self-pardon (the Constitution Annotated calls the question "unresolved," with textual arguments on both sides — see Review Additions), the 1974 OLC opinion binds no court, and Congress cannot restrict the pardon power by statute (*Ex parte Garland*; *Schick v. Reed*, both cited in [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md)). **The rejection is entirely a polling result.** That is why the category is held rather than dismissed, and why the pardon target's "shielding the President from legal liability" clause carries the principle in the meantime.

**Reading of the table.** Two framings clear the Strong bar for Republicans — "inappropriate" (63%) and the 2018 "unacceptable if charged" (~75%) — and both are statements about the act, not the rule. The one framing that is honestly the thing we would draft ("prohibit") is Weak. The advocacy norm number (59% R) suggests the remedy *could* poll near Moderate nationally, but it was a battleground-voter sample and a "should," not a "prohibit." No framing rescues the category today. What the table does show is that the norm is bipartisan and durable — the failure lies in the remedy wording and the timing of measurement — framing dependency and the abuse-vs-remedy gap, the two §4.3 failure modes for which the method names a path back.

## 5. Path back

**Named poll, named threshold.** Commission an independent national poll (Pew, AP-NORC, Marquette, or YouGov under the foundation's own instrument) that asks the ban in forward-looking, both-parties wording — *"No future president, of either party, may pardon himself or herself"* — with the family, sale, naming, and public-justification provisions of the pardon target asked in the same instrument, so the self-pardon clause is measured as a rule for everyone rather than as a verdict on one person. **Reopen the category if the Republican share supporting the ban reaches ~60% (Strong) with Democrats holding above 60% — i.e., a floor at or above the pardon target's own 64% R family-ban anchor.** A Moderate result (45–59% R) with the "not sure" share collapsing toward support would justify carrying the explicit ban inside the pardon package rather than as a standalone article, matching how the associates clause is treated. This is the first poll the foundation intends to commission on the pardon package: it resolves the self-pardon question and, per the Review Additions in [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md), re-bases the family ban's Democratic floor in the same forward-looking wording.

**Two other triggers.** (1) An independent national replication of the Protect Democracy 59% R norm figure would move the norm from advocacy-only to independent evidence and justify testing the ban framing again. (2) A court ruling on an actual self-pardon — in either direction — would change the why-an-amendment answer from "text is silent" to "text is settled" or "text is settled the wrong way," and would need to be re-run through §5.3.

Absent those, the category stays rejected: the principle rides inside the pardon target's self-benefit clause, and the explicit sentence waits for a floor measured behind the veil.
