# Eliminate Qualified Immunity for Policing — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Verdict in one line:** 42% (Republicans) on "eliminate qualified immunity so police can be sued," Cato/YouGov July 2020 — Weak; fails high-topline/low-floor, staleness, and framing-dependency, and the why-an-amendment test is weak because the doctrine is statutory — path back is fresh independent fieldwork showing the accountability framing holds ≥60% among Republicans when attached to the actual mechanism.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

Qualified immunity is a judge-made defense to civil-rights damages suits — suits under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (enacted 1871) against state and local officers, and *Bivens* suits against federal officers. The Supreme Court set the modern test in [*Harlow v. Fitzgerald* (1982)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/457/800/): officials are shielded unless they violated "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." [*Pearson v. Callahan* (2009)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/555/223/) let courts grant immunity without ever deciding whether the conduct was unconstitutional — the mechanism critics say keeps the law from becoming "clearly established" in the first place. Because the doctrine is a judicial gloss on the damages remedy (the § 1983 statute for state and local officers; the judge-made *Bivens* action for federal officers, see Review Additions) rather than on the Constitution's substantive guarantees, every serious proposal to end it has been a bill, not an amendment:

- **Ending Qualified Immunity Act, H.R. 7085 (116th Cong., June 2020)** — the first federal abolition bill; sponsor was a Libertarian, and its 66 cosponsors were 65 Democrats and 1 Republican — [congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/house-bill/7085/cosponsors).
- **George Floyd Justice in Policing Act, H.R. 1280 (117th Cong.)** — abolished QI for law enforcement as one section of an omnibus; passed the House **220–212 on March 3, 2021** — [congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/1280/all-actions) — with **1 Republican yea and 210 nays; Democrats 219–2** — [Clerk, Roll 60](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2021/roll060.xml). Never voted on in the Senate.
- **119th Congress, both directions:** the *Qualified Immunity Act of 2025* (S. 122, Jan. 2025) would **codify** the doctrine by statute — [congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/122); the *Qualified Immunity Abolition Act of 2026* (S. 3625, Jan. 2026) would end it — [congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/3625). Both sit in the Senate Judiciary Committee; the codification bill was introduced by a Republican senator, the abolition bill by a Democratic senator.
- **States have already done it by statute.** Colorado's [SB 20-217](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb20-217) (signed June 19, 2020; House 52–13, Senate 32–1) created a state civil action in which "qualified immunity is not a defense," with a $25,000 / 5% officer-liability cap and mandatory indemnification. New Mexico's Civil Rights Act (signed [April 7, 2021](https://www.governor.state.nm.us/2021/04/07/gov-lujan-grisham-ratifies-civil-rights-act/)) bars the defense for suits under the state bill of rights, effective July 1, 2021, with a $2 million cap. Connecticut's 2020 HB 6004 created a related cause of action — [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/civil-and-criminal-justice/qualified-immunity).

**Pipeline entry.** "Eliminate Qualified Immunity for Policing" was one of the founder's four seed categories (Michael Hamilton; see [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)), with no draft text beyond elimination. As an amendment it would read roughly: *no officer of government shall be immune from civil liability for violating a person's constitutional rights on the ground that the right had not previously been clearly established.* Rejected 2026-08-06 with the note that the accountability framing "could return as a target if reframed around accountability and validated with fresh polling" ([CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md)).

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Cato Institute / YouGov, July 1–6, 2020 (n=2,000 adults).** "Eliminate qualified immunity so that police officers can be sued for misconduct even if there is no previous legal case with similar facts": **63% favor / 37% oppose — 42% R / 64% I / 79% D**; **58% of Republicans oppose**. Same survey: police officers should be "held accountable for misconduct even if they were unaware at the time that their actions were illegal" — **79% overall — 64% R / 75% I / 91% D**; and 77% say police should not be able to avoid lawsuits using ignorance of the law as a defense (no party split published for that item). Cato is a libertarian think tank that advocates ending QI; the fieldwork is YouGov's and crosstabs are published — [Cato/YouGov](https://www.cato.org/survey-reports/poll-63-americans-favor-eliminating-qualified-immunity-police).
- **Pew Research Center, June 16–22, 2020 (n=4,708 adults).** Forced choice: "civilians need to have the power to sue police officers to hold them accountable for misconduct and excessive use of force, even if that makes the officers' jobs more difficult" vs. "in order for police officers to do their jobs effectively, they need to be shielded from such lawsuits" — **66% / 32% — 45% R (incl. leaners) / 84% D (incl. leaners)** (no separate independent figure). Same survey, for contrast: a federal misconduct database 85% R / 94% D; civilian oversight boards 58% R / 89% D — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2020/07/09/majority-of-public-favors-giving-civilians-the-power-to-sue-police-officers-for-misconduct/). A forced-choice share is not a standalone favor/oppose number (SELECTION_METHOD §11); it is reported here as a bound, not a floor.
- **UMD Program for Public Consultation, July 2–9, 2020 (n=3,226 RV, deliberative — respondents were briefed and read pro/con arguments).** "Amending qualified immunity" so officers cannot be granted immunity solely because they believed their conduct lawful or because no prior case matched: **63% favor — 41% R (58% R oppose) / 64% I / 84% D**; on a 0–10 acceptability scale **56% of Republicans** rated it at least "tolerable" (5+), vs. 88% of Democrats. Companion items in the same survey drew majority Republican support (misconduct registry 70% R, duty to intervene 71% R, chokehold ban 55% R) — [PPC report PDF](https://www.publicconsultation.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/PoliceReformReport0720.pdf); [press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/large-majorities-favor-police-reforms-in-congressional-bills-new-umd-survey-finds-301093289.html).
- **UMass Amherst / YouGov, April 2021 → May 5–9, 2022 (n=1,000 each).** "Allow citizens to sue individual police officers that are accused of the excessive use of physical force or misconduct": **59% → 54% support**; by party **R 30% → 28%, I 52% → 51%, D 84% → 77%** — [toplines](https://www.umass.edu/poll/documents/may-2022-national-poll-toplines), [crosstabs](https://www.umass.edu/poll/documents/may-2022-national-poll-crosstabs), [release](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/new-national-umass-amherst-poll-finds-decreasing-support-police-reform-black-lives). This is the newest independent national reading with a party split that could be found; it is also the lowest Republican number in the record.
- **Morning Consult, June 19–21, 2020 (n=1,988 RV).** Ending qualified immunity and allowing civil suits against officers: **45% of Republicans support / 40% oppose** (overall and Democratic figures not published on the page; the article calls it the most divisive item in a battery where chokehold and no-knock bans drew 60% and 52% of Republicans) — [Morning Consult](https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/articles/polling-policing-reform-chokehold-floyd).

**Legislative votes and state action**

- House passage of the omnibus containing QI abolition: 220–212, Republicans 1–210 (March 2021) — [Clerk, Roll 60](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2021/roll060.xml). Not a cross-party vote.
- Colorado SB 20-217 (52–13 / 32–1, June 2020) and New Mexico HB 4 (April 2021) — enacted by statute in states with Democratic legislative majorities; no state has put QI abolition to voters, so there is no ballot result to weigh — [Colorado](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb20-217), [New Mexico](https://www.governor.state.nm.us/2021/04/07/gov-lujan-grisham-ratifies-civil-rights-act/).

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **Data for Progress (progressive), April 2–5, 2021 (n=1,209 LV).** "Ending the practice of 'qualified immunity'" as one item in a list of congressional reforms: **59% support / 27% oppose — 46% R (38% oppose) / 59% I / 73% D** — [dataset PDF](https://www.filesforprogress.org/datasets/2021/4/dfp-vox-police-reform.pdf).
- **Data for Progress / The Appeal (progressive), April 23–25, 2021 (n=1,189 LV).** After the Chauvin verdict, with QI described as leaving officers "mostly immune from civil suits": **56% support — 39% R / 55% I / 73% D** — [The Appeal](https://theappeal.org/the-lab/polling-memos/voters-support-broad-reforms-to-scope-of-police-work-and-accountability-after-chauvin-verdict/).
- **Data for Progress, Feb. 23–25, 2021 (n=1,182 LV).** The Justice in Policing Act described by its non-QI provisions (registry, profiling ban, chokehold ban, body cameras): **74% — 55% R / 78% I / 88% D**; after a one-paragraph counter-argument, **62% — 34% R / 86% D** — [DFP](https://www.dataforprogress.org/blog/2021/3/12/voters-support-justice-in-policing-act), [dataset](https://www.filesforprogress.org/datasets/2021/3/dfp-george-floyd-justice-in-policing.pdf). QI was not named in the question, so this is package evidence, not QI evidence.

**Nuances & caveats**

- Every Republican reading on the *elimination / right-to-sue* mechanism falls between **28% and 46%** (five pollsters, 2020–2022); every Democratic reading falls between 73% and 84%. Gaps run 27–56 points.
- All headline numbers are from June 2020–April 2021, the peak-salience window after the George Floyd killing. The only re-ask found (UMass, May 2022) shows the topline down 5 points and the Republican share at 28%. Searches for 2023–2026 national fieldwork on QI or suing police from Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, Cato, and Morning Consult returned nothing (see §5). The most recent academic entry — an October 2020 MTurk convenience sample published in *Policing* (2026), finding a 45/46 split on keeping QI and support for suits varying by scenario — is not a national probability sample and is not used for the verdict — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/policing/article/doi/10.1093/police/paag021/8688018).
- The 79% / 64% R accountability figure is a *principle* ("held accountable … even if unaware"), asked in the same survey where the *mechanism* (eliminate QI so officers can be sued) drew 42% R. That 22-point gap inside one sample is the abuse-vs-remedy pattern, not a mismeasurement.
- Deliberation and information do not close the gap: PPC's briefed respondents landed at 41% R, the same as Cato's unbriefed sample; 74% of Republicans found the anti-reform argument convincing (57% found the pro argument convincing).
- Cato's own summary is candid that support is soft: 55% of respondents agreed that ending QI could leave police "too afraid to do their jobs effectively" — a costs-named framing that has never been run head-to-head with the elimination question by an independent pollster.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **42% (Republicans)** on "eliminate qualified immunity so police can be sued," Cato/YouGov July 2020 → **Weak** per §4.1. Corroborating floors: 45% R (Pew 2020, forced choice), 41% R (PPC 2020, deliberative), 46% and 39% R (DFP 2021, advocacy), 30% → 28% R (UMass 2021 → 2022). No reading of the mechanism reaches the Moderate band.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — 63–66% overall hides a 42–45% R / 79–84% D split (Cato, Pew 2020).
  - *Staleness* — every credible number is from June 2020–May 2022; the one re-poll declined (59→54, R 30→28); nothing since. This is the failure mode the 2026-08-06 rejection named, and the 2026 search confirms it.
  - *Framing dependency* — the accountability principle clears 60% R (64%); the elimination mechanism does not (42%). The framing that clears the bar is not the thing an "eliminate qualified immunity" amendment would draft.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — both parties reject "ignorance of the law" as an excuse (79% overall, 64% R) and back registries and duty-to-intervene at 70%+ R; the structural remedy — personal civil liability without the clearly-established-law shield — splits.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem* — arguable either way: unremedied constitutional violations by state actors bear on rule of law, but the case is contested and, on current polling, is heard by one party as a policing-policy dispute.
  - *Symmetry* — the doctrine binds officers regardless of which party governs, so the rule itself is neutral; but the abuse record is coded to one side of the policing debate and no matched pairing of both parties' grievances has been identified. Not regime-coded, but not neutral in reception.
  - *Why-an-amendment* — **weak, and this is decisive alongside the polling.** QI is a judicial construction layered on § 1983 (and on the *Bivens* remedy for federal officers, a cause of action the Court now treats as Congress's to define — *Egbert v. Boule*, 2022); Congress can end it by ordinary statute (H.R. 7085, S. 3625) and two states have done so by statute. No court has struck a statute abolishing QI, the Court has not exempted itself, and the text is not silent — the statute already says "shall be liable." None of the four accepted answers in §5.3 applies. Even at a Strong floor, this would sit on the statutory list.

The accountability framing is the only one that clears the bar, and it does so at 64% R on 2020 data with no re-poll. Whether it rescues the category turns on one question: is "held accountable, ignorance of the law is no defense" the thing we would actually draft? Written as constitutional text it becomes a liability rule — which is exactly the mechanism that polls at 42% R when named. The 22-point drop inside a single Cato sample is the reason the framing does not yet rescue the category: until a survey attaches the accountability language to the operative clause and the Republican number holds, the accountability figure is adjacent evidence (§11 trap: broad-battery numbers are not evidence for a specific mechanism). No ballot measure has ever tested any framing.

## 5. Path back

Reopen if an independent pollster (Pew, Marquette, AP-NORC, Economist/YouGov, or a foundation-commissioned survey using their panels) with 2026-or-later fieldwork finds **≥60% of Republicans** supporting the operative clause in accountability language — e.g., *"a government official who violates a person's constitutional rights may be sued for damages, and not knowing the law is not a defense"* — **and** the number survives a costs-named follow-up ("even if some officers say they will fear lawsuits") without dropping into the Moderate band. A second route: a red- or purple-state legislature or ballot measure adopting a Colorado-style civil action with a cross-party majority would count as §4.2 evidence and would also test the why-an-amendment answer (it would suggest the statutory route works, which argues for the statutory list rather than the platform). Absent either, the category stays rejected; a Strong floor alone would move it to the statutory list, not to a target, because a statute can end the doctrine.

Searched for and not found (2026-08-17): any 2023–2026 national poll on qualified immunity or suing police with party breakdowns from Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox News, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, Cato, Morning Consult, or UMD PPC; any state ballot measure on qualified immunity.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)

*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- Corrected: §1 and §3 described qualified immunity only as a construction of § 1983. The defense applies equally to *Bivens* damages suits against federal officers — *Harlow v. Fitzgerald* itself was a damages action against senior White House aides, not a § 1983 case ([Cornell LII, 457 U.S. 800](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/457/800)) — and the passages now say so. No change to the gate result.
- **The federal-officer side (*Bivens*).** *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (June 21, 1971), held that a complaint "states a federal cause of action under the Fourth Amendment for which damages are recoverable" against federal agents — a judge-made remedy, not a statute — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/403/388). *Egbert v. Boule* (June 8, 2022) declined to extend *Bivens* to Fourth Amendment excessive-force and First Amendment retaliation claims against a Border Patrol agent, asking "whether there is any reason to think that Congress might be better equipped" to create a damages remedy — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/21-147). Bearing on §5.3: the reviewer suggested this makes the federal-officer half of the problem constitutional rather than statutory. It does not change the answer — the Court's own reasoning treats the remedy as Congress's to supply, and a statute creating a damages action against federal officers without a clearly-established-law defense would reach the same result an amendment would; no court has held that Congress lacks that power. The why-an-amendment test remains weak.
- Reviewer tone note on "the framing that clears the bar is not the thing an 'eliminate qualified immunity' amendment would draft": not applied — the sentence states the framing-dependency finding, not a view on the merits.
