# Abortion — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Verdict in one line:** 25% (Republicans) on a federal amendment protecting a right to abortion, UMass Amherst/YouGov Oct 2024 — Weak; high-topline/low-floor in both directions (a rights amendment loses Republicans, a life/personhood amendment loses Democrats) and no symmetric abuse-vs-remedy principle to draft around — path back: none identified; would need ≥45% in both parties for one specific amendment text.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

Three distinct amendments travel under this heading, and they point in opposite directions:

- **(a) A right-to-abortion ("codify *Roe*") amendment.** Federal constitutional text guaranteeing the right to decide whether to continue a pregnancy, usually with a viability line and a health exception. The statutory version — the Women's Health Protection Act — is the vehicle Congress has actually voted on: [S.4132 (117th)](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1172/vote_117_2_00170.htm) failed cloture 49–51 on May 11, 2022, with no Republican in favor and one Democrat opposed. This memo located no floor vote in either chamber on a rights-direction amendment.
- **(b) A "right to life" / fetal-personhood amendment.** Text declaring that constitutional protection of life or personhood begins at conception, or returning authority over abortion to Congress and the states. The last floor vote on any abortion amendment was of this kind: the Hatch–Eagleton [S.J.Res.3 (98th)](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/votes/98-1983/s169), "to establish legislative authority in Congress and the States with respect to abortion, thereby overturning" *Roe*, failed 49–50 on June 28, 1983 (two-thirds required). Its statutory descendant, the [Life at Conception Act, H.R.431 (118th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/431/cosponsors), listed 131 cosponsors, all Republican, and never left committee. The Republican Party's 2024 platform dropped its four-decade call for a constitutional amendment applying the Fourteenth Amendment before birth ([PBS, July 9, 2024](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/republicans-change-platform-to-reflect-trumps-position-opposing-federal-abortion-ban)).
- **(c) "Leave it to the states."** Post-*Dobbs* this is the status quo, not an amendment; it is listed only because it is the framing most often offered as the "compromise," and Section 4 explains why it does not rescue the category.

**How it entered the pipeline.** Abortion was placed on the avoid list by the founder at the first triage session (2026-08-06: "track a list of categories to avoid, including abortion, voting rights to start with") and recorded in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) the same day. It has no entry in the contributor lists in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md); it later surfaced organically in the public-thread mining ([REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md): "codify *Roe*" from u/alabasterskim and u/Kursed_Valeth; "constitutionally define 'person'" from u/Journey2Jess), where each instance was sorted to the rejected list.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — the amendment itself**

- **Federal constitutional amendment protecting a woman's right to choose to have an abortion: 56% support overall (43% strongly), 27% oppose — 87% D / 50% I / 25% R.** In the same battery, a **national abortion ban: 15% support — 7% D / 10% I / 28% R.** [UMass Amherst / YouGov national poll, Oct 11–16, 2024, n=1,500](https://www.umass.edu/poll/media/113/download) (crosstabs; toplines [here](https://www.umass.edu/poll/media/111/download); release [here](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/two-thirds-americans-oppose-national-abortion-and-ivf-bans-according-new-umass)). This is the only independent national poll located that asks the amendment question directly with a party split; it is the decisive number.
- **Congress should pass a law guaranteeing access to legal abortion nationwide: 60% — 84% D / 32% R.** [AP-NORC, June 22–26, 2023, n=1,220](https://apnorc.org/projects/most-americans-support-legal-abortion-with-some-restrictions-ap-norc/). The statutory version of framing (a); the June 2024 wave put the topline at 64% ([AP-NORC, June 20–24, 2024](https://apnorc.org/projects/support-for-legal-abortion-increased-since-roe-v-wade-was-overturned/); no party split published for that item).

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — the underlying principle (legality)**

- **Legal in all or most cases: 60% — 84% D / 36% R** (63% of Republicans say illegal in all or most cases); partisan gap 48 points, up from 24 in 2007. [Pew, Jan 20–26, 2026, n=8,512](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/03/12/majority-of-americans-continue-to-say-abortion-should-be-legal-in-all-or-most-cases/). Republican support was 41% in 2024 and 38% in March 2022 ([Pew, May 2022](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/05/06/wide-partisan-gaps-in-abortion-attitudes-but-opinions-in-both-parties-are-complicated/)).
- **Legal in all or most circumstances: 48% overall — 75% D / 48% I / 15% R**; pro-choice identification 53% overall — 81% D / 54% I / 17% R. [Gallup Values & Beliefs, May 1–17, 2026, n=1,001](https://news.gallup.com/poll/711401/dobbs-lasting-impact-abortion.aspx). (Gallup's three-way wording — any/certain/no circumstances — produces a lower "legal" share than Pew's; the party gap is the same story.)
- **Legal in all or most cases: 64% — 85% D; 59% of Republicans say illegal in all or most cases.** Abortion "for any reason": 52% favor, down from 61% in 2024 (75% D favor; 70% R oppose). [AP-NORC, July 10–14, 2025, n=1,437](https://apnorc.org/projects/support-for-legal-abortion-remains-strong/).
- **Always legal or legal with some restrictions: 61% — 85% D / 67% I / 29% R**; "only in special circumstances" 31% (53% R); always illegal 9% (18% R). [Economist/YouGov, June 13–15, 2026, n=1,549, crosstab p. 27](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_GpnDRNm.pdf).
- **"Human life begins at conception, so a fetus is a person with rights" describes my view extremely/very well: 56% R / 23% D** (about 38% overall). [Pew, March 7–13, 2022, n=10,441](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/05/06/wide-partisan-gaps-in-abortion-attitudes-but-opinions-in-both-parties-are-complicated/). The closest independent measure of the principle behind framing (b).

**Legislative votes**

- Hatch–Eagleton amendment (legislative authority over abortion restored to Congress and the states), [S.J.Res.3, June 28, 1983](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/votes/98-1983/s169): 49–50, two-thirds required; 34 R yea / 19 R nay; 15 D yea / 31 D nay. The only Senate floor vote on an abortion amendment since *Roe*, and it split both caucuses.
- Women's Health Protection Act, [S.4132, May 11, 2022](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1172/vote_117_2_00170.htm): cloture rejected 49–51; 0 Republicans yea, 1 Democrat nay.

**Advocacy-commissioned polls.** None used; the independent record is sufficient and unambiguous.

**Nuances & caveats**

- The state results are majorities of *state* electorates on *state* constitutional text, not two-thirds/three-fourths supermajorities on federal text; the best 2024 rights results in states that voted Republican for president were 57.8% (Montana) and a bare 51.6% (Missouri), and three of the five such measures failed (Florida under its 60% rule, Nebraska, South Dakota).
- Question wording moves the topline 15–20 points: "always legal or with some restrictions" (Economist/YouGov) reads 61%; Gallup's three-way item reads 48%; Pew's four-way item reads 60%. The *party gap* is stable across all of them (44–60 points), which is what the floor measures.
- Republican support for legality is not zero and not monolithic — 15–41% across 2025–2026 waves depending on wording — and Democratic support for a personhood principle (23%) is not zero either. But no wave of any independent pollster puts the *lower* party above 45% for any abortion proposal.
- The Economist/YouGov write-up's sentence on Republicans reads "always (5%) or mostly (24%) illegal"; the crosstab shows those are the *legal* shares. The memo uses the crosstab.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** 25% (Republicans) on "a federal constitutional amendment that protects a woman's right to choose to have an abortion," UMass Amherst/YouGov Oct 2024 → **Weak** per §4.1. For the opposite-direction amendment there is no direct national poll; the nearest proxies are 7% D for a national ban (same UMass poll) and 23% D for the personhood principle (Pew 2022) → **Weak** as well.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor*, in both directions. A rights amendment carries a 56% topline over a 25% Republican floor (62-point party gap). A ban carries a 28% Republican ceiling over a 7% Democratic floor. Legality itself: 75–85% D vs. 15–41% R across Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, and Economist/YouGov 2025–2026.
  - *Framing dependency* — the framing that wins state ballots (state-level "reproductive freedom" text, 51–58% in Republican-voting states) is not federal text and does not survive translation into a national supermajority; the framing that wins Republicans ("leave it to the states") is not an amendment.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding is not the issue here* — the split is stable regardless of who holds power (Pew's 24-point gap in 2007 has widened monotonically to 48 points), which makes the case for rejection stronger, not weaker: no re-poll under a different administration will move it.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem test:* not reached — the category fails on the floor before this test is applied.
  - *Symmetry (veil-of-ignorance) test:* fails by construction. Every abortion amendment is a substantive rule on a contested moral question, not a limit on power that binds both parties equally; there is no "abuse" both sides condemn and no "remedy" that binds a future majority of either side symmetrically. This is the deeper reason the abuse-vs-remedy logic that carried the pardons and DOJ-independence targets does not apply.
  - *Why-an-amendment test:* answered on both sides (each side wants to bind the other's future majorities and courts), but that answer is exactly what makes the proposal partisan-coded.

## 4. Framings tested

- **(a) Right-to-abortion amendment** — 56% / 87 D / 25 R (UMass 2024). Weak. Statutory analogue 60% / 84 D / 32 R (AP-NORC 2023). Weak.
- **(b) Right-to-life / personhood amendment** — no direct national poll located; proxies 15% for a national ban (7 D / 28 R, UMass 2024) and 56 R / 23 D on the personhood principle (Pew 2022); 0-for-6 on state ballots. Weak, and it now sits outside the 2024 platform of the party that historically carried it.
- **(c) "Leave it to the states"** — this is *Dobbs*, i.e., current law; drafting it as an amendment would constitutionalize the status quo, which one side reads as a permanent defeat and the other reads as either unnecessary or insufficient — the largest national pro-life organization has publicly rejected a "states-only" strategy in favor of national minimum protections ([SBA Pro-Life America, June 6, 2023](https://sbaprolife.org/newsroom/press-releases/sba-statement-on-chris-christie-presidential-campaign-announcement); [SBA Pro-Life America, Apr. 30, 2026](https://sbaprolife.org/latest-news/marjorie-dannenfelsers-speech-at-the-2026-sba-pro-life-america-gala): "A states-only strategy will not defeat this threat"). It is not the thing either coalition would draft, so it cannot accurately be called an abortion amendment that "clears the bar" — it is a different proposal wearing the name.
- **(d) Narrow, high-consensus slices** (life-of-the-mother exceptions, protection of IVF, emergency-room care) poll far better — e.g., a federal law requiring emergency rooms to provide an abortion to save the life or health of a pregnant woman: 74% overall, 89 D / 68 I / 61 R (UMass 2024) — but they are statutory health-care rules, not constitutional principles, and each one is contested inside the abortion fight itself. None is "the thing we would actually draft" as an amendment.
- **Verdict on framings:** none clears the bar as the text a drafting committee would adopt (the "honest framing" test of §4.3). The only framings that reach both parties are either not amendments (c) or not about the constitutional question (d).

## 5. Path back

None identified. Reopening would require an independent national poll (Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, YouGov, or Fox) showing at least 45% support in *both* parties — i.e., a Moderate floor — for one specific amendment text that a drafting committee would actually adopt, sustained across two waves under different administrations. No such number has appeared in any wave from 2022 through mid-2026, and the trend in the party gap runs the other way (Pew: 24 points in 2007 → 48 points in 2026). The November 2026 measures in Idaho, Missouri, Nevada, and Virginia are state constitutional votes; their results will be recorded here but cannot by themselves change a federal-amendment verdict.

A separate note for the privacy target: the reason the foundation's privacy draft is written in informational/electronic-data terms only is precisely this memo. "Right to privacy" in autonomy language is the *Griswold*/*Roe* doctrinal root (see [SELECTION_METHOD.md §11](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) and [PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md](../PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md)); an autonomy-framed privacy amendment would be heard as framing (a) and would inherit its 25% floor.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Equal Rights Amendment language as a rights-direction vehicle (state level).** Sex-equality text has been used to reach abortion without an abortion amendment: on Jan. 29, 2024, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court, 3–2 on the point, held that the state ERA applies to the Medicaid abortion-coverage ban, that such sex-based classifications are presumptively unconstitutional under strict scrutiny, and overruled *Fischer v. Department of Public Welfare* (1985), remanding without deciding the ban's validity ([State Court Report, Feb. 14, 2024](https://statecourtreport.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/pennsylvania-supreme-court-ruling-overturns-decades-old-precedent)). New York's Proposal 1 (Nov. 5, 2024) added "pregnancy, pregnancy outcomes, and reproductive healthcare and autonomy" to the state constitution's equal-protection clause and passed 62.47%–37.53%; it cleared the state Senate 49–14 (Democrats 42–1, Republicans 7–13) ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/New_York_Proposal_1,_Equal_Protection_of_Law_Amendment_(2024))). These are state constitutional results, already counted in kind in Section 2, and no independent national poll with a party breakdown on ERA-as-abortion-vehicle text was located; the memo's statement that no congressional floor vote has occurred on a rights-direction *abortion* amendment stands. Bearing on the gate: none — if anything it confirms framing dependency (the winning text is equality language, not the abortion text a committee would draft).
