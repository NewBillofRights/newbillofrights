# Balanced Budget — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Verdict in one line:** 68% (Democrats) on the abstract question, Economist/YouGov June 2026 — Strong on paper, but the category fails the depth test: the only trade-off experiment on record (Fox News, June 2011) drops support from 72% to 31% overall (79% of Democrats opposed; Republicans split 47/46) once entitlement cuts are specified, and the coalition splits asymmetrically (Democrats on benefit cuts, Republicans on taxes) — path back is a modern trade-off poll, mechanisms named, clearing ~60% in both parties.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The core idea is a constitutional rule that federal outlays may not exceed receipts in a fiscal year. Every serious draft then adds machinery, and the machinery is where the versions diverge:

- **Waiver threshold.** A supermajority of each chamber (three-fifths or two-thirds) may authorize a deficit; most drafts add a war or military-emergency exception. [H.J.Res. 17 (119th Congress)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/17), introduced January 13, 2025, uses a two-thirds roll-call vote and requires the President to submit a balanced budget.
- **Tax and debt-limit supermajorities.** [H.J.Res. 2 (119th Congress)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/2), introduced January 3, 2025, flatly bars any increase in the limit on debt held by the public and bars any revenue-raising bill unless two-thirds of each chamber approves by roll call ([text, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres2ih/html/BILLS-119hjres2ih.htm)). In the 112th Congress, [H.J.Res. 1](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41907) paired a three-fifths deficit waiver with a two-thirds vote for revenue increases and a spending cap at 18% of economic output, while [H.J.Res. 2](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41907) — the version that reached the floor — dropped the tax supermajority and the cap: it kept a three-fifths roll-call vote for a deficit or a debt-limit increase and required only "a majority of the whole number of each House" for a revenue increase ([text, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-112hjres2ih/html/BILLS-112hjres2ih.htm); [CRS R41907](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41907), [mirror](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41907.html): "H.J.Res. 2 required only a majority of each chamber to agree to a revenue increase, and it did not include a provision creating a specified limit on spending").
- **Article V route.** Advocates also pursue a convention of states limited to a balanced budget amendment. The Congressional Research Service recorded the Balanced Budget Amendment Task Force claiming 28 live state applications in 2017, counting unrescinded 1970s–80s applications ([CRS R44435, Nov. 2017](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R44435/R44435.5.pdf)); a January 2026 op-ed by a House committee chair puts the count at 27, seven short of the 34 required ([Comer, Jan. 2026](https://comer.house.gov/2026/1/comer-in-herald-leader-it-s-time-to-finally-balance-america-s-budget)). Both are advocates' counts; there is no official register.

The idea is live in the current Congress: the House Judiciary Subcommittee on the Constitution and Limited Government held a hearing, "Balancing the Federal Budget: Examining Proposals for a Balanced Budget Amendment," on December 3, 2025 ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/event/119th-congress/house-event/118721/text)). Forty-nine states are commonly reported to carry some balanced-operating-budget requirement, with Vermont the exception ([NCSL, 2016](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Fiscal/NCSL_A_Guide_to_Better_State_Budgeting_Practices_Oct_2016.pdf)), which is the comparison advocates most often draw.

**How it entered our pipeline.** Balanced budget was not on the founder's seed list or in any contributor's notes. It surfaced in the polling-derived gap search of 2026-08-06 ("69–80% abstract support including majorities of both parties") and was rejected the same day ([AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md), "Rejected Ideas"). Attribution: polling research (2026-08-06).

## 2. The evidence

Best evidence first. Independent polls with party breakdowns, then the trade-off experiment, then votes, then advocacy polls.

- **Economist/YouGov, June 13–15, 2026** (1,549 adult citizens). "Would you favor or oppose making the following amendments to the U.S. Constitution? — Require the federal budget to be balanced each year": **72% favor / 7% oppose / 21% not sure**; **Republicans 84%, Democrats 68%, independents 65%** favor. Floor 68% (D) — Strong on the abstract question ([article](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/54990-trumps-job-approval-rebounds-americans-lack-confidence-in-elections-abortion-views-and-more-june-13-15-2026-economist-yougov-poll); [crosstabs, table 13C](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_GpnDRNm.pdf)).
- **Cato Institute / Morning Consult, June 25–26, 2026** (2,253 adults; this item asked of a 1,229 half-sample). "Would you support, or oppose, the following constitutional amendments? — Require a balanced federal budget": **69% support (41% strongly) / 13% oppose / 18% don't know**; **Republicans 77%, Democrats 69%, independents 62%**. Cato is a think tank with a position on the amendment, but the fieldwork is Morning Consult's national tracking poll and full crosstabs are published; the same battery found 73% for a right to health care and 69% for campaign-spending limits, illustrating that respondents endorse most amendment ideas asked in isolation ([crosstabs, table CATO20_1](https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/2026-07/Cato%20Fourth%20of%20July%202026%20Survey%20Crosstabs.pdf); [Cato summary](https://www.cato.org/blog/new-poll-nearly-half-americans-dont-know-what-americas-250th-celebrating); [Newsweek write-up](https://www.newsweek.com/how-americans-would-change-the-constitution-new-poll-12152648)).
- **Harvard CAPS/Harris, July 19–20, 2023** (2,068 registered voters). "Do you support or oppose a constitutional amendment that would require a balanced budget within 10 years?": **80% support / 20% oppose** (forced choice, no undecided option); **Republicans 83%, Democrats 79%, independents 77%** ([crosstabs, table 143](https://harvardharrispoll.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/HHP_CAPS_July2023_crosstabs.pdf)).
- **THE TRADE-OFF TEST — Fox News, June 26–28, 2011** (912 registered voters; Anderson Robbins Research (D) / Shaw & Company Research (R)). Abstract: **72% favor / 20% oppose**; **Republicans 82%, Democrats 62%**. "Sentiment is reversed if balancing the budget meant 'major spending cuts' to entitlement programs like Medicare and Social Security: **31% favor / 63% oppose**." Under that condition **79% of Democrats oppose** and **Republicans split 47% favor / 46% oppose**. If it meant "major tax increases," **62% oppose** (no party breakdown published for the tax condition; no independent breakdown published) ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/fox-news-poll-balanced-budget-amendment-favored-obamas-stimulus-panned.amp)). This remains the only rigorous split-condition test we can find; no independent pollster has rerun it in the 2020s.
- **Modern depth data — the mechanisms voters reject.** No 2020s poll conditions the amendment itself, but the programs any binding rule would reach have been polled repeatedly, and both parties reject cutting them:
  - Pew, April 8–14, 2024 (8,709 adults): **79%** say Social Security benefits "should not be reduced in any way," including **77% of Republicans and 83% of Democrats**; 19% say some future reductions need to be considered ([Pew, June 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2024/06/24/americans-views-of-government-aid-to-poor-role-in-health-care-and-social-security/); [fieldwork](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2024/06/24/americans-views-of-governments-role-persistent-divisions-and-areas-of-agreement/)).
  - AP-NORC, March 16–20, 2023 (1,081 adults): reducing Social Security benefits **6% favor / 79% oppose**; raising the full-benefit age from 67 to 70 **10% / 75%**; raising the Medicare age from 65 to 67 **15% / 70%**; increasing Medicare premiums **10% / 67%**; raising taxes on households over $400,000 to pay for Medicare **58% / 23%** — with **75% of Democrats** in favor versus **42% of Republicans** (37% opposed) ([topline](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/APNORC_Mar2023_Topline.pdf); [AP write-up for the party split](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/most-americans-oppose-social-security-medicare-cuts-ap-norc-poll-says)).
  - KFF, April 8–15, 2025 (1,380 adults): oppose "major cuts" to Social Security **84%**, Medicare **79%**, Medicaid **76%**; among Republicans **73% / 64% / 55%** respectively ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/kff-health-tracking-poll-april-2025-publics-view-on-major-cuts-to-federal-health-agencies/)).
  - YouGov, February 24–27, 2025: across a long battery of federal spending areas, "in none of these areas did a majority of Americans support a reduction in spending"; foreign aid is the lone near-exception at 49% (73% of Republicans, 22% of Democrats) ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/51707-as-trump-cuts-federal-funding-most-americans-support-increases)).
- **The asymmetry on method.** Gallup, November 9–12, 2012: asked how to cut the deficit, **69% of Republicans** chose only-or-mostly spending cuts, while **57% of Democrats** chose an equal mix of tax increases and cuts and Democrats were the group most open to a tax-led approach ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/158828/balanced-approach-cutting-deficit.aspx)). The AP-NORC tax item above (75% D / 42% R) shows the same shape a decade later.
- **Salience is R-tilted even when the topline is not.** Pew, January 16–21, 2024: reducing the budget deficit a "top priority" for **54%** — **68% of Republicans, 40% of Democrats**; January 2023: **57%** (71% R / 44% D) ([Pew, Feb. 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2024/02/29/americans-top-policy-priority-for-2024-strengthening-the-economy/); [Pew, May 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/05/24/6-facts-about-americans-views-of-government-spending-and-the-deficit/)).
- **Roll-call record (the amendment has never carried both chambers).** Senate, March 2, 1995, H.J.Res. 1: **65–35**, two short of 67 (Republicans 51–2, Democrats 14–33; the majority leader switched to "nay" to preserve a motion to reconsider, so the effective margin was one vote) ([senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1041/vote_104_1_00098.htm); [CRS R41907](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41907)). Senate, March 4, 1997, S.J.Res. 1: **66–34** (Republicans 55–0, Democrats 11–34) ([senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1051/vote_105_1_00024.htm)). House, January 26, 1995, H.J.Res. 1: passed **300–132** (Republicans 228–2, Democrats 72–129) ([Clerk roll 51](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/1995/roll051.xml)). House, November 18, 2011, H.J.Res. 2: **261–165**, short of two-thirds (Republicans 236–4, Democrats 25–161) ([Clerk roll 858](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2011/roll858.xml)). House, April 12, 2018, H.J.Res. 2: **233–184** (Republicans 226–6, Democrats 7–178) ([Clerk roll 138](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2018/roll138.xml)). Earlier: Senate 69–31 in 1982 and 66–34 in 1986; House 280–153 in 1992 and 271–153 in 1994, all short of two-thirds ([CRS R41907](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41907)). Democratic support in the House fell from 72 votes (1995) to 25 (2011) to 7 (2018).
- **Advocacy polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict).** America's New Majority Project (Gingrich-affiliated), McLaughlin & Associates, January 13–18, 2022, 1,000 likely voters: amendment "requiring congress to pass a balanced federal budget each year" **70% support / 13% oppose / 17% not sure**; **Republicans 74%, Democrats 67%, independents 70%** ([report, Feb. 4, 2022](https://americasnewmajorityproject.com/analysis-americans-want-a-return-to-balanced-budgets/); [PDF](https://americasnewmajorityproject.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/20220204-Jan-BalancedBudget-PollGroups.pdf)). ANMP again, July 21–24, 2025, 2,000 registered voters: **69% support / 12% oppose / 18% don't know**; the report's party chart shows Republicans 80%, Democrats 62%, independents 65% (the web summary states 75% R / 56% D on a different party definition). The same survey found **64%** agree it is "possible to balance the federal budget without raising taxes or cutting benefits in popular programs like Medicare, Medicaid, and Social Security" and **72%** agree waste and fraud reduction could save "hundreds of billions" without cutting benefits ([release](https://americasnewmajorityproject.com/voters-want-congress-to-balance-the-budget-without-raising-taxes-or-cutting-benefits/); [topline, Q67–69](https://americasnewmajorityproject.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/20250724-NationalANMP-Topline.pdf); [report](https://americasnewmajorityproject.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/20250825-BalanceBudget.pdf)). Peterson Foundation Fiscal Confidence Index (fiscal-policy foundation; bipartisan pollster pair, registered voters): debt as a top-three priority **77%** (87% R / 73% D / 70% I) September 2024; **74%** (88% R / 62% D / 71% I) February 2025; **81%** (87% R / 75% D / 79% I) April 2026 ([Sept. 2024](https://www.pgpf.org/press/2024-9-fci-press-release/); [Feb. 2025](https://www.pgpf.org/press/2025-02-fci-press-release/); [Apr. 2026](https://www.pgpf.org/press/2026-4-fci-press-release/)).

**Nuances & caveats**

- The abstract number is genuinely bipartisan and stable across administrations: 82R/62D under a Democratic president (2011), 83R/79D in 2023, 84R/68D and 77R/69D under a Republican president (2026). This is not a regime-coded topline (incumbent/regime coding, §4.3, does not apply here). What moves is *urgency*: Democratic "top priority" numbers were 40–44% in 2023–24 and Peterson's Democratic figure rose from 62% to 75% between February 2025 and April 2026.
- The abstract number rests on a belief that no trade-off exists: 64% of ANMP's 2025 sample think the budget can be balanced without tax increases or benefit cuts. That belief is what the trade-off condition removes, and when it is removed the Fox 2011 result is roughly a 40-point drop.
- The Fox experiment is fifteen years old, one poll, one pollster; the corpus should not overstate its precision. But its direction is corroborated by every modern program-level poll above: neither party's voters will endorse cuts to Social Security or Medicare, and the parties diverge sharply on taxes.
- The Harvard/Harris item is forced-choice (no "not sure"), which inflates both sides relative to YouGov's 21% and Cato's 18% undecided.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** 68% (Democrats) on "require the federal budget to be balanced each year," Economist/YouGov June 2026 → **Strong** on the abstract question per §4.1. On the conditioned question — the amendment "if it required major cuts to Medicare and Social Security" — the floor collapses: 31% overall, Democrats 79% opposed, Republicans 47/46 (Fox 2011) → **Weak**. The abstract floor is not the number the drafted amendment would face.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):**
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — 72% → 31% overall; Democrats 62% → 79% opposed; Republicans 82% → 47/46 when entitlement cuts are named; 62% oppose if it means major tax increases (Fox 2011). Modern corroboration: 79% say Social Security should not be reduced in any way (77R/83D, Pew 2024); 6% favor reducing benefits (AP-NORC 2023).
  - *Asymmetric coalition* — the two halves of any binding rule each lose a party: benefit cuts lose Democrats (79% opposed) and a large share of Republicans; tax increases lose Republicans (69% want only-or-mostly cuts, Gallup 2012; 42% R vs 75% D on a high-earner tax, AP-NORC 2023). This is a species of framing dependency: the honest framing that clears the bar (unconditioned) is not the thing we would draft.
  - *High-topline / low-floor* does **not** apply to the abstract question — the floor is 65–79% in every independent poll — but the roll-call record shows the elite floor is far lower than the voter floor: 7 of 185 House Democrats in 2018, 11 of 45 Senate Democrats in 1997.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Why-an-amendment* — arguably passes: Congress cannot bind future Congresses by statute, and the statutory attempt (Gramm-Rudman-Hollings, 1985) had its automatic sequestration mechanism struck in *Bowsher v. Synar* (1986) and had to be reworked by statute in 1987 ([CRS R41907](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R41907)). This test is not why the category failed.
  - *Existential-problem* — contested rather than failed; the mission's "no policy preferences" bar is a live objection to a fiscal rule, but the rejection was made on the evidence gate, not here.
  - *Symmetry* — the rule binds both parties formally, but every operative draft embeds one side's preferred method (tax supermajorities in H.J.Res. 2 [119th] and H.J.Res. 1 [112th]); the "clean" H.J.Res. 2 (112th) is the only version that even attempts neutrality on method, and it lost 25 of 186 House Democrats. "Principles, not machinery" (§6.2) also cuts against it: a working balanced-budget rule is thresholds, exemptions, and enforcement, which the mission assigns to statute.

## 4. Framings tested

- **Unconditioned "require a balanced budget" (the press-release framing).** Clears the bar in every independent poll: 68% D floor (YouGov 2026), 69% D (Cato/Morning Consult 2026), 79% D (Harvard/Harris 2023). Does not rescue the category because it is not what would be drafted; it measures approval of the outcome, not the rule.
- **"Balanced within 10 years" (Harvard/Harris 2023).** 80% (83R/79D/77I). Same limitation, with the added forced-choice inflation.
- **Conditioned on entitlement cuts (Fox 2011).** 31% overall; D floor at most 21%. Fails.
- **Conditioned on tax increases (Fox 2011).** 62% oppose overall; no party split published, but the Gallup and AP-NORC method data make the Republican floor the binding one under this framing. Fails.
- **"Balance the budget by cutting waste and fraud, without raising taxes or cutting benefits" (ANMP 2025).** 64% agree it is possible; 72% agree waste reduction could save hundreds of billions. This is the framing that keeps the topline high, but it is a claim about arithmetic, not a constitutional rule, and it comes from an advocacy poll. It cannot be drafted.
- **Debt-limit-plus-amendment package ("cut, cap and balance," 2011).** Widely reported at ~66% support with majorities in both parties, but the pollster's own document could not be retrieved; listed as unverified below and not used.
- **Non-amendment fiscal reforms** (statutory PAYGO, spending caps, debt-stabilization targets) were not polled with party splits in anything we could verify and are in any case statutory by design.

No framing that both clears the bar and describes an enforceable constitutional rule was found.

## 5. Path back

The category reopens on a modern, independent, split-sample poll that names the mechanism — "a constitutional amendment requiring a balanced federal budget, even if that meant [a specified mix of spending cuts to major programs and tax increases], with a supermajority waiver for war or recession" — and clears **~60% in both parties**, with the Democratic number surviving the benefit-cut condition and the Republican number surviving the tax condition. A weaker but useful signal would be a Fox-style rerun in which the drop from unconditioned to conditioned support is under 15 points in each party. Absent that, the 2011 result stands, and every program-level poll since points the same way. This is item 3 on the corpus's commissioned-polling list ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md)).

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Clarified (reviewer's proposed correction rejected on the record):** an outside note asserted that H.J.Res. 2 (112th) — the version voted 261–165 on Nov. 18, 2011 — carried a two-thirds tax supermajority and a spending cap. The introduced text and CRS say otherwise: three-fifths for a deficit or a debt-limit increase, "a majority of the whole number of each House" for a revenue increase, no spending cap; the two-thirds revenue vote and 18%-of-output cap were in H.J.Res. 1 (112th) as reported by the Judiciary Committee ([H.J.Res. 2 text, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-112hjres2ih/html/BILLS-112hjres2ih.htm); [CRS R41907, mirror](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41907.html)). Section 1's sentence now states the retained three-fifths provisions explicitly.
- **State analogue — operating budgets only.** The 49-state comparison advocates draw is narrower than the federal drafts: NCSL's guide states that state "balanced budget requirements refer to the state operating budget and not to the capital budget," that states issue long-term debt for capital expenditures, and that "it is commonly reported that all states except Vermont have a formal requirement to balance their operating budget" ([NCSL, Oct. 2016, PDF](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Fiscal/NCSL_A_Guide_to_Better_State_Budgeting_Practices_Oct_2016.pdf)). The federal texts apply to *all* outlays: H.J.Res. 2 (112th) §7 — "Total outlays shall include all outlays of the United States Government except for those for repayment of debt principal" ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-112hjres2ih/html/BILLS-112hjres2ih.htm)); H.J.Res. 2 (119th) §1 — "Total outlays for any fiscal year shall not exceed total receipts for that fiscal year" with no capital exclusion ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres2ih/html/BILLS-119hjres2ih.htm)). Bearing on the gate: none on the numbers; it is a further reason the "states do it" framing is not the thing that would be drafted.
- **A post-2011 trade-off item (deficit vs. programs).** Fox News, March 24–27, 2023 (1,007 registered voters; Beacon Research (D) / Shaw & Company Research (R)): "Which is more important to you? Reducing the federal budget deficit, or continuing to fund entitlement programs such as Social Security and Medicare at their current levels" — **26% deficit / 71% fund the programs** (April 2013: 40% / 54%). Fox's write-up reports the shift toward funding the programs as +12 among Democrats, +19 among Republicans and +25 among independents since 2013, and that "majorities, including Democrats, Republicans, and independents," oppose reducing Social Security benefits for some future retirees (82% oppose), raising income tax rates for all Americans (80%), and raising the Medicare age from 65 to 67 (66%); the published topline does not give party splits for the deficit-vs-programs item itself ([release](https://www.foxnews.com/official-polls/fox-news-poll-71-choose-funding-social-security-medicare-over-budget-cuts); [topline PDF, Q38–45](https://static.foxnews.com/foxnews.com/content/uploads/2023/03/Fox_March-24-27-2023_Complete_National_Topline_March-30-Release.pdf)). This is a priority item, not a conditioned amendment question, so it does not replace the 2011 experiment; it corroborates its direction with 2023 data. Bearing on the gate: none; the depth failure stands.
