# Election Technology & Modernization — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Verdict in one line:** 15% (R) on allowing people to vote entirely online, AP-NORC Sept 2020 (39% D; 28% overall) — Weak floor, and no majority in *either* party in any independent poll since the pandemic spike (MIT SPAE 2024: ≈19% R / ≈45% D); fails the topline ("not popular"), is partisan-coded where it splits, and fails the why-an-amendment test because Article I §4 and the Help America Vote Act already give Congress the tools — path back: none identified as an amendment; statutory track.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The category entered the pipeline through **Austin Burns** ([AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)): the electoral process "needs major revision to account for 250 years of societal and technological change," and with technologies like quantum cryptography "legitimate ballots could be securely cast by people from their phones." As an amendment, that reads as one of two things:

1. **A right to cast a ballot over the internet or from a mobile device** — the version with a live advocacy movement (the [Mobile Voting Project](https://www.mobilevoting.org/), funded by Bradley Tusk, which is working state-by-state for municipal-election pilots) and the version that has been polled. No congressional joint resolution proposing this as constitutional text was identified; the vehicles are state bills, e.g. Minnesota [SF 4559 (2026)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/bills/94/2026/0/SF/4559), introduced March 18, 2026 with DFL and Republican chief authors, which was referred to the Senate Elections Committee and had no further action when the session ended.
2. **A general "modernization" mandate** — machines, registration systems, ballot design, audits. This is not a proposal; it is a portmanteau for dozens of statutory choices, several of which *are* bipartisan (paper backups, audits, nonpartisan administration) and are already governed by federal statute.

The existing legal frame matters for the gate. Article I §4 provides that the "Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof; but the Congress may at any time by law make or alter such Regulations" ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei)). Congress used that power in the [Help America Vote Act of 2002](https://www.eac.gov/about/help-america-vote-act), which created the Election Assistance Commission (EAC), funded replacement of punch-card and lever machines, and charged the EAC with voting-system guidelines and the federal certification program. HAVA's conference report passed the House **357–48 (172 R / 184 D / 1 I yea)** ([Clerk, Roll 462, Oct. 10, 2002](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2002/roll462.xml)) and the Senate **92–2** ([Senate Vote 238, Oct. 16, 2002](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1072/vote_107_2_00238.xml)). Voting-system standards are still being rewritten by the political branches without any amendment: [Executive Order 14248 (Mar. 25, 2025)](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/03/preserving-and-protecting-the-integrity-of-american-elections/), §4(b), directs the EAC to amend the Voluntary Voting System Guidelines so that systems "should not use a ballot in which a vote is contained within a barcode or quick-response code … and should provide a voter-verifiable paper record." As for online voting itself, [NCSL (updated June 30, 2026)](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/electronic-ballot-return-internet-voting) counts 32 states plus D.C. that allow some electronic return of voted ballots for military and overseas voters, 13 that extend it to voters with disabilities, 11 that allow a web portal, and 18 that permit mail-only return — an accommodation for specific groups, not a general channel.

## 2. The evidence

- **AP-NORC, September 11–14, 2020 (n=1,108 adults):** "Allowing people to vote entirely online by submitting their ballot through a website" — **28% favor / 51% oppose** overall ([topline, VOTE3](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/topline_september_pdf.pdf)). AP-NORC's write-up says only that "Republicans still oppose casting votes by mail or online" ([project page](https://apnorc.org/projects/casting-a-ballot-in-2020/)); the party figures below were **computed by us from AP-NORC's public-use file** ([PUF](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/September-2020-AP-NORC-Poll-PUF.zip), FINALWT, self-identified party): **Democrats 39% favor / 37% oppose; Republicans 15% favor / 70% oppose; independents and others (incl. leaners) 30% / 44%.** Our overall recomputation (28.5 / 50.2) matches the published topline.
- **AP-NORC, April 16–20, 2020 (n=1,057):** same wording, at the height of the pandemic — **40% favor / 39% oppose** ([topline](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/april_topline_clean.pdf); [project page](https://apnorc.org/projects/voting-in-the-time-of-the-coronavirus/)). Computed from the [April PUF](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/April-2020-AP-NORC-PUF.zip): **Democrats 48% / 27%; Republicans 26% / 58%; independents 44% / 36%.** This is the high-water mark in the record, and it did not reach a majority in either party.
- **AP-NORC, September 13–16, 2018 (n=1,059):** same wording — **28% favor / 53% oppose** (in the [Sept. 2020 topline](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/topline_september_pdf.pdf), trend table; no party breakdown published).
- **MIT Election Lab, Survey of the Performance of American Elections 2024** (YouGov, 10,200 registered voters, post-election): "Allow voting over the Internet" — approximately **33% overall, ≈45% Democrats, ≈19% Republicans**; "Voting using cell phones" — approximately **23% / ≈30% D / ≈14% R** (values read from Figure 35 of [*How We Voted in 2024*](https://electionlab.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2025-07/HowWeVotedIn2024.pdf), pp. 37–38; the report's text states that "support for cell phone voting, universal vote-by-mail, Internet voting, and mandating that all votes be cast on hand-marked paper ballots did not receive majority backing from either party," and that internet voting is one of the two reforms with no upward drift over the decade). The underlying data set requires a Dataverse guestbook, so the party figures are chart reads, ±2 points.
- **MIT SPAE 2022** (same series): internet voting approximately **36% overall, ≈52% D, ≈18% R**; cell-phone voting ≈26% / ≈37% D / ≈14% R (chart, p. 35 of [*How We Voted in 2022*](https://electionlab.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-05/HowWeVotedIn2022.pdf)). The text: internet voting was "opposed by a majority of respondents but supported by a majority of Democrats," and cell-phone voting "was opposed by majorities of Democrats and Republicans." The 2022 Democratic figure is the only majority for the proposal in either party in the record, and it was gone by 2024.
- **The technology items that *are* bipartisan — Pew, August 4–10, 2025 (n=3,554):** "Requiring electronic voting machines to print a paper backup of a voter's ballot" — **84% favor (87% R / 82% D)** ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/08/22/majority-of-americans-continue-to-back-expanded-early-voting-voting-by-mail-voter-id/)); Election Day holiday 74% (74 R / 76 D). SPAE 2024 agrees: paper backups ≈87% in both parties, "the only reform in 2024 without some partisan divide," and nonpartisan selection of election officials ≈70% R / ≈80% D. **AP-NORC, June 22–26, 2023 (n=1,220):** 53% extremely/very confident that votes on electronic machines *with* paper receipts are counted accurately vs. 26% for machines *without* ([topline, VOTE4](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/June-2023-AP-NORC-Topline-Final.pdf); [project page](https://apnorc.org/projects/partisan-views-of-the-electoral-system/)).
- **The technology items that are partisan-coded the other way — SPAE 2024:** "Prohibit the use of computers to mark or count ballots" ≈50% R / ≈33% D; **SPAE 2022:** "count all ballots by hand" ≈59% R / ≈35% D (chart reads, same reports). Skepticism of election technology is as party-sorted as enthusiasm for it.
- **Security consensus (the reason the topline is low on the merits of this specific proposal):** the National Academies' [*Securing the Vote* (2018)](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/25120): "Ballots that have been marked by voters should not be returned over the Internet or any network connected to it, because no current technology can guarantee their secrecy, security, and verifiability." CISA, the EAC, the FBI and NIST, [*Risk Management for Electronic Ballot Delivery, Marking, and Return* (May 8, 2020)](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-02/Final_%20Risk_Management_for_Electronic-Ballot_05082020_508c.pdf): "we recommend paper ballot return as electronic ballot return technologies are high-risk even with controls in place," and use "should be limited to voters who have no other means to return their ballot."
- **Advocacy poll (flagged):** Hart Research for Tusk Philanthropies, December 2021, D.C. voters only: "70 percent of D.C. voters support digital voting tools" — reported by [Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/mobile-voting-project-dc-tusk-philanthropies-122821); no pollster page, sample, wording or party breakdown available. Cannot carry a verdict.

**Nuances & caveats**

- The pandemic produced a real but temporary spike (40% in April 2020 → 28% by September 2020, back to the 2018 level); every reading since is at or below one-third.
- Democratic support has ranged from 39% to ~52% across the four readings with a party breakdown (2020 ×2, 2022, 2024); Republican support has never exceeded 26%. The gap (25–34 points) makes the item partisan-coded, but the more important fact is that it lacks a majority in the *more* supportive party.
- The SPAE party figures are chart reads from the published report, not tabulated numbers; they are corroborated by the AP-NORC PUF computations and by the report's own prose.
- No independent 2025–2026 national poll on internet or mobile voting with a party breakdown was found; the newest is SPAE 2024.
- "Modernization" as a general aspiration is not measurable; every concrete instance is either bipartisan-and-statutory (paper backups) or partisan-coded (online voting, hand counts).

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **15% (Republicans)** on "allowing people to vote entirely online by submitting their ballot through a website," AP-NORC Sept 2020 (computed from PUF; 39% D, 28% overall) → **Weak** per §4.1. Corroborated by SPAE 2024 (≈19% R / ≈45% D / ≈33% overall). Even the pandemic peak (26% R, April 2020) is Weak.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):** *Not popular on the topline* — 28–33% overall, no majority in either party since 2022; this is rarer than high-topline/low-floor and more disqualifying. *Incumbent/regime coding is not the driver* — the split has been stable across the 2018, 2020, 2022 and 2024 readings regardless of who held the presidency, which means it is unlikely to flip with a change of administration. *Framing dependency* — the only framings that clear the bar (paper backups, nonpartisan officials) are different proposals wearing the "modernization" name (§4).
- **Non-polling tests (§5):** *Existential-problem* — the security bodies say the specific proposal would *create* the risk to elections rather than solve one; the problem the category names (turnout, convenience) is real but is not a threat to the country's future in the mission's sense. *Symmetry* — the rule would bind everyone equally, so this test does not fail on its own. *Why-an-amendment* — fails outright: Article I §4 already lets Congress "make or alter" the manner of elections for Senators and Representatives, and Article II §1 lets Congress "determine the Time of choosing the Electors" while each state appoints its presidential electors "in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct" ([Cornell LII, Article II](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articleii)); HAVA (357–48, 92–2) and the EAC's guidelines are the operative machinery, and both parties are actively using it (EO 14248 §4(b), 2025). No court has struck a technology statute, Congress can bind itself here, and the text is not silent. This is the "principles, not machinery" line drawn in MISSION.md — voting technology is machinery by definition.

## 4. Framings tested

- **"Allow voting over the internet" / "vote entirely online through a website"** — 28–33% overall; ≈15–19% R; 39–45% D (AP-NORC 2020, SPAE 2024). Fails.
- **"Voting using cell phones"** — the version closest to the filed idea: ≈23% overall, ≈14% R, ≈30% D (SPAE 2024). Fails by a wider margin than internet voting.
- **"Secure" / "quantum-cryptography" internet voting** — never polled with a security qualifier by an independent pollster; the National Academies and CISA/EAC/FBI/NIST assessments state that *no current technology* meets the bar, so a "secure" qualifier is a claim, not a framing, and would be contested at once.
- **Electronic ballot return for military, overseas and disabled voters** — existing statutory practice in 32 states + D.C.; unpolled as a standalone; already the scope CISA recommends. Nothing to constitutionalize.
- **"Require electronic voting machines to print a paper backup"** — **84% (87 R / 82 D)**, Pew Aug 2025; the one framing that clears the bar Strong. But it is not the proposal that was filed, it is the *opposite* instinct (paper over network), it is the direction federal standards are already moving (EO 14248 §4(b), 2025), and it is a HAVA/VVSG standard, not a constitutional principle. It does not rescue the category; it confirms that the popular part is statutory.
- **"Select election officials on a nonpartisan basis"** — ≈70% R / ≈80% D (SPAE 2024 chart read). Clears the bar but belongs to election *administration*, has never been polled as constitutional text, and sits closer to the rejected Voting Rights category's terrain than to technology.
- **"Prohibit computers from marking or counting ballots" / "hand-count all ballots"** — ≈50% R / ≈33% D (SPAE 2024); ≈59% R / ≈35% D (2022). The mirror-image technology proposal is equally partisan-coded, in the other direction.

None of the framings that clear the bar is the thing we would draft under this heading; each is a statutory standard the political branches already set and revise.

## 5. Path back

None identified as an amendment; the category belongs on the statutory track. For the record, reopening the *polling* question would require independent national polling showing "allow voting over the internet" (or the cell-phone item) at **≥60% in both parties** across two consecutive federal cycles — roughly a tripling of Republican support and a doubling of the 2024 overall figure — together with a reversal by the National Academies and CISA/EAC/FBI/NIST of the "high-risk / should not be returned over the Internet" findings. Even then the why-an-amendment test would remain unmet unless a court held that Congress lacks power under Article I §4 (congressional elections) and Article II §1 (the time of choosing presidential electors) to set voting-technology standards for federal elections, which no court has suggested. The bipartisan technology items (paper backups, audits, nonpartisan administration) can be pursued by the foundation as statutory or state-level work without touching the amendment slate.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)

*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Blockchain / mobile-app voting, the mechanism the filed idea most resembles.** The only internet-voting app used in U.S. federal elections, Voatz (West Virginia, 2018 midterms, for overseas military and other absentee voters; later Denver, Oregon and other pilots), was the subject of the first public security analysis by MIT researchers Michael A. Specter, James Koppel and Daniel Weitzner (Feb. 13, 2020): the app "has vulnerabilities that allow different kinds of adversaries to alter, stop, or expose a user's vote," and "the system's use of the blockchain is unlikely to protect against server-side attacks" — [MIT Internet Policy Research Initiative, *The Ballot is Busted Before the Blockchain* (PDF)](https://internetpolicy.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SecurityAnalysisOfVoatz_Public.pdf); the authors' FAQ: "Nothing done by the server — e.g., printed receipts, emailed receipts, or the use of a blockchain — changes this analysis, because the tampering will occur *before* the vote reaches this part of the process" — [MIT IPRI FAQ](https://internetpolicy.mit.edu/faq-on-the-security-analysis-of-voatz/). The National Academies' 2018 report reached the same conclusion in advance: "The use of blockchains in an election scenario would do little to address the major security requirements of voting, such as voter verifiability," and "Blockchains do not provide ballot secrecy" — [*Securing the Vote*, ch. 5](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25120/chapter/7). No independent poll has tested "blockchain voting" with a party breakdown; the finding bears on §4 (a "secure blockchain" qualifier is a contested claim, not a framing) and does not change the gate result.
- Reviewer tone note (that the memo states the security bodies' conclusion as fact): not applied — the sentence already attributes the conclusion ("the security bodies say") and quotes the National Academies and CISA/EAC/FBI/NIST language directly in §2.
