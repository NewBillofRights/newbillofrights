# Supreme Court Expansion — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Verdict in one line:** 39% Republican floor (Marquette, May 2026; 18–27% R in Pew and YouGov) — high-topline/low-floor, incumbent/regime coding, symmetry, and why-an-amendment all fail — path back only if an independent poll shows ≥45% support in *both* parties for a size rule written to bind both sides, sustained across a change in the Court's appointing-party majority; none identified.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The core idea is to add seats to the Supreme Court so that the sitting president and Senate can fill them — usually four seats, from nine to thirteen. That is the design of the [Judiciary Act of 2021 (H.R. 2584, 117th)](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/117/hr2584), reintroduced as the [Judiciary Act of 2023 (H.R. 3422, 118th)](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/118/hr3422), both Democratic-sponsored, and of a one-page House bill introduced [May 4, 2026 (H.R. 8647, 119th)](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hr8647) "to increase from 9 to 13 the number of justices." The Senate sponsor's 2021 release framed the number as ["thirteen justices for thirteen circuits"](https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/expand-the-supreme-court-senator-markey-and-reps-nadler-johnson-and-jones-introduce-legislation-to-restore-justice-and-democracy-to-judicial-system). None of these bills has received a committee vote. In July 2026 several Democratic candidates and members were reported to be campaigning on adding justices alongside term limits ([Bloomberg Law, July 8, 2026](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/campaigning-democrats-amplify-calls-to-overhaul-supreme-court)).

Two facts define the category for our purposes. First, **expansion needs no amendment.** The Constitution does not fix the Court's size; Congress set it at six in 1789, moved it several times, and has left it at nine since 1869 ([CRS, "Court Packing": Legislative Control over the Size of the Supreme Court](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB10562.html)). The 2021 Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court recorded "widespread agreement among legal scholars that Congress has the constitutional authority to expand the Court's size" while taking "no position on the wisdom of expansion" ([final report, Dec. 2021, ch. 2](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/SCOTUS-Report-Final-12.8.21-1.pdf)). Second, **the only amendment actually in play runs the other way.** The Republican-sponsored "Keep Nine" amendment — "The Supreme Court of the United States shall be composed of nine justices consisting of one chief justice and eight associate justices" — has been introduced in each recent Congress ([H.J.Res. 11, 117th](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/117/hjres11); [H.J.Res. 1](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hjres1), [H.J.Res. 28](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hjres28), and [S.J.Res. 16](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/sjres16), 119th). On June 3, 2026 the House Judiciary Committee ordered H.J.Res. 1 reported **15–8** after a May 21 hearing titled "Court Packing: A Threat to the Supreme Court's Legitimacy" ([H. Rept. 119-732, July 6, 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-119hrpt732/html/CRPT-119hrpt732.htm)); no committee Democrat voted for it and the resolution has no Democratic cosponsors ([The Federalist, June 3, 2026](https://thefederalist.com/2026/06/03/not-a-single-house-judiciary-dem-supports-amendment-keeping-scotus-at-9-justices/)). Its sponsor said he did not expect it to have the votes to pass the full House ([Arizona Daily Independent, June 11, 2026](https://arizonadailyindependent.com/2026/06/11/house-panel-opposes-adding-u-s-supreme-court-justices/)). So the category contains two mirror-image proposals, each sponsored by one party alone.

The idea entered our pipeline from **Ryan Ward** ("expand the court dramatically"), recorded in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) alongside his term-limits and ethics suggestions, which were accepted into Target 2.

## 2. The evidence

- **Marquette Law School Poll, May 20–26, 2026** (n=1,001 adults; the only recurring independent tracker of this item; forced-choice format, no "not sure" option). "Increase the number of justices on the U.S. Supreme Court": **50% favor / 50% oppose**; **Republicans 39% favor / 61% oppose; independents 52/48; Democrats 62/38** (leaners as partisans). Support has held between 48% and 54% in nine polls since 2021, from 42% in September 2019. — [press release](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32PressRelease_CourtIssues.pdf), [crosstabs](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32Crosstabs.html), [trend toplines](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32TrendToplines.html).
- **Marquette, Oct. 1–10, 2024** (n=1,005). Same wording: **54% favor / 46% oppose; 35% R / 56% I / 71% D** in favor — the high-water mark for both the topline and the Democratic number, measured in the month before a presidential election. — [release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2024/10/17/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-approval-of-u-s-supreme-court-edges-upward-but-55-disapprove-of-the-courts-work/).
- **Pew Research Center, July 10–16, 2023** (n=8,480). "Increase the number of justices on the Supreme Court": **46% favor / 51% oppose; 27% of Republicans and Republican leaners favor (72% oppose); 66% of Democrats and Democratic leaners favor.** Pew notes support falls steeply with age among Republicans (44% under 30, 13% at 65+) and is flat across ages among Democrats. — [report](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/), [methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/views-of-politics-methodology/).
- **YouGov, July 17–20, 2024** (n=1,140 adult citizens; "not sure" offered). "Expanding the size of the Supreme Court": **37% support / 42% oppose / 21% not sure; Republicans 18/70; independents 33/39; Democrats 59/20.** The same survey found term limits at 56% R / 89% D and an enforced ethics code at 62% R / 86% D — the same respondents, a 40-point-lower Republican number for expansion. — [article](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50159-most-americans-support-term-limits-poll), [toplines and crosstabs (PDF)](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Supreme_Court_Reforms_20240723.pdf).
- **New York Times/Siena College, Oct. 15–18, 2020** (n=987 likely voters, fielded during a Supreme Court confirmation). "Democrats should not increase the size of the Court": **58% agree / 31% would increase; Republicans 89% opposed to more than nine; independents 65% opposed; Democrats 57% in favor.** Party-attributed wording — a floor for a *party's* action, not for a rule. — [Siena release](https://sri.siena.edu/2020/10/20/the-new-york-times-siena-college-national-poll-biden-leads-trump-50-41/), [crosstabs (PDF)](https://sri.siena.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/US101520-Crosstabs1-1.pdf).
- **Legislative record.** No expansion bill has been marked up in any Congress. The mirror-image Keep Nine amendment cleared House Judiciary **15–8 on June 3, 2026** with no Democratic votes ([H. Rept. 119-732](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-119hrpt732/html/CRPT-119hrpt732.htm)); a 2022 attempt to bring the earlier version to the floor was blocked by the then-majority ([sponsor's release, July 13, 2022](https://dustyjohnson.house.gov/media/press-releases/house-democrats-block-consideration-johnsons-keep-nine-constitutional)). Every recorded action on either side of the question has divided along party lines.
- **Advocacy-commissioned (flagged): Navigator Research, May 13–18, 2026** (n=1,000 registered voters, online opt-in; Democratic-aligned). "Adding more justices to the Supreme Court beyond the current nine seats": **40% support**, net +2 overall; **Democrats net +27, independents net −1, non-MAGA Republicans net −27, MAGA Republicans net −18** (only net figures published by party). Cited by [Fix the Court](https://fixthecourt.com/2026/05/scotus-reforms-from-term-limits-to-ethics-enforcement-remain-popular/) (advocacy aggregator), which contrasts it with 74% for term limits in the same survey. — [Navigator](https://navigatorresearch.org/following-callais-views-on-supreme-court-remain-mixed-but-reforms-are-popular/).

**Nuances & caveats**

- Format moves the topline more than the split. Marquette's forced choice yields ~50%; YouGov's "not sure" option yields 37%; Pew's yields 46%. Under every format the Republican number stays between 18% and 39% and the party gap between 23 and 41 points.
- Marquette's own release states the contrast in one sentence: "Unlike expansion of the court, term limits for justices enjoy strong bipartisan support, with 73% of Republicans, 71% of independents, and 87% of Democrats in favor" ([May 2026 release](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32PressRelease_CourtIssues.pdf)).
- The party split has never been observed to flip because the condition that would flip it has not occurred: Republican-appointed justices have been a majority of the Court continuously since the 1970–71 term except for fourteen months in 2016–17, when, after Justice Scalia's death, the eight-member Court "were evenly divided in terms of the party of their appointing president" — no bloc held a majority (Marquette, same release). Every number above is therefore measured under one regime.
- Within that regime the numbers move with events. Democratic support ran 57% during the October 2020 confirmation (Siena, party-attributed wording), 71% in October 2024 (Marquette), and 62% in May 2026 (Marquette); Republican support ran 6% (Siena 2020), 35% (Marquette 2024), 39% (Marquette 2026). Different pollsters and wordings, so read as direction, not magnitude — but Republican support rose and Democratic support fell after Republicans regained the presidency in 2025, which is what regime-sensitivity looks like.
- We found no independent poll of the "fix the Court at nine" amendment with a party breakdown; the coalition promoting it publishes no numbers on its site ([Keep Nine](https://www.keepnineonthesupremecourt.org/)). Opposition to expansion (61–72% R; 20–38% D) is the closest proxy and points to a mirror-image split.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **39% (Republicans)** on "increase the number of justices," Marquette May 2026 — the most favorable independent reading; 27% R (Pew 2023) and 18% R (YouGov 2024) on the same idea → **Weak** per §4.1 under every independent measurement since 2019.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor.* Even the 50–54% Marquette toplines rest on 62–71% D against 35–39% R; the Pew 46% rests on 66% D / 27% R.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding.* Support is a function of which party's appointees hold the majority and which party would fill the new seats; the D-favor/R-oppose direction has been constant across seven years only because the majority has been. Ratification requires 38 states over a decade or more, spanning conditions under which the sponsoring coalition would be on the other side of the rule.
  - *Framing dependency.* No wording tested by an independent pollster brings the lower party above 39%; the honest wording ("increase the number of justices") is the one that fails.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3):* fails outright for expansion. Congress can change the number by statute, as it did repeatedly in the nineteenth century; there is no struck statute, no self-exemption, and no textual silence for an amendment to cure. The only version that needs an amendment is the one that *fixes* the number, and that is a different proposal (see §4).
  - *Symmetry (§5.2):* fails for expansion — an amendment authorizing more seats has no veil-of-ignorance form, since the benefit accrues to whoever holds the presidency and Senate when it takes effect. A fixed-number rule is symmetric by construction, but its only recorded votes are single-party, and it currently reads as protecting the present majority.
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1):* not reached; the category fails before it.

The last two rows are the point. The public demand that expansion advocates cite — a Court seen as motivated by politics (55% in Marquette Oct. 2024, up from 35% in 2019) — is real and cross-partisan; the two reforms on which both parties' numbers clear the bar are **eighteen-year fixed terms on a biennial appointment schedule** and **an enforceable ethics code**, both drafted in [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md). Fixed terms with regular appointments also address the vacancy-timing grievance that drives most expansion arguments, without changing the number of seats. Neither framing that clears is court expansion wearing another name; each is a different rule with a different bipartisan floor, and each is what we would actually draft.

A "Keep Nine" clause deserves one more sentence because it is the only expansion-adjacent text that satisfies why-an-amendment. It fails elsewhere: it has no independent polling with a party split that we could verify, its only votes are party-line, and — because a size cap taken alone entrenches whichever majority exists when it is ratified — it would need to ride *with* the term-limits and biennial-appointment design to be sellable as a rule that binds both sides. That combination is already the Target 2 package's territory (see the appointment-default candidate in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md)); a bare size cap does not rescue this category.

## 5. Path back

Reopen only on **all** of the following: (1) an independent pollster (Marquette, Pew, AP-NORC, Gallup, YouGov/Economist) shows ≥45% support in *each* party — Moderate floor — for a size rule written to bind both parties (e.g., "the number of justices may be changed only by a two-thirds vote of both houses, effective after the next presidential election," or a bare fixed-at-nine rule); (2) that reading is replicated at least once after the Court's appointing-party majority has changed hands, so the number is not regime-coded; and (3) a version of the rule is introduced with cosponsors from both parties. Absent all three, the demand for court reform continues to be served by the Term Limits and Ethics targets, and no path back for expansion itself is identified.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Clarified:** the Section 2 sentence on the Court's appointing-party majority now states that during the fourteen months in 2016–17 the eight-member Court was evenly divided by appointing party — no bloc held a majority — quoting the Marquette release ([May 2026 release](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32PressRelease_CourtIssues.pdf)). The original wording was accurate but could be read as implying a Democratic-appointed majority; no change to the gate result.
- **The 1937 precedent behind the Keep Nine argument.** The one prior attempt to add seats for the sitting president is the Judicial Procedures Reform Bill of 1937, proposed Feb. 5, 1937, which would have let the President appoint an additional justice for each sitting justice over 70 — up to six. The Senate Judiciary Committee's June 1937 report called it "a measure which should be so emphatically rejected that its parallel will never again be presented to the free representatives of the free people of America," and the Senate set the bill aside in July 1937 ([National Constitution Center, Feb. 5, 2024](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/how-fdr-lost-his-brief-war-on-the-supreme-court-2)). This history is the strongest available answer to the why-an-amendment test for the *fixed-number* proposal discussed in Section 4 — a norm that has held only politically since 1937 could be written into text — but it supplies neither a party-split poll nor bipartisan sponsorship for that proposal. Bearing on the gate: none.
