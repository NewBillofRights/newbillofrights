# Electoral College & Presidential Elections — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Verdict in one line:** 46% (Republicans) on direct popular vote, Pew Aug–Sep 2024 — and 26–43% R on every poll that uses amendment wording — fails high-topline/low-floor and incumbent/regime coding — path back: R ≥ 55% for a national-popular-vote amendment in an independent poll, sustained across an election in which the other party benefits from the Electoral College.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The core proposal is a constitutional amendment abolishing the Electoral College and electing the President and Vice President by direct national popular vote. That is the text that has been introduced in every recent Congress by Democratic members — most recently [S.J.Res.121 (118th Cong., Sen. Schatz, introduced Dec. 12, 2024, no cosponsors, referred to Judiciary)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-joint-resolution/121) and its House companion [H.J.Res.227 (Rep. Cohen, same day, referred to Judiciary)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-joint-resolution/227). Neither received a hearing.

Three variants travel under the same name:

- **Direct popular vote by amendment** (the Bayh–Celler design of 1969–70, with a 40% threshold and runoff) — the only version that has ever reached a floor vote.
- **The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact (NPV)** — a statutory workaround in which member states pledge their electors to the national popular-vote winner once states holding 270 electoral votes have joined. As of this writing the compact is enacted in **18 states plus DC, 222 electoral votes**, 48 short of activation ([National Popular Vote, state status](https://www.nationalpopularvote.com/state-status)); Virginia became the 19th jurisdiction on April 13, 2026 ([NPR, Apr. 14, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/14/nx-s1-5742595/virginia-popular-vote-compact)).
- **Allocation reforms short of abolition** — proportional or congressional-district allocation of a state's electors (the Maine/Nebraska model), which change the mechanism without touching the institution.

The category entered the pipeline from contributor **Ryan Ward** ("Eliminate the electoral college"), logged in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md). The founder's one-line rationale in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md): "Fails the bipartisan test. Open question: what is the real issue here other than perceived unfairness from the left?" The evidence below substantiates the first sentence and speaks directly to the second: the party split is real, large, and moves with which party last won the presidency while losing the popular vote.

## 2. The evidence

- **Pew Research Center, Aug 26–Sep 2, 2024 (n=9,720 adults):** 63% would prefer to change the system so the candidate who receives the most votes nationwide wins; 35% would keep the Electoral College. **80% of Democrats/leaners vs. 46% of Republicans/leaners** (53% of Republicans prefer keeping the current system). Among Republicans, 63% of conservatives prefer the current system while 61% of moderate/liberal Republicans support a popular vote — the R number is an average of two very different groups. Pew notes only 27% of Republicans supported a popular vote in November 2016, immediately after a Republican Electoral College win without a popular-vote plurality — [Pew, Sept. 25, 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/09/25/majority-of-americans-continue-to-favor-moving-away-from-electoral-college/); same figures restated in [Pew's July 28, 2026 constitutional-change roundup](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/07/28/most-americans-favor-big-changes-to-the-political-system-that-likely-would-require-amending-the-constitution/), which is the source already in the corpus.
- **Gallup, Sept 3–15, 2024 (n=1,007 adults), amendment wording** — "amend the Constitution so the candidate who receives the most total votes nationwide wins" vs. keep the Electoral College: **58% amend / 39% keep; 82% D / 60% I / 32% R.** Gallup's own by-party series is the cleanest regime-coding record in the file: **Republicans 42% (2000) → 46% (2004) → 53% (2011) → 17% (Nov 2016) → 25% (2019) → 23% (2020) → 32% (2024); Democrats 74 → 73 → 71 → 81 → 84 → 89 → 82; Independents 60 → 66 → 61 → 46 → 57 → 68 → 60.** In Gallup's 1960s–80s surveys the parties were indistinguishable — an average of 70% of Republicans and 66% of Democrats approved of an amendment to elect the president by popular vote, peaking at 80% overall in November 1968 — [Gallup, Sept. 27, 2024](https://news.gallup.com/poll/651353/americans-favor-replacing-electoral-college-system.aspx).
- **Economist/YouGov, June 13–15, 2026 (n=1,549 adult citizens)** — "Would you favor or oppose making the following amendments to the U.S. Constitution? Choose the president using popular vote, not the Electoral College": **56% favor / 23% oppose / 21% not sure; 78% D / 49% I / 43% R** (Republicans 43 favor / 40 oppose) — [crosstabs, p. 15](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_GpnDRNm.pdf) ([poll write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/54990-trumps-job-approval-rebounds-americans-lack-confidence-in-elections-abortion-views-and-more-june-13-15-2026-economist-yougov-poll)). Newest independent number in the file; the R floor is 43% with a large undecided share.
- **Economist/YouGov, Dec 29–31, 2024 (n=1,552 adult citizens)** — "Amend the Constitution so the candidate who receives the most total votes nationwide wins" vs. "keep the current system": **44% amend / 36% keep / 20% not sure; 66% D / 41% I / 26% R** (Republicans 26 amend / 61 keep). Fielded seven weeks after an election in which the Republican nominee won both the popular vote and the Electoral College — [crosstabs, p. 54](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_Goy6hsR.pdf) ([poll write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/51212-new-government-maga-republicans-january-6-election-2025-december-21-24-2024-december-29-31-2024-economist-yougov-poll)).
- **Pew, July 2023 (published Sept 19, 2023):** 65% change to popular vote / 33% keep; **82% D / 47% R** — [Pew, "How Americans View Proposals to Change the Political System"](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/). Pew's overall trend (same question family): 61% (Nov 2000), 62% (2011), 51% (Nov 2016), 55% (2018), 60% (Jan 2020), 55% (Jan 2021), 63% (2022), 65% (2023), 63% (2024) — [Pew trend chart](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/by-a-wide-margin-americans-want-popular-vote-not-electoral-college-to-decide-who-is-president/). Pew's Jan 2020 write-up records that "about half of Republicans (51%)" favored amending in 2011 and that "nearly two-thirds" of Republicans preferred keeping the current system by 2020, with 81% of Democrats favoring amendment — [Pew, Mar. 13, 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/03/13/a-majority-of-americans-continue-to-favor-replacing-electoral-college-with-a-nationwide-popular-vote/).
- **Ballot result — Colorado Proposition 113 (Nov 3, 2020), referendum on the state's NPV law:** approved **1,644,716 yes (52.3%) to 1,498,500 no (47.7%)** — [Colorado Secretary of State, official results](https://historicalelectiondata.coloradosos.gov/contest/3498). The only statewide popular vote ever held on the compact; it passed narrowly in a state the Democratic presidential nominee carried the same day. This is a same-lean state result, not the opposite-lean wide-margin result the evidence hierarchy (§4.2) weights heavily.
- **Legislative record — the 1969–70 near miss:** the House passed H.J.Res.681 (direct election, 40% threshold with runoff) on Sept. 18, 1969, **338–70**, after the Judiciary Committee reported it 28–6; both the Speaker and the House Republican Leader had called for electoral reform after the 1968 election — [U.S. House, History, Art & Archives](https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1951-2000/1969_09_18_Constitutional-Amendment-to-Abolish-the-Electoral-College/). In the Senate, cloture on S.J.Res.1 failed **54–36 (Sept. 17, 1970)** and **53–34 (Sept. 29, 1970)** under the two-thirds rule then in force, and the motion was withdrawn Oct. 5 — [U.S. Senate, cloture motions, 91st Congress](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/cloture/91.htm). This is the only time either chamber has voted on abolition, and it was genuinely cross-party — before the split of the last quarter-century existed.
- **Legislative record — the compact:** enacted in 18 states plus DC (222 electoral votes) and passed by one chamber in six additional states with 61 electoral votes (Arizona, Arkansas, Michigan, Nevada, North Carolina, Oklahoma) — [NPV state status](https://www.nationalpopularvote.com/state-status) (advocacy site; enactment counts are matters of public record and match [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/14/nx-s1-5742595/virginia-popular-vote-compact)). NPR attributes the most recent enactment, Virginia's, to the Democratic trifecta produced by the 2025 state elections; the compact's own senior consultant told NPR that lawsuits would be likely if it reaches 270.

**Nuances & caveats**

- Wording moves the R number by 10–20 points. Pew's "change the current system" phrasing (46–47% R, 2023–24) runs consistently above amendment-worded questions fielded in the same period (Gallup 32% R, Sept 2024; Economist/YouGov 26% R, Dec 2024). Pew's own Jan 2020 split-sample found "no substantive differences" between "amending the Constitution" and "changing the system," so the gap is more likely pollster/mode and timing than wording alone — but the amendment is what we would draft, and every amendment-worded independent poll puts the R floor below 45.
- The June 2026 Economist/YouGov item (43% R favor, 40% oppose, 16% unsure) is a favor/oppose battery of five amendments, not a forced choice against the current system; per §11, battery numbers are softer than head-to-head numbers, and it should not be read as a recovery to the Pew level.
- Independents are not a tiebreaker: 60% (Gallup 2024), 49% (Economist/YouGov June 2026), 41% (Dec 2024) — Moderate at best, and the same regime dip in 2016 (46%).
- The Democratic side is also regime-sensitive, just less visibly: D support rose from 71–74% (2000–2011) to 81–89% after 2016 and eased to 82% in 2024. Neither party's number is a fixed principle.
- The Colorado 2020 result is the only real ballot on this question and it was a 4.7-point margin in a state the Democratic nominee carried. There is no opposite-lean-state result of any kind.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **46% (Republicans)** on replacing the Electoral College with a national popular vote, Pew Aug–Sep 2024 (n=9,720) → **borderline Moderate/Weak** per §4.1 — the single best-polled number sits at the bottom edge of Moderate (45–59), while every independent poll using amendment wording is Weak: 32% R (Gallup 2024), 26% R (Economist/YouGov Dec 2024), 43% R (Economist/YouGov June 2026). No independent poll in this file since 2011 shows a Republican majority for abolition; Gallup's last R majority was 53% in October 2011.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor:* 63% overall hides an 80/46 split (Pew 2024); 58% overall hides 82/32 (Gallup 2024); 56% overall hides 78/43 (Economist/YouGov 2026). A 34–50-point party gap on every independent measurement.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding:* Republican support was 53% in Oct 2011, fell to 17% (Gallup) / 27% (Pew) in Nov 2016 after a Republican Electoral College win without a popular-vote plurality, recovered to 32–46% by 2024, and dropped again to 26% (Economist/YouGov) in the weeks after a Republican won both the popular and electoral vote — while Democratic support moved in the opposite direction after 2016. The 1960s–80s parity (70% R / 66% D) shows the split is a product of the 2000 and 2016 outcomes, not of durable ideology. Under §11, a floor measured while one side believes the rule favors it is not a floor.
  - *Framing dependency:* the only wording that reaches the mid-40s among Republicans is "change the system," not "amend the Constitution"; there is no honest amendment framing on record that clears 45.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem:* passable in principle — the possibility of a president elected against the national plurality, and of a contingent election in the House, are structural questions about democratic legitimacy — but the founder's open question stands: the record shows the felt problem is currently one-sided (the two modern popular-vote/electoral-vote splits both went the same way).
  - *Symmetry (veil of ignorance):* fails as currently framed. A rule can be written symmetrically, but the evidence shows both parties evaluate it by who won last; the 1969–70 vote is the only moment on record when it did not read as one side's remedy.
  - *Why an amendment:* satisfied — Article II and the Twelfth Amendment fix the mechanism; the compact is a statutory workaround whose constitutionality (Compact Clause, presidential-elector clause) would be litigated on activation, as the compact's own consultant acknowledged to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/14/nx-s1-5742595/virginia-popular-vote-compact). This test is not the reason for rejection.

## 4. Framings tested

- **"Change the system so the most-votes candidate wins" (Pew):** 46–47% R (2023–24). Highest R number on record since 2011; borderline Moderate; still a 33–35-point gap. Not a different proposal — it is the amendment described without the word "amend."
- **"Amend the Constitution so the candidate with the most total votes nationwide wins" (Gallup 2024; Economist/YouGov Dec 2024):** 32% R and 26% R. Weak. This is the wording closest to what we would draft.
- **Favor/oppose amendment battery (Economist/YouGov June 2026):** 43% R favor, 40% oppose. Weak, and softer than a forced choice.
- **The compact route (statute, not amendment):** 52.3% yes in Colorado 2020; 19 enactments, 48 electoral votes short. Wearing the same name but a different instrument, and one that fails §5.3 in reverse — it is the argument that no amendment is needed.
- **Proportional or district allocation of electors:** no independent national poll with party splits found for 2020–2026 (see JSON `unverified`). Structurally these are state-law choices (Maine and Nebraska already use districts) and do not require an amendment, so they would fail §5.3 even if they polled well.
- **No framing clears the bar.** The historical framing that did — the 1969 House vote and the 1968 Gallup 80% — predates the modern split and cannot be recreated by wording.

## 5. Path back

Reopen if an independent pollster (Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac) shows **≥55% of Republicans favoring an amendment to elect the president by national popular vote** in head-to-head wording, **and** the number holds in a poll fielded after a presidential election the Democratic nominee won — the configuration under which Republican support last approached parity (Gallup, Oct 2011: 53% R, three years after a Democratic nominee's win). Short of that, the category can also return if a genuinely symmetric sub-issue emerges with its own polling (for example, contingent-election reform, which no independent pollster has tested with party splits); today none is identified. Republican support has been climbing since 2020 (Gallup 23 → 32; Pew 27 → 46; Economist/YouGov 26 → 43), so a re-check after the 2028 election is warranted.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)

*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Compact Clause, cited.** §3 already names the Compact Clause as the constitutional question the compact would face on activation; the text is Article I §10, clause 3: "No State shall, without the Consent of Congress, … enter into any Agreement or Compact with another State" — [Cornell LII, Article I §10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei). Whether the compact needs congressional consent, and whether Congress could give it by statute, is unlitigated; either way the compact is a statutory route and the amendment remains the only instrument that changes Article II and the Twelfth Amendment themselves. No change to the §5.3 result ("satisfied").
- Reviewer tone notes: "perceived unfairness from the left" is the founder's recorded rationale and is marked as a quotation from CATEGORIES.md; the phrase "neither can be sold with paired abuses because each side denies the other's premise" does not appear in this memo (it is in the Voting Rights memo). Neither was changed.
