# Senate / Congressional Structure — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Verdict in one line:** 19% (Republicans) on amending the Constitution so more-populous states have more senators, Pew 2023 → Weak — fails high-topline/low-floor (in fact low-topline/low-floor), symmetry, and the feasibility half of why-an-amendment (Article V's equal-suffrage clause) — path back: none identified for proportionality or abolition; the live, bipartisan-testable energy on "congressional structure" sits in the House-expansion candidate.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

"Reform the Senate" entered the pipeline as a next-tier target named by **Ryan Ward** ("the Senate," specifics not discussed — [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)). The public-thread mining in [REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md) then showed what "the Senate" means when a crowd fills in the blank: proportionality and abolition drew a large share of the thread and the sharpest partisan fights in it, with the original poster repeatedly citing Article V's consent requirement. Four distinct ideas travel under this label and must be kept apart, because they have different constituencies and different legal status:

- **(a) Proportional Senate representation** — amend Article I, §3 so that states with more people have more senators. No joint resolution proposing this has been introduced in the current Congress that we could locate; it lives in commentary and advocacy (e.g., the Democratic Socialists of America's August 2026 platform, below) rather than in bills.
- **(b) Abolishing the Senate** (unicameral Congress) — the DSA's "Workers Deserve More" platform, unveiled August 2026, calls for abolishing the Senate and replacing the presidency and Supreme Court with organs "chosen by and subordinate to Congress"; Sen. Bernie Sanders' response when asked was "No" ([VTDigger, Aug 4, 2026](https://vtdigger.org/2026/08/04/democratic-socialists-of-america-wants-to-abolish-the-u-s-senate-bernie-sanders-says-no/)); the National Republican Senatorial Committee has demanded that nine named Democratic Senate candidates disavow it ([NRSC, July 28, 2026](https://www.nrsc.org/press-releases/will-democrats-disavow-dsas-calls-to-abolish-the-senate-2026-07-28/)).
- **(c) Repealing the Seventeenth Amendment** — returning the choice of senators to state legislatures. This is the one Senate-structure idea with live legislative vehicles, all Republican-sponsored: [H.J.Res.198, 119th Cong.](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hjres198), introduced June 25, 2026 by Rep. Keith Self with nine cosponsors, all Republican; Arizona's [HCM 2010](https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-01-29/proposed-measure-to-urge-congress-to-restore-legislature-not-voters-picking-u-s-senators) (Jan. 2026), a memorial urging Congress to repeal the amendment, sponsored by a Republican legislator and opposed by the Democratic House leader as "a power grab"; and an [ALEC model resolution](https://alec.org/model-policy/draft-resolution-recommending-constitutional-amendment-restoring-election-of-u-s-senators-to-the-legislatures-of-the-sovereign-states-2/) to the same effect.
- **(d) and (e) — not in this memo.** House expansion / re-apportionment (the Wyoming Rule, cube-root rule, repeal of the 1929 cap) is a **candidate**, not a rejection, added 2026-08-12 from the Reddit thread and gated on new polling ([CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md)). Filibuster reform is likewise a candidate (verdict "unstable" — [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). DC and Puerto Rico statehood, which would expand the Senate, are handled under the statehood candidate. Each is mentioned here only to say where it lives.

The rejected category is therefore (a), (b), and (c): changes to *how the Senate is constituted* — its apportionment, its existence, or the mode of choosing its members.

## 2. The evidence

- **Proportional representation — Pew Research Center, July 10–16, 2023 (N=8,480; item asked of half-sample, N=4,247).** Wording: "the Constitution requires that all states have two U.S. senators, regardless of how many people live in the state. Which comes closer to your view?" — *amend so that states with larger populations have more senators than smaller states* **32%**, vs. *should not be changed; all states continue to have two senators* **66%**. **Republicans/lean Rep 19% amend / 80% keep; Democrats/lean Dem 45% / 54%; independents 32% / 65%.** Adults under 30: 45% amend; 65+: 18% — [Pew report page](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/); party table and topline in the [full report PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/09/PP_2023.09.19_views-of-politics_REPORT.pdf) (pp. 93–94, 141). This is the best-polled core proposal and the anchor for the verdict.
- **Same item, Pew, Jan. 29–Feb. 13, 2018.** Amend **24%** / keep **75%**; **Republicans 14% amend / 85% keep; Democrats 31% / 68%** (no independent breakdown published) — [Pew, ch. 5, "The Electoral College, Congress and Representation"](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/5-the-electoral-college-congress-and-representation/). Between 2018 and 2023 Democratic support rose 14 points and Republican support 5; the gap widened from 17 to 26 points.
- **Bright Line Watch (YouGov public sample, Nov. 10–19, 2021, N=2,750; expert sample Nov. 5–19, 2021, N=564).** Asked to choose between "equal representation for all states in the U.S. Senate or a system in which states with more people would have more senators": **36% of the public** favor representation by population, vs. **72% of political-science experts**; **Democrats 56%; Republicans 17% (83% keep two per state); independents ~25% (75% keep)** — [Bright Line Watch, Jan. 2022 report](https://brightlinewatch.org/tempered-expectations-and-hardened-divisions-a-year-into-the-biden-presidency/). Academic survey, forced choice between two options rather than a favor/oppose item; it corroborates Pew's Republican floor within two points and shows the Democratic majority for the idea is bare even at its 2021 high.
- **Abolishing the Senate — no independent national poll with a party breakdown located.** Newsweek's August 8, 2026 review of the DSA platform's polling notes that Senate abolition is among the planks with "limited national polling" and reports no figure — [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/is-the-dsa-platform-unpopular-what-polls-reveal-about-5-key-issues-12297503). Treat as **Unpolled**; the political record (Sanders' "No"; NRSC's use of it as an attack line) is evidence of coding, not of support.
- **Repealing the Seventeenth Amendment — no independent poll with a party breakdown could be verified** (see the JSON `unverified` list). What can be documented is that every vehicle is single-party: H.J.Res.198's ten sponsors are all Republican ([GovTrack record](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hjres198)); the Arizona memorial split on party lines in the coverage ([KJZZ](https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-01-29/proposed-measure-to-urge-congress-to-restore-legislature-not-voters-picking-u-s-senators)). Idea (c) is the mirror image of (a): each side's Senate-structure proposal is one the other side reads as shifting power toward the proposer's coalition.
- **Legislative votes.** None. No Senate-structure amendment has received a committee vote in the modern era; H.J.Res.198 was referred on introduction and has 9 cosponsors ([GovTrack](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hjres198)).
- **Ballot results.** None exist; there is no state-level analogue to a change in the federal Senate's apportionment.
- **The structural bar — constitutional text.** Article V ends: "*and that no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate*" — [National Archives transcript](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript). The Constitution Annotated (Library of Congress; LII mirror) treats this as an unamendable subject: a state's consent is required to alter its Senate representation, and the argument that the clause could first be repealed and the Senate then re-apportioned in a second step "would still violate Article V's plain language" — [ArtV.5 Unamendable Subjects](https://law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/article-5/unamendable-subjects), citing Mader, 99 Marq. L. Rev. 841 (2016), and Albert, 13 Int'l J. Const. L. 655 (2015). The National Constitution Center's common-interpretation essay (David A. Strauss) reads it the same way: "every state will have the same number of Senators, no matter how many members of Congress or other states want to change that part" — [NCC](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/articles/article-v/interpretations/277). Practically, proportionality requires the consent of every state that would lose seats — a unanimity rule, not three-fourths.

**Nuances & caveats**

- The Pew and Bright Line Watch items are binary choices (amend vs. keep; population vs. equal), not favor/oppose scales; the "amend" shares are therefore *ceilings* for support of any specific proportional design, not measures of intensity.
- Neither item names a mechanism (how many senators per state, floor for small states, transition). Support for a concrete design would almost certainly be lower than the abstract 32–36%.
- No 2024–2026 independent re-ask was found. The trend (2018 → 2021 → 2023) moves in one direction — Democratic support rising, Republican support flat below 20% — which widens rather than narrows the split. Both parties' figures are **regime-coded** in the structural sense: each side's view tracks whether the small-state bonus currently helps or hurts its coalition, and that alignment has changed over the country's history and can change again.
- Bright Line Watch's public sample was fielded by YouGov, so the two anchors are not fully independent in method (both online panels), though they are independent in sponsorship and wording.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **19% (Republicans)** on amending the Constitution so more-populous states have more senators, Pew July 2023 → **Weak** per §4.1. Corroborated by 17% R (Bright Line Watch, Nov. 2021) and 14% R (Pew, 2018). Abolition: **Unpolled**. Seventeenth-Amendment repeal: **Unpolled** by our standard (no verified independent party breakdown); elite support single-party.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — the category's canonical failure, in an unusual form: the topline itself is a minority (32–36%), and the floor is a fifth of that party. Even the *higher* party is split (45% D in Pew, 56% D in BLW). There is no majority anywhere in the electorate for the core proposal.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — the split maps onto which coalition currently benefits from equal state suffrage; the 17th-Amendment variant is coded the opposite direction by the same logic. Neither variant can be written behind the veil because both are about who wins under the new map.
  - *Framing dependency* — no honest framing found that clears the bar (Section 4).
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem:* arguable, and the memo takes no view; the pipeline entry named no specific problem, and the thread's arguments were about fairness of representation — a real concern but one the mission cannot adjudicate without a floor.
  - *Symmetry:* **fails.** Proportionality shifts power from less-populous to more-populous states; abolition removes the states' chamber; 17th-Amendment repeal shifts power from voters to legislatures. Each has an identifiable winner at the moment of adoption and cannot be paired with "the other side's abuse" because there is no abuse — there is a design each side dislikes.
  - *Why-an-amendment:* passed trivially — only an amendment can touch Article I, §3 — **but the same clause that makes it amendment-only makes it a feasibility fail.** Article V's equal-suffrage proviso means (a) requires unanimous consent of the losing states and (b) is at minimum contested under the same clause; even a Strong floor could not carry them to ratification. Only (c), repeal of the Seventeenth Amendment, is procedurally an ordinary amendment, and it is the variant with no cross-party support to point to.

## 4. Framings tested

- **"States with more people should have more senators" (Pew 2023):** 32% overall, **19% R** / 45% D. Weak. This is the thing we would actually draft if we drafted anything, and it fails.
- **Same idea as a forced choice against equal representation (Bright Line Watch 2021):** 36% overall, **17% R** / 56% D / ~25% I. Weak. Confirms Pew.
- **"Abolish the Senate":** unpolled independently; politically owned by one faction and used by the other party's Senate committee as an attack ([NRSC](https://www.nrsc.org/press-releases/will-democrats-disavow-dsas-calls-to-abolish-the-senate-2026-07-28/)). No independent number to test; nothing suggests it would poll above proportionality.
- **"Repeal the Seventeenth Amendment / let legislatures choose senators":** no verified independent party breakdown; all vehicles single-party. Coded right as squarely as (a) and (b) are coded left. Not a rescue — the opposite coding of the same category.
- **Structure-preserving variants (e.g., a different number of senators per state with equality kept, or term or staggering changes):** amendable under Article V by the ordinary route, per the Constitution Annotated reading that equality, not the number, is what is entrenched ([ArtV.5](https://law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/article-5/unamendable-subjects)). Never polled; they also do not answer the grievance that motivates the category, so they are a different proposal wearing the name.
- **Framings that clear the bar — none in this category.** The bipartisan energy that reads as "congressional structure" is elsewhere and already filed: **House expansion** (Pew 2023: 29% increase / 53% keep 435 / 15% decrease; 23% R / 35% D — the same [Pew report](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/); the top-scored idea in the Reddit thread), which is a **candidate** gated on Wyoming-Rule / cube-root polling and is not rescued by any number here; **congressional term limits** (87%, 90R/86D — a target); and the **filibuster** (candidate, unstable). None of these changes how the Senate is apportioned or chosen, and none should be described as a Senate-structure amendment.

## 5. Path back

**None identified for proportional representation or abolition**, for two independent reasons that each suffice: the Republican floor has never exceeded 19% in three independent measurements across five years, and Article V's equal-suffrage proviso means that even a Strong floor could not produce ratification without every losing state's consent. Repeal of the Seventeenth Amendment could in principle be re-examined if an independent pollster (Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, YouGov/Economist) published a favor/oppose item on "senators chosen by state legislatures rather than by voters" with a Democratic floor of at least 45% — no such figure is known, and the legislative record points the other way. The category should be revisited only if a future Pew or comparable re-ask of the 2023 Senate item shows Republican support at 45% or better; even then the Article V analysis would have to be resolved before drafting. Where "congressional structure" *can* move, the corpus already tracks it: the House-expansion candidate.
