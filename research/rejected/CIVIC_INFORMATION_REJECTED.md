# Civic Information & Informed Citizenry — Rejected Category Memo

*Rejected 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-17 to substantiate the rejection under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Verdict in one line:** Unpolled / not applicable — fails the existential-problem test (§5.1) and the why-an-amendment-not-a-statute test (§5.3), not the polling gate; no poll has ever asked about a civic-information amendment, and the one mechanism proposed (AI) is distrusted by both parties (Pew 2024: 54% R / 49% D expect AI to harm the news) — path back: none as an amendment; build it as a nonprofit, a company, or a statute.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The idea entered the pipeline from **Austin Burns** ([AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md), "Rejected Ideas"): incorporate **bias-free AI systems** to keep citizens informed of changing legislation and how it directly affects their lives — described by the contributor as "a game changer for a completely democratic electoral process." The founder's one-line rejection, recorded in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md): "Great idea for a new company or non-profit, but doesn't yet meet the bar for popular support. Adding good-government ideas is interesting, but unclear how to build enough popular support to pass an amendment." [SELECTION_METHOD.md §5.1](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) files the rejection under the **existential-problem test**, and pairs it with the parked candidate "good-government data (USAFacts-style)," held back for the same reason despite likely toplines of 70–90%.

As an amendment the idea has three possible shapes, none of which anyone has drafted:

1. **A mandate on the government** to operate or fund a nonpartisan (or "bias-free") information system that explains pending and enacted legislation to citizens.
2. **A citizen's right to be informed** — a positive right to plain-language, timely information about laws that affect them, enforceable against Congress and agencies.
3. **A constitutional duty of transparency / right to know** — the shape state constitutions actually use (Florida Art. I §24; California Art. I §3(b), added by Prop 59 in 2004): a right of access to government records and meetings, with the machinery left to the legislature.

Only the third has any track record. No joint resolution proposing a federal civic-information, right-to-know, or informed-citizenry amendment was located on congress.gov (searches for "right to know," "right to information," and "informed" among proposed amendments return campaign-finance disclosure and corporate-personhood resolutions, not this). The federal statutory analogue is the [Plain Writing Act of 2010 (Pub. L. 111-274)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/111th-congress/house-bill/946), which requires agencies to write public-facing documents in plain language; it passed the House 386–33 and 341–82 and the Senate by unanimous consent — a cross-party vote, and evidence that the underlying goal is *legislatively* achievable without constitutional text.

## 2. The evidence

No poll asks about a constitutional amendment on civic information, an AI-run civic-information system, or a federal "right to be informed." The evidence below is therefore adjacent: (a) public trust in the AI mechanism the proposal names; (b) civic-knowledge baselines that describe the problem; (c) support for civics education, the closest bipartisan item; (d) ballot results for state right-to-know amendments, the closest constitutional precedent.

**(a) Trust in AI as a civic-information source — independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Pew Research Center, Aug 12–18, 2024 (n=5,410; published April 2025).** 50% of U.S. adults say AI will have a negative impact on "the news people get in the U.S." over the next 20 years (24% very, 26% somewhat); 10% say positive. **54% R / 49% D** say negative. **67% R / 68% D** are extremely or very concerned about people getting inaccurate information from AI (66% overall). 41% say AI would do a worse job than journalists writing a news story; 19% better. — [Pew, "Americans largely foresee AI having negative effects on news, journalists"](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/04/28/americans-largely-foresee-ai-having-negative-effects-on-news-journalists/)
- **Pew, June 9–15, 2025 (n=5,023).** 50% are "more concerned than excited" about increased use of AI in daily life (up from 37% in 2021); **50% R / 51% D** — the parties have converged; Republicans down 9 points since 2023, Democrats up from 31% (2021) and 46% (2023). — [Pew, Nov 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/11/06/republicans-democrats-now-equally-concerned-about-ai-in-daily-life-but-views-on-regulation-differ/); [Pew, Sept 2025 report](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2025/09/17/how-americans-view-ai-and-its-impact-on-people-and-society/)
- **Pew, March 24–30, 2025 (n=3,605) — regime-coded item.** Trust in "the U.S." to regulate AI effectively: 44% overall; **54% R / 36% D**; distrust 38% R / 56% D. — same Pew Nov 2025 short read. **Pew, Feb 17–23, 2026 (n=5,119):** 67% have little or no confidence in the U.S. government to regulate AI; **61% R / 74% D** lack confidence — Pew notes "in a shift, Democrats are now more skeptical than the GOP," with Republican skepticism falling from 70% (2024) to 61% and Democratic skepticism rising from 54% to 74%. Six-in-ten lack confidence in U.S. companies to develop AI responsibly (**53% R / 65% D**). 49% of adults have used a chatbot; 13% of users use them for news. — [Pew, "Americans and AI 2026"](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)
- **Pew, Aug 2025.** 9% of U.S. adults get news at least sometimes from AI chatbots; 75% never; about half of those who do say they at least sometimes encounter news there they think is inaccurate (no party breakdown published). — [Pew key findings, March 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/03/12/key-findings-about-how-americans-view-artificial-intelligence/)
- **AP-NORC / USAFacts "State of the Facts," July 29–Aug 8, 2024 (n=1,019).** 64% not confident that information from AI chatbots or AI search results is reliable and factual; 27% trust AI even a moderate amount to provide accurate information about the election outcome; 43% think AI will make it harder to find factual election information, 16% easier; 49% believe federal-government information is politically influenced (no party breakdown published for the AI items). — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/state-of-the-facts-2024/); [USAFacts write-up](https://usafacts.org/articles/state-of-the-facts-2024/). USAFacts is a nonpartisan data nonprofit that co-sponsors the series; the fieldwork is AP-NORC's.
- **Gallup / Bentley University, May 4–11, 2026 (n=3,270).** 39% say AI does more harm than good (31% in 2025); 27% trust businesses "a lot" or "some" to use AI responsibly (31% in 2025); Republicans remain the most trusting group at **35% R** (41% in 2025); Democratic figure not published on the page. — [Gallup, July 2026](https://news.gallup.com/poll/712751/americans-cool-toward.aspx)
- **Reuters Institute (Oxford), Generative AI and News Report 2025 — six countries incl. the U.S., fieldwork June 5–July 15, 2025, ~2,000 per country.** Only 12% are comfortable with news made entirely by AI vs. 62% for entirely human-made news (six-country aggregate; no U.S. party breakdown). Respondents expect AI to make news cheaper (+39 net) and less trustworthy (−19 net). — [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2025-how-people-think-about-ais-role-journalism-and-society). The [2026 Digital News Report](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/dnr-executive-summary) puts trust in AI-chatbot answers at 20% globally (vs. 37% for news overall) and reports no increase in U.S. chatbot news use over the year.

**(b) The problem the proposal describes — civic-knowledge baselines**

- **Annenberg Public Policy Center Constitution Day Civics Survey, 2025 (three waves, March–Aug 2025; n≈1,300–1,700 each; ±3.5).** 70% can name all three branches of government (65% in 2024; 32% in 2018); 13% cannot name any. 79% can name freedom of speech as a First Amendment right; 34% freedom of the press; 12% right to petition. Annenberg reports "no significant difference … based on party affiliation." — [APPC, Sept 2025](https://www.annenbergpublicpolicycenter.org/americans-knowledge-of-civics-increases-annenberg-survey-finds/)

**(c) The bipartisan adjacent item — civics education**

- **USC Dornsife CESR / Understanding America Study, Oct–Dec 2024 (n=4,200; 81% response), report "Agreement Across the Aisle," March 2025.** 97% say preparing students to "be good citizens" should be an educational priority (**97% R / 97% D**, 90% other). Learning "how the U.S. government system works (e.g., the three branches)" rated important by **95% R / 94% D** — the single most bipartisan item of sixteen. Requirements for voting: 88% R / 90% D. Federal influence over state and local affairs: 82% R / 86% D. Learning about the Constitution and learning to detect false information online also rate in the high 80s–90s across parties (figures shown graphically in the report; not reproduced here). Partisan gaps narrowed since 2021, driven by rising Republican ratings. — [USC report PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/cesr/wp-content/uploads/sites/54/2025/03/UAS_CARE_Agreement_Across_The_Aisle.pdf); [USC Rossier summary](https://rossier.usc.edu/news-insights/news/2025/february/while-most-americans-strongly-support-civics-education-schools-partisan-divide-dei-policies-and-free)

**(d) Constitutional precedent — state right-to-know amendments (real ballot results)**

- **Florida Amendment 2, Nov 3, 1992 — Access to Government Records and Meetings (now Art. I §24).** Approved **83.04%** (3,883,637 yes / 793,232 no). — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_Amendment_2,_Access_to_Government_Records_and_Meetings_Amendment_(1992)); ballot text at [FSU College of Law, 1992 amendments](https://library.law.fsu.edu/Digital-Collections/CRC/CRC-1998/conhist/1992amen.html)
- **California Proposition 59, Nov 2, 2004 — right of access to public records and meetings made a constitutional right.** Approved **83.4%** (9,334,852 / 1,870,146). — [UC Berkeley IGS](https://igs.berkeley.edu/library/california-ballot-proposition-guides/november-2-2004-general-election-summary/november-2-1)

**(e) Advocacy / sponsor-adjacent**

- **USAFacts / Harris Poll "State of the Facts," 2017 (n=2,521; sponsor-commissioned).** 90% say data is critical to believing information; 81% say information must come from a nonpartisan source to be credible; 76% feel government expenditure information is biased; 77% rate civic education fair or poor (no party breakdown published). — [USAFacts release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/usafacts-releases-inaugural-state-of-the-facts-poll-300488327.html). These are the numbers already carried in [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) for the good-government-data candidate; they re-verified.

**Nuances & caveats**

- Every number above is adjacent. Not one item measures favor/oppose on the thing that would be drafted. Civics-education support (95% R / 94% D) is support for a *school curriculum decision made by states and districts*, not for a federal information system, and cannot be attached to this category as a floor (§11: broad or adjacent numbers are not evidence for a specific mechanism).
- The AI numbers describe *distrust of the mechanism*, not opposition to the goal. They are also moving fast — Pew's concern measure went from 37% to 50% in four years and Gallup's harm measure from 31% to 39% in one — so any AI-anchored framing would be built on a shifting base.
- The one regime-coded item is exactly the one this proposal would depend on: trust in the *government* to oversee AI flipped between parties when the White House changed hands (Pew: R skepticism 70→61, D 54→74, 2024→2026). A government-run "bias-free" information system inherits that coding.
- State right-to-know results are twenty to thirty years old and were fought over records access, not AI or legislative explanation; they show a *transparency duty* is ratifiable at 83%, nothing more.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **Unpolled** — no credible national data on any version of the proposal (§4.1). The nearest adjacent bipartisan item is 94% D / 95% R for teaching how government works (USC/UAS 2024), which is a different proposal; the nearest AI-mechanism item shows both parties expecting harm (54% R / 49% D, Pew 2024). Neither can serve as a floor.
- **Failure mode(s) (§4.3):** Not the primary basis. Two are latent if the idea were polled: **incumbent/regime coding** (trust in government to oversee AI flips with the presidency — Pew 2025/2026) and **framing dependency** (an "informed citizenry" or "transparency" wording would likely poll far above an "AI system run by the government" wording, but the former is not the contributor's proposal).
- **Non-polling tests (§5):** This is where the category fails, and it fails two of three.
  - **Existential-problem test (§5.1): fails.** Civic-knowledge baselines are low (30% cannot name all three branches; 66% cannot name freedom of the press) but the corpus files it as a good-government nicety, not a threat that requires a rule binding future majorities forever. The founder's own words — "a great idea for a new company or non-profit" — are the test's application. The parallel USAFacts-style candidate is parked on the same ground.
  - **Why-an-amendment test (§5.3): fails.** None of the four accepted answers applies: no court has struck a civic-information statute; the Court has not exempted itself; Congress can plainly self-bind (it did, in the Plain Writing Act, 386–33); and the text's silence is not what any defiant actor cites. Every deliverable the proposal names — plain-language bill summaries, impact explainers, notification of changes in law — is a statute, an appropriation, or a product.
  - **Symmetry test (§5.2): passes on its face** (an information duty binds all administrations equally), but with a First Amendment complication stated below.
- **The First Amendment problem, stated factually.** A constitutionally mandated, government-operated system that decides what "bias-free" information citizens receive would be *government speech* about pending legislation, an area where each side already accuses the other of official messaging campaigns; it would sit beside the Press Clause and Petition Clause without displacing them, and would invite litigation over who certifies "bias-free." A *right of access* to records (the Florida/California shape) has no such problem — which is why that shape, not an information-provision mandate, is what states have ratified.

## 5. Path back

**None identified as an amendment.** The evidence that would reopen the category is not a better poll on AI; it is a change in what the proposal *is*. Two things would be required together: (1) a named independent poll on a specific constitutional wording — e.g., "a constitutional right of every person to access the records and proceedings of the federal government, with exemptions set by law" — showing a floor of ≥60% in the less-supportive party; and (2) a demonstrated statutory failure (a court striking a federal transparency or plain-language statute, or a documented refusal by both branches to comply) that answers §5.3. Absent both, the right shape for the idea is a nonprofit or company (the founder's framing), a plain-language-legislation statute extending the 2010 Act's principle to bills, or a state right-to-know amendment where none exists. Should the foundation ever pursue the parked good-government-data candidate, this category would ride with it under a transparency heading, not an AI heading.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **A pending-legislation "informed citizenry" proposal exists — as a statute.** The Read the Bills Act, drafted by the advocacy group Downsize DC and introduced in the Senate as [S. 3879 (116th Congress), June 3, 2020](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-116s3879is/html/BILLS-116s3879is.htm), would bar a final-passage vote unless the full text has been posted on an official website "at least 7 days before the vote," read verbatim to a quorum of each House, and each member voting yes signs an affidavit under penalty of perjury; it was referred on introduction to the Committee on Rules and Administration ([Downsize DC](https://downsizedc.org/rand-paul-read-bills/)). It is a bill amending Title 1 of the U.S. Code, not a proposed constitutional amendment, and no independent poll of it with a party breakdown could be located (an outside note called it "highly polled"; that claim was not verifiable). Bearing on the gate: none on the floor — it is one more instance of the pattern in §5.3 that every civic-information deliverable is drafted, when drafted at all, as a statute or a chamber rule.
