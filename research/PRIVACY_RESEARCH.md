# Privacy Research — Federal Bills, State & International Models, Amendment Framework

Research supporting Target Category 4 (Privacy) in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md). Compiled 2026-08-06. Companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md) (public-opinion evidence). Framework seeded by Michael Hamilton; bill, state-model, and international research from primary sources linked throughout.

**The headline findings:**

1. Across every major federal privacy bill from both parties (2018–2026), the *substance* is largely settled — individual rights over data, minimization, sensitive-data protection, security. Every bill died over *machinery*: preemption and the private right of action. A constitutional amendment resolves exactly those two questions at the level of principle (an individually enforceable right that sets a floor).
2. There is **no failed federal privacy amendment** in history — Congress deliberately chose the statutory path after Watergate. No baggage, but no muscle memory either.
3. Eleven states already have constitutional privacy text, some 50+ years old and court-tested. Voters ratify this stuff by landslides: New Hampshire's 2018 informational-privacy amendment passed with ~81%, Michigan's 2020 electronic-data amendment with ~89%, Missouri's 2014 with ~75%.
4. The government-facing side is the bipartisan sweet spot (a data-purchase warrant rule passed the House 219–199 with majorities of both parties' yes votes); the commercial side is a live statutory contest an amendment can anchor but never fully resolve.
5. Internationally, the US is a conspicuous outlier: roughly **186 of ~193 national constitutions** contain a privacy provision. The core principles are globally settled and 45+ years stable (legality, proportionality, purpose limitation, security, access/correction, remedy); the transferable ratification model is Brazil's — statute first, court recognition second, unanimous constitutional lock-in third. See International Models below.

## Amendment Framework — What a Privacy Amendment Might Cover

A constitutional amendment on privacy would explicitly enshrine an individual's right to be let alone and to control their personal data, moving beyond today's implied protections (Fourth Amendment penumbras, *Griswold*, *Carpenter*). It would address modern digital surveillance, data collection by corporations and governments, and personal autonomy. ([EPIC — Fourth Amendment](https://epic.org/issues/privacy-laws/fourth-amendment/), [Cornell LII — Fourth Amendment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/fourth_amendment), [Cornell LII — Privacy](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/privacy))

### Core provisions

1. **Explicit right to privacy** — state clearly that the right of the people to privacy in their persons, homes, communications, and personal data shall not be violated. *Tested models:* Alaska Art. I §22 ("The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed"); Florida Art. I §23 ("right to be let alone and free from governmental intrusion").
2. **Digital and data protections** — extend protection explicitly to electronic data, genetic information, location tracking, and digital communications, mirroring physical-property safeguards. *Tested models:* Missouri Amendment 9 (2014) and Michigan Proposal 2 (2020) added "electronic communications and data" to search-and-seizure clauses with 75–89% voter support; New Hampshire Pt. I Art. 2-b (2018) protects "private or personal information."
3. **Corporate and state limits** — restrict both government surveillance and commercial harvesting or selling of personal data without explicit, informed consent. *Note:* California Art. I §1 is the only state text courts apply against **private actors** (*Hill v. NCAA*) — the key precedent question for whether the amendment binds corporations directly or empowers Congress to regulate them.
4. **Right to deletion** — grant individuals the legal ability to request correction or deletion of their personal data from public and private databases. *Support:* access/correction/deletion is the one substantive core that appears in every federal bill from both parties (see Common Features below).
5. **Strict exceptions** — define narrow, transparent thresholds under which government may lawfully breach privacy, such as targeted judicial warrants based on probable cause. *Tested model:* Montana Art. II §10's "compelling state interest" standard — arguably the most consequential single clause anywhere, because the standard of review dictates how courts actually enforce the right. The distilled bipartisan principle from the FISA/data-broker fights: **government may not obtain — by compulsion or purchase — personal data it could not seize without a warrant.**

### Challenges in drafting and adoption

- **Defining scope** — balancing privacy against legitimate public interests: national security, law-enforcement investigations, public-health tracking. The §702 warrant fight (212–212 House tie, April 2024) shows exactly where the seam runs.
- **Free-speech conflicts** — tension with First Amendment rights to free speech, public reporting, and open government records. (A deletion right against *public* records and journalism is where U.S. courts would diverge sharply from the EU's right-to-be-forgotten.)
- **Consent architecture** — "informed consent" for commercial collection is where every statute bogged down (opt-in vs. opt-out mechanics). Amendment text should state the principle and leave mechanics to legislation.
- **The abortion shadow** — "right to privacy" is the doctrinal root of *Griswold*/*Roe*; post-*Dobbs*, opponents will hear an abortion amendment inside a privacy amendment. Framing matters enormously given our rejected-categories list. The state amendments that passed by landslides (MO, MI, NH) were all **informational/electronic-data framings, government-facing** — not autonomy framings.
- **High political bar** — Article V requires two-thirds of both houses and three-fourths of the states. Mitigant: digital-privacy text has repeatedly cleared 75%+ in *state* ratification votes, and data privacy polls with single-digit partisan gaps ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md) §5: 72% overall, 68% R / 78% D for more regulation of company data use).

### Strategic read

Constitutionalize the **government-facing side** (explicit right, digital/data coverage, warrant/judicial-process requirement, standard of review, enforceability) where bipartisan votes and 50 years of state precedent exist. For the **commercial side**, the amendment's role is to anchor: either a California-style text that reaches private actors, or an enumerated congressional power plus a floor of individual rights (access, correction, deletion) — leaving thresholds, consent mechanics, and enforcement design to statute, where the SECURE/APRA fight will continue regardless.

**Sequencing (the Brazil lesson):** the amendment should *ratify* consensus, not create it. Brazil's data-protection amendment passed the Senate unanimously because it came third — after the statute (LGPD 2018) and the Supreme Court ruling (2020) had de-risked the question. The US analogs are already accumulating: eleven state constitutional privacy clauses, *Carpenter*/*Riley*, and bipartisan House votes on the data-broker loophole. Near-term work that looks statutory or state-level (supporting FANFSA-style bills, state ballot amendments, litigation) is stage-setting for ratification, not a detour from it.

## Major Federal Comprehensive Privacy Bills (2018–2026)

### American Data Privacy and Protection Act (ADPPA) — H.R. 8152 (2022)

- **Who:** Rep. Frank Pallone (D-NJ) + Cathy McMorris Rodgers (R-WA), Bilirakis (R), Schakowsky (D); Senate support from Wicker (R). ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/8152), [CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10776))
- **Progress:** Passed House Energy & Commerce **53–2** (July 2022) — the furthest any comprehensive US privacy bill has ever advanced. Never got a floor vote. ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/house-committee-passes-comprehensive-federal-privacy-legislation))
- **Features:** access/correction/deletion/portability; opt-out of targeted ads and transfers; strong data minimization ("necessary, proportionate, limited to" permitted purposes); opt-in for sensitive data; algorithmic impact assessments for large holders; tiered by size.
- **Enforcement:** FTC + state AGs + private right of action delayed 2 years. **Preemption:** broad, with carve-outs (notably Illinois BIPA).
- **Why it died:** Pelosi sided with California (CPPA) against preempting CCPA/CPRA; Cantwell called the enforcement too weak. ([Civil-rights letter](https://civilrights.org/resource/letter-to-speaker-pelosi-urging-vote-on-the-american-data-privacy-and-protection-act/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Data_Privacy_and_Protection_Act))

### American Privacy Rights Act (APRA) — H.R. 8818 (2024)

- **Who:** Cantwell (D-WA) × McMorris Rodgers (R-WA) — the bicameral, bipartisan deal. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/8818), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Privacy_Rights_Act))
- **Progress:** Subcommittee voice vote May 2024; **full-committee markup canceled minutes before start** (June 27, 2024); expired without reintroduction. ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/american-privacy-rights-act-markup-canceled-next-us-house-steps-uncertain), [The Record](https://therecord.media/apra-data-privacy-bill-markup-cancelled-congress))
- **Features:** minimization-as-default architecture; access/correction/deletion/portability; data-broker registry with "Delete My Data" mechanism; opt-in for sensitive data. June revision **stripped the civil-rights/algorithms section**. ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/house-committee-postpones-markup-amid-new-privacy-bill-updates))
- **Enforcement:** FTC + state AGs + a broader private right of action (the core GOP objection). **Preemption:** broad with carve-outs; [CPPA opposed](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240626.html).
- **Why it died:** two-front collapse — stripping civil-rights provisions lost the left; the private right of action lost GOP leadership (Scalise); McMorris Rodgers retired.

### SECURE Data Act — H.R. 8413 (2026) *(the bill in the Wiley alert)*

- **Who:** Rep. John Joyce (R-PA), from the House E&C Privacy Working Group (Chair Guthrie, R-KY); introduced April 22, 2026 alongside a companion **GUARD Financial Data Act** (House Financial Services, Chair Hill, R-AR). Republican-only product. ([Wiley alert](https://www.wiley.law/alert-SECURE-Act-US-House-Introduces-New-National-Privacy-Framework), [E&C release](https://energycommerce.house.gov/posts/committees-on-energy-and-commerce-and-financial-services-introduce-pair-of-privacy-bills-to-establish-comprehensive-data-protections-for-all-americans), [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill), [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8413/text))
- **Scope:** 200k+ consumers/yr + $25M revenue, or 100k+ consumers + 25% revenue from data sales; exempts GLBA/HIPAA entities, some nonprofits, universities; covers common carriers.
- **Features:** access/correction/deletion; opt-out of sales, targeted ads, certain profiling; opt-in for sensitive data; minimization with secondary-use limits; no impact-assessment mandate; Commerce studies instead of a universal opt-out mechanism.
- **Enforcement:** FTC primary, limited state AG role, **no private right of action**. **Preemption:** the defining feature — displaces any state law that "relates to" its subject matter (a ceiling, not a floor), likely mooting all 20+ state comprehensive laws. ([TechPolicy.Press critique](https://www.techpolicy.press/congresss-new-privacy-bill-is-built-on-empty-promises/), [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/one-federal-privacy-bill-to-rule-them-all/))
- **Status (Aug 2026):** headed for subcommittee markup; hearing split on party lines. ([StateScoop](https://statescoop.com/house-subcommittee-secure-data-act-preempts-state-privacy-laws-2026/), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/22/data-privacy-bill-congress-states.html), [Consumer Finance Monitor](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2026/05/12/u-s-house-committee-releases-secure-data-act-to-establish-new-federal-privacy-framework/))

### Consumer Online Privacy Rights Act (COPRA) — S. 2968 (2019) / S. 3195 (2021)

- **Who:** Cantwell (D-WA) + Schatz, Klobuchar, Markey — the Senate Democrats' flagship. Never marked up. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/senate-bill/2968/text), [Cantwell one-pager](https://www.cantwell.senate.gov/download/copra-one-pager&download=1))
- **Features:** duty of loyalty; minimization; access/correction/deletion/portability; civil-rights protections + algorithmic impact assessments; whistleblower protections; new FTC privacy bureau.
- **Enforcement:** strong private right of action with statutory damages. **Preemption:** floor, not ceiling — stronger state laws preserved.

### SAFE DATA Act — S. 4626 (2020)

- **Who:** Wicker (R-MS) + Thune, Fischer, Blackburn — the Republican counter to COPRA. Never marked up. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/senate-bill/4626), [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/consolidating-u-s-privacy-legislation-the-safe-data-act))
- **Features:** transparency/access/correction/deletion/portability; opt-in for sensitive data; weaker minimization; filter-bubble/algorithmic transparency provisions.
- **Enforcement:** FTC + state AGs, **no private right of action**. **Preemption:** full. ([Access Now critique](https://www.accessnow.org/the-safe-data-act-is-safe-in-name-only/))

### Online Privacy Act — H.R. 4978 (2019) / H.R. 6027 (2021)

- **Who:** Eshoo (D-CA) + Lofgren (D-CA). The most aggressive structural proposal; never marked up. ([Eshoo release](https://eshoo.house.gov/media/press-releases/eshoo-lofgren-introduce-online-privacy-act), [Lofgren](https://lofgren.house.gov/media/press-releases/lofgren-eshoo-reintroduce-sweeping-privacy-legislation))
- **Features:** GDPR-style; novel **right to impermanence** (user-set retention limits); human review of automated decisions; dark-pattern consent ban; creates an independent **Digital Privacy Agency**; private enforcement via nonprofit class actions.

### Data Care Act — S. 3744 (2018), reintroduced through S. 744 (2023)

- **Who:** Schatz (D-HI) + 15 Democrats. The "information fiduciary" model — duties of **care, loyalty, and confidentiality** instead of enumerated rights. No preemption, no private right of action; FTC + state AGs. Never marked up. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/senate-bill/3744), [Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/data-privacy/democratic-senators-introduce-privacy-bill-seeking-to-impose-fiduciary-duties-on-online-providers/))

### Other notable proposals

- **Data Protection Act** — Gillibrand (D-NY), S. 3300/S. 2134: independent Data Protection Agency with merger-review powers. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/senate-bill/2134/text))
- **Mind Your Own Business Act** — Wyden (D-OR), 2019: criminal liability for executives who lie in privacy certifications; universal Do Not Track. ([Harvard JOLT](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/sen-wyden-introduces-federal-data-privacy-bill))
- **Information Transparency & Personal Data Control Act** — DelBene (D-WA), H.R. 1816: sensitive-data opt-in + FTC rulemaking; preemption without PRA. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/1816))
- **Consumer Data Privacy and Security Act** — Moran (R-KS), S. 1494; plus the 2019 cluster (Rubio, Cortez Masto, Klobuchar–Kennedy). ([Cornell DLI survey](https://dli.tech.cornell.edu/post/us-data-privacy-law-federal-and-state-legislation-impact-and-risk-mitigation))

## Government-Facing Privacy Bills (the Fourth Amendment side)

An amendment binds government first — so these are the most directly relevant, and the most bipartisan.

### Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act — H.R. 4639

- Bars law enforcement/intelligence from **buying** Americans' data from brokers when they'd otherwise need a warrant — closing the "data broker loophole" around *Carpenter*. Lead: Davidson (R-OH), with Nadler (D), Biggs (R), Lofgren (D), Jayapal (D), Massie (R).
- **Passed the House 219–199 (April 17, 2024)** — 123 R + 96 D voting yes; died in the Senate. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/4639), [vote](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/votes/118-2024/h136), [EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/04/fourth-amendment-not-sale-act-passed-house-now-it-should-pass-senate), [EPIC](https://epic.org/epic-statement-on-house-passage-of-fourth-amendment-is-not-for-sale-act/))

### Government Surveillance Reform Act — S. 3234 / H.R. 6262, reintroduced March 2026

- Wyden (D-OR) + Lee (R-UT); Davidson (R-OH) + Lofgren (D-CA). Reauthorizes FISA §702 with a **warrant for U.S.-person queries**, bans data-broker purchases, protects location/browsing data, adds judicial redress. ([Wyden release](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/press-releases/wyden-lee-davidson-and-lofgren-introduce-bill-to-reform-fisa-section-702-protect-americans-constitutional-rights-and-plug-data-broker-surveillance-loophole))

### FISA Section 702 fights

- April 2024: warrant-requirement amendment **failed on a 212–212 tie**; §702 reauthorized 2 years (RISAA). ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/fisa-section-702-reauthorized-for-two-years), [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/section-702-foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act))
- 2026: House leadership blocked a warrant-amendment vote under a closed rule; after a 45-day extension, **§702 lapsed at its June 12, 2026 sunset — the first lapse since 2008**. The warrant question remains unresolved. ([EPIC](https://epic.org/fisa-section-702-almost-certain-to-expire-after-house-votes-against-extension-epic-continues-to-urge-reforms/), [Brennan 2026 page](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/section-702-foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act-fisa-2026-resource-page), [Cato](https://www.cato.org/blog/house-votes-continue-subverting-fourth-amendment))

### DELETE Act — S. 1287 (119th)

- Ossoff (D-GA) + Cassidy (R-LA); Trahan (D-MA) + Edwards (R-NC). FTC one-stop deletion dashboard propagating to all registered data brokers + do-not-track list. Genuinely bipartisan; in committee. ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1287/text), [The Record](https://therecord.media/delete-act-reintroduced-congress-data-brokers))

## State Constitutional Privacy Provisions (models for amendment text)

**Eleven states** have express constitutional privacy language; five make it a freestanding right. ([Li, *State Constitutional Rights to Privacy*, Georgia L. Rev.](https://georgialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Tiffany-C.-Li_State-Constitutional-Rights-to-Privacy.pdf), [National Academies survey](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24730/chapter/12))

| State | Provision | Why it matters as a model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| California | Art. I §1 (1972) | "Privacy" among inalienable rights; the only state text courts apply to **private actors** (*Hill v. NCAA*) |
| Montana | Art. II §10 (1972) | "…shall not be infringed without the showing of a **compelling state interest**" — strongest review standard in the country |
| Alaska | Art. I §22 (1972) | Cleanest one-sentence formulation |
| Florida | Art. I §23 (1980) | "Right to **be let alone** and free from governmental intrusion" — cleanest government-facing text |
| Hawaii | Art. I §6–7 (1978) | Freestanding right + compelling-interest test; communications covered |
| New Hampshire | Pt. I Art. 2-b (2018) | Most modern: "private or personal **information**" — passed with ~81% of voters |
| Arizona / Washington | Art. II §8 / Art. I §7 | "Private affairs… without authority of law" — read more broadly than the Fourth Amendment |
| Illinois / Louisiana / South Carolina | Art. I §6 / §5 / §10 | Search-seizure clauses extended to "invasions of privacy" (IL adds communications interception) |
| Missouri (2014) / Michigan (2020) | Amendment 9 / Proposal 2 | Added "**electronic communications and data**" to search-and-seizure clauses — ratified with ~75% / ~89% |

## Prior Federal Privacy Amendment Proposals

Essentially none. Searches of Congress.gov and [CRS RL30671](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL30671.html) surface **no joint resolution proposing an explicit constitutional privacy right that ever received committee action**. After Watergate and the Church Committee, Congress deliberately chose statutes (Privacy Act of 1974, FERPA, RFPA, ECPA), reasoning that *Griswold* + the Fourth Amendment sufficed. Post-*Dobbs* amendment calls have come from academics and commentators, not members ([Above the Law 2022](https://abovethelaw.com/2022/05/a-constitutional-amendment-guaranteeing-the-right-to-privacy/), [Li, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5146734)). **A foundation-led privacy amendment enters a genuine vacuum: no failed-amendment baggage, but no congressional muscle memory.**

## Common Features Across All Federal Bills (the consensus core)

Present in essentially every bill, both parties, 2018–2026:

- Individual rights: **access/transparency, correction, deletion**, and (usually) portability
- **Opt-out** of data sales/transfers and targeted advertising
- **Opt-in consent for sensitive data** (biometrics, precise location, health, children's data)
- **Data minimization / purpose limitation** — by 2022 the bipartisan *default architecture*, replacing notice-and-consent
- **Duty of reasonable data security**
- **FTC + state AG enforcement**
- Non-retaliation for exercising rights; obligations flow down to processors; tiering by company size

## Key Differences (what killed every bill)

1. **Preemption** — GOP bills demand a federal ceiling (SECURE's "relates to" language); Democratic bills set a floor preserving stronger state laws; the compromises (ADPPA/APRA) were killed partly *by California* for preempting CCPA anyway.
2. **Private right of action** — GOP bills: none; Democratic bills: strong PRA with damages. ADPPA's delayed PRA was too weak for Cantwell and too strong for Scalise; APRA's PRA is why GOP leadership canceled the markup.
3. **Civil-rights / algorithmic provisions** — stripped from APRA in June 2024, which collapsed the left coalition without gaining GOP votes.
4. Secondary: FTC vs. new Data Protection Agency; rights-based vs. fiduciary vs. consent-centric models.

## International Models

### The EU twin-rights architecture — Charter Articles 7 & 8

The EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (binding with treaty rank since 2009) is the only constitutional-level text with **two separate, adjacent rights** — privacy and data protection:

- **Art. 7:** "Everyone has the right to respect for his or her private and family life, home and **communications**." (The drafters swapped the ECHR's "correspondence" for "communications" specifically to be technology-neutral — one word-swap has quietly covered email, metadata, and messaging for 25 years.)
- **Art. 8:** "(1) Everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning him or her. (2) Such data must be processed fairly for specified purposes and on the basis of the consent of the person concerned or some other legitimate basis laid down by law. Everyone has the right of access to data which has been collected concerning him or her, and the right to have it rectified. (3) Compliance with these rules shall be subject to control by an independent authority."

Why two rights: privacy is a **shield** (protects a sphere against intrusion); data protection is a **rulebook** (conditions all processing of information about a person, private or not, and grants rights-in-the-data). Art. 8(3) constitutionalizes the *institution* — an independent supervisory authority — which the CJEU polices strictly (*Commission v Germany* 2010, *Commission v Hungary* 2014). The CJEU has annulled an entire EU directive (*Digital Rights Ireland*, 2014) and two successive EU–US transfer frameworks (*Schrems I* 2015, *Schrems II* 2020 — holding FISA §702 lacked proportionality and redress) under these articles. ([Charter text, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:12012P/TXT); [official Explanations](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32007X1214(01)); [FRA on Art. 8](https://fra.europa.eu/en/eu-charter/article/8-protection-personal-data); [González Fuster](https://www.elgaronline.com/downloadpdf/edcollchap/edcoll/9781800371675/9781800371675.00010.pdf))

### ECHR Article 8 — the limitation-clause model

The 1950 Convention states the right *and* the exclusive conditions for limiting it: "no interference by a public authority... except such as is **in accordance with the law** and is **necessary in a democratic society**" for enumerated aims. Strasbourg turned this into a fixed four-step test (interference → legality → legitimate aim → necessity/proportionality), where "legality" is *qualitative*: the law must be accessible and foreseeable (*Malone v UK*, 1984 — secret, standardless surveillance fails at this step). Key cases: *Klass v Germany* (1978 — secret surveillance carries "the danger of undermining or even destroying democracy on the ground of defending it"); *Weber and Saravia* (2006 — six minimum safeguards any interception law must state); *S. and Marper* (2008 — "blanket and indiscriminate" DNA retention fails); *Roman Zakharov v Russia* (2015); *Big Brother Watch v UK* (GC 2021 — bulk interception permitted only with "end-to-end safeguards" and prior independent authorization; criticized for legitimizing mass surveillance by regulating rather than forbidding it); *Podchasov v Russia* (2024 — compelled encryption backdoors are disproportionate per se). ([ECHR text](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/convention_ENG); [Big Brother Watch judgment](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-210077); [Strasbourg Observers critique](https://strasbourgobservers.com/2021/06/28/much-ado-about-mass-surveillance-the-ecthr-grand-chamber-opens-the-gates-of-an-electronic-big-brother-in-europe-in-big-brother-watch-v-uk/); [ECtHR Art. 8 guide](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_8_eng))

### Convention 108 / 108+ — the only binding international data-protection treaty

Council of Europe Convention 108 (1981) applies to public and private sectors; its Art. 5 principles — fair/lawful processing, purpose specification, "adequate, relevant and not excessive," accuracy, storage limits — are recognizably GDPR Art. 5's direct ancestors, **stable for 45 years**. 55 parties including nine non-European states (Uruguay, Mexico, Argentina, Senegal, Tunisia, more); the US has observer status but never acceded. The 108+ modernization (2018) is still not in force — stuck at 34 of 38 required ratifications after eight years, a lesson about amendment thresholds. ([Convention text](https://rm.coe.int/1680078b37); [108+ text](https://rm.coe.int/convention-108-convention-for-the-protection-of-individuals-with-regar/16808b36f1); [CoE ratification status](https://www.coe.int/en/web/data-protection/-/republic-of-moldova-becomes-the-34th-state-to-ratify-the-convention-108-))

### Germany — informational self-determination from open-ended text

Germany's Basic Law contains no privacy clause; the Constitutional Court built one from dignity (Art. 1) + free development of personality (Art. 2). The **1983 Census Judgment** announced the "right to informational self-determination": "the authority of the individual, in principle, to determine for himself the disclosure and use of his personal data." Two load-bearing holdings: the **chilling-effect/democracy rationale** (a person who cannot tell what is known about them "can be substantially inhibited in his freedom to plan and decide... self-determination is an elementary functional condition of a free democratic community") and **"there is no insignificant data"** under modern processing — aggregation defeats any "knowingly exposed" logic. The **2008 Online-Search Judgment** added a right to "the confidentiality and integrity of information technology systems" — protecting any system whose contents give "insight into significant parts of the life of a person" — plus an absolutely untouchable "core area of private life" no balancing can reach. This doctrinal move (protect the *system*, not the place) anticipated *Riley* (2014) and *Carpenter* (2018) by a decade. ([Census Judgment, official EN translation](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Entscheidungen/EN/1983/12/rs19831215_1bvr020983en.html); [2008 judgment](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Entscheidungen/EN/2008/02/rs20080227_1bvr037007en.html))

### GDPR — durable principles, painful machinery

GDPR Art. 5's principles layer (lawfulness/fairness/transparency, purpose limitation, minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, security, accountability) has been copied by ~140 national laws and every US state statute — the principles are the export success. The pain lives in the procedural superstructure: the **Draghi competitiveness report (2024)** flagged regressive compliance costs hitting small firms hardest; consent pop-ups became "compliance theatre"; the one-stop-shop routed Big Tech enforcement through an overwhelmed Ireland; and the text's rigidity around AI has the EU itself debating reform. Lesson: constitutionalize the principles, never the machinery. ([GDPR text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj); [Draghi coverage](https://www.lexology.com/pro/content/draghi-report-inconsistent-gdpr-enforcement-hinders-innovation); [FPF on reform](https://fpf.org/blog/the-draghi-dilemma-the-right-and-the-wrong-way-to-undertake-gdpr-reform/))

### The right to be forgotten vs. the First Amendment

*Google Spain* (CJEU 2014) lets individuals compel de-listing of "inadequate, irrelevant or no longer relevant" search results even where the underlying publication is lawful — privacy and expression are formally co-equal in Europe, balanced case-by-case. Every element fails settled US doctrine: truthful, lawfully obtained information cannot be suppressed (*Florida Star*, *Cox Broadcasting*, *Smith v. Daily Mail*); protection doesn't fade with time; search results are protected editorial judgment; and ad hoc balancing is precisely what *United States v. Stevens* (2010) forbids. This confirms the right-to-be-forgotten trap already flagged in abstraction candidate 3: a US amendment must govern collection, aggregation, and use — never publication of truthful information. ([Google Spain, C-131/12](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62012CJ0131); [MTSU First Amendment Encyclopedia](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/right-to-be-forgotten/))

### India — *Puttaswamy* and the proportionality test that spares government programs

A nine-judge bench ruled **unanimously** (2017) that privacy is a fundamental right inherent in Art. 21 ("life or personal liberty") — overruling 60 years of contrary precedent. The test it set: **legality → legitimate aim → proportionality → procedural safeguards**. Applied to Aadhaar, the national biometric ID (2018): the Court **upheld** the system for welfare delivery and tax administration but **struck** private-sector access and mandatory bank/SIM linking — proof that a constitutional privacy right with a proportionality test preserves legitimate state functions while amputating overreach. Caveat: the implementing statute (DPDP Act 2023) carries broad government exemptions — a judicially announced right doesn't automatically constrain the legislature. ([SCObserver case page](https://www.scobserver.in/cases/puttaswamy-v-union-of-india-fundamental-right-to-privacy-case-background/); [Oxford HRH analysis](https://ohrh.law.ox.ac.uk/defining-the-right-to-privacy-in-india-in-light-of-justice-ks-puttaswamy-anr-v-union-of-india-2017/); [Carnegie on DPDP](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2023/10/understanding-indias-new-data-protection-law))

### Brazil — the transferable ratification model

Constitutional Amendment 115 (Feb 2022) added to Art. 5: *"the right to the protection of personal data is ensured, under the terms of the law, including in digital media"* — one sentence, digital-explicit, machinery delegated to statute. It also gave the federal government **exclusive competence** over data-protection law (their answer to our preemption fight). The sequence: **LGPD statute (2018) → Supreme Court recognition** (ADI 6387, 2020 — struck a pandemic-era order handing 140M+ telecom subscribers' data to the statistics agency) → **constitutional lock-in (2022), passed the Senate unanimously (64–0, then 76–0)**. Consensus was possible because the court had de-risked the question and the contentious details already lived in the statute. ([Kasznar Leonardos](https://www.kasznarleonardos.com/en/data-protection-becomes-a-fundamental-right-in-brazil/); [FPF on ADI 6387](https://fpf.org/blog/a-landmark-ruling-in-brazil-paving-the-way-for-considering-data-protection-as-an-autonomous-fundamental-right/); [Datasphere on constitutionalization](https://www.thedatasphere.org/news/the-constitutionalization-of-data-protection-rights-the-case-of-brazil/))

### Canada and Japan — the cost of inference

- **Canada:** Charter s. 8 ("unreasonable search or seizure") took 30 years of case-by-case litigation to reach informational privacy — the "biographical core" (*R v. Plant*, 1993) and anonymity/ISP subscriber data (*R v. Spencer*, 2014). Meanwhile the statutory modernization (Bill C-27) died on prorogation in Jan 2025 after three years in committee. ([Spencer, CanLII](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2014/2014scc43/2014scc43.html); [C-27 status](https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bill/44-1/c-27))
- **Japan:** privacy inferred from Art. 13's "pursuit of happiness" clause (1969 portrait-right case; 2008 *Juki Net* — national resident registry upheld because data was non-sensitive, purpose-confined, and safeguarded). Thin constitutional text plus a strong independent regulator still earned the world's first reciprocal EU adequacy arrangement (2019). ([EU–Japan adequacy](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2019/419/oj/eng); [Juki Net](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2008/03/07/national/top-court-juki-net-not-against-the-constitution/))

### Global survey and international instruments

- Roughly **186 of ~193 national constitutions** contain a privacy provision; the US is the conspicuous outlier ([Constitute Project](https://www.constituteproject.org/topics/privacy?lang=en); [Li, Georgia L. Rev.](https://georgialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Tiffany-C.-Li_State-Constitutional-Rights-to-Privacy.pdf); [Privacy International](https://www.privacyinternational.org/blog/1591/privacy-constitutions-data)). Distinctive texts: **South Africa s. 14** ("the right to privacy, which **includes**..." — open-ended enumeration, the anti-frozen-list pattern); **Kenya Art. 31** (adds "information relating to their family or private affairs unnecessarily required or revealed"); **Chile** (2018 amendment constitutionalizing data protection *before* the modern statute); **Mexico Art. 16** (constitutionalized access/rectification/cancellation/opposition — the "ARCO" rights); **Estonia §42** (anti-dossier clause: the state "may not collect or store information on the beliefs of any Estonian citizen against his or her free will").
- International instruments converge on a narrow core: **UDHR Art. 12** / **ICCPR Art. 17** (binding on the US since 1992 — "no arbitrary or unlawful interference," with General Comment 16 already anticipating access/correction rights in 1988), **OECD Privacy Guidelines** (1980 — the FIPPs, which are an *American* invention, descended from the 1973 HEW report), and the **APEC Privacy Framework**. The recurring six: lawfulness, proportionality/reasonableness, purpose limitation, security, individual access/correction, and remedy. ([ICCPR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights); [GC 16](https://www.refworld.org/legal/general/hrc/1988/en/27539); [OECD Guidelines](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-privacy-and-transborder-flows-of-personal-data_9789264196391-en.html); [APEC Framework](https://www.apec.org/publications/2005/12/apec-privacy-framework))

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Split the shield from the rulebook.** Privacy (intrusion into private life/home/communications) and data protection (rules for processing information about a person) are different rights with different logics — Charter Arts. 7/8 prove a constitution can hold both. Covering only "searches" or only "data" replicates known gaps. Maps onto our candidates 1–2 (shield) and 3 (rulebook).
2. **Write a limitation clause instead of pretending the right is absolute.** ECHR Art. 8(2)'s structure — clear/accessible/foreseeable law, enumerated purposes, necessity/proportionality — has produced 50 years of workable doctrine and is the most transplant-ready piece of text surveyed. Validates our candidate 4 (tiered review) and answers the disruption objection head-on.
3. **Name the right expressly; don't rely on inference.** India, Japan, and Canada each spent 30–50 years litigating privacy out of "liberty," "happiness," or "search and seizure" — with reversals along the way. And the US has lived the inference path's downside: *Dobbs* showed inferred privacy rights can be un-inferred.
4. **Constitutionalize principles, not procedures.** What survived 45 years unchanged (Convention 108 → Charter → GDPR Art. 5): purpose limitation, minimization, accuracy, security, access/correction. What causes Europe's current agony: consent pop-ups, org charts, and process rules frozen in text.
5. **Use the "includes" pattern, not closed lists.** South Africa's "which includes..." gives courts enumerated anchors plus open texture — the opposite of the Fourth Amendment's closed "persons, houses, papers, and effects" list that US courts have strained to extend to metadata. Validates candidate 6's illustrative-list approach.
6. **Ratify consensus; don't create it.** Brazil's unanimous amendment came *after* the statute and the court ruling had de-risked the question. Our analog: eleven state constitutions + *Carpenter*/*Riley* + bipartisan House votes are the pre-existing consensus a federal amendment would lock in.
7. **Protect aggregates and systems, not just secrets and places.** Germany's "there is no insignificant data" and IT-systems right are the best doctrinal language yet written for the mosaic/aggregation problem — codify the *Carpenter* principle so it stops depending on 5–4 majorities.
8. **Mark at least one bright line that balancing cannot erode.** *Big Brother Watch* shows safeguard-checklist doctrine ends up legitimizing bulk surveillance; the CJEU's "essence of the right" tool (generalized access to communications content is per se void) worked better. Consider one flat prohibition — e.g., no generalized, suspicionless collection of the population's communications or movements.
9. **Guarantee remedy and individual participation; leave the regulator's design to Congress.** Art. 8(3)'s constitutionalized independent authority is Europe's most original move, but the Ireland bottleneck shows institutional specifics can nullify strong text. Matches our candidate 5 (redress without frozen machinery).
10. **Proportionality survives contact with big government.** India upheld Aadhaar's welfare core; Japan upheld Juki Net; nowhere did a constitutional privacy right cripple census-taking, benefits delivery, or warrant-based law enforcement — the strongest available answer to the administrative-disruption objection (see candidate 1's trap note).
11. **The one European element that cannot cross the Atlantic:** erasure/de-listing of published truthful information. Any US text must stay clear of publication or expressly preserve press freedom, or *Google Spain* logic will doom it in court and in the ratification campaign.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. All seven below clear it — the acquisition rule tracks a bipartisan 219–199 House vote and 75–89% state ratifications, the freestanding right and informational rights track 68R/78D data-privacy polling and landslide state precedents, and candidates 4, 5, and 7 are structural machinery for the polled rights rather than separate asks. The one clause to keep under watch: the bright-line mass-surveillance ban rests on votes and warrant polling rather than direct survey data — worth including in the commissioned poll.*

1. **No investigative acquisition of personal information without judicial process** — the distilled principle behind FANFSA (bipartisan 219–199), GSRA, and the 212–212 warrant amendment. Proposed formulation: *"Government may not acquire personal information for investigative or intelligence purposes — whether by compulsion, purchase, or exchange — that it could not lawfully obtain through search or seizure, except with judicial authorization, consent, or in exigent circumstances. Generalized, suspicionless collection or retention of the communications or movements of the people is prohibited."*
   - **The bright line (Int'l Lesson 8):** the second sentence is deliberately immune to balancing. *Big Brother Watch* (ECtHR 2021) shows what happens when doctrine is all safeguards and proportionality — courts end up regulating mass surveillance rather than forbidding any of it. The CJEU's "essence of the right" tool (*Schrems I*: generalized access to communications content voids the right, full stop) worked better. One flat prohibition; proportionality analysis governs everything else.
   - ⚠️ **The administrative-collection trap:** an unscoped "no acquisition without judicial process" would sweep in routine government function — IRS third-party reporting (W-2s/1099s), the Census, Social Security/Medicaid eligibility matching, vital records and licensing, mandatory public-health disease reporting, and anti-money-laundering bank reports — none of which involve a judge today, and none of which the public expects to. The principle must distinguish **collection incident to a service or legal obligation where the individual is the knowing source** (untouched) from **investigative or surveillance acquisition, especially via third parties** (covered). The models that won ratification landslides already encode this: Arizona/Washington's "without authority of law" preserves statutory collection, and Missouri/Michigan simply extended existing *search-and-seizure* doctrine to electronic data — inheriting its settled boundaries rather than inventing new ones.
2. **A freestanding, express right of privacy against government intrusion** — Alaska/Florida text as models. Proposed formulation: *"Every person has the right to be secure against unreasonable governmental intrusion into their private life, including their home, communications, movements, and personal information. This right is not diminished because information has been disclosed to another or is insignificant in isolation."*
   - **The open enumeration (Int'l Lesson 5):** "including" follows South Africa s. 14's pattern — enumerated anchors plus open texture — deliberately avoiding the Fourth Amendment's *closed* "persons, houses, papers, and effects" list that forced US courts to strain over metadata for decades.
   - **The aggregation clause (Int'l Lesson 7):** the second sentence constitutionalizes *Carpenter* and ends the third-party doctrine's erosion of digital privacy — Germany's census-judgment insight ("under modern data processing there is no insignificant data") in one sentence. Arguably the highest-value clause in the package: without it, every other protection leaks through data held by phone carriers, banks, and platforms.
   - ⚠️ **The decisional-autonomy trap:** an unqualified "right to privacy" or "right to be let alone" gets read as substantive autonomy — Florida's Art. I §23 was the doctrinal basis for state abortion protections for 35 years (*In re T.W.*, 1989) before the Florida Supreme Court receded in 2024. An unscoped text will be litigated, and campaigned against, as an abortion/assisted-suicide amendment. Anchoring the right to *intrusion into private life and information* (not "decisions") keeps it in Fourth-Amendment-adjacent territory, and "unreasonable" imports two centuries of settled doctrine — taxation, subpoenas, regulatory inspections, and court process all remain lawful.
3. **Informational self-determination** — protection of "personal information" as such (New Hampshire model). Proposed formulation: *"Every person has the right to know what personal information the government holds about them and to obtain correction of material inaccuracies. Personal information acquired by government for one purpose may not be used for an unrelated purpose, except as provided by law consistent with this article. Congress and the States may by law secure rights of access, correction, and deletion of personal information held by others."*
   - **Purpose limitation (Int'l Lesson 4):** the second sentence adds the single most durable principle in the international record — textually stable from Convention 108 (1981) through GDPR Art. 5 (2016) — and the true anti-aggregation workhorse for government-held data: census answers can't be repurposed for immigration enforcement; toll records can't quietly become a movement database. Previously present in our framework section but missing from every candidate formulation.
   - ⚠️ **The right-to-be-forgotten trap:** a constitutional *deletion* right applied to public records, court records, criminal history, credit files, or journalism becomes an EU-style right-to-be-forgotten fight that loses to the First Amendment — and deleting government records undermines accountability and archives. The scoped version constitutionalizes access + correction against government (essentially the Privacy Act of 1974, elevated — non-disruptive by construction) and converts the commercial suite (access/correction/deletion — the one substantive core every statute from both parties agrees on) into an express *legislative power*, which simultaneously settles the constitutional-authority question and makes the federal floor explicit.
4. **A defined standard of review** — Montana's "compelling state interest" clause; the single most consequential drafting choice. Proposed formulation: *"This right shall not be infringed except under authority of public, accessible, and foreseeable law; intrusions into a person's private communications, sensitive personal information, or continuous movements require a compelling public interest served by narrowly tailored means."*
   - **The quality-of-law requirement (Int'l Lesson 2):** "public, accessible, and foreseeable" imports the ECHR's most transplantable doctrine — legality is *qualitative*, not formal (*Malone v UK* 1984, *Roman Zakharov v Russia* 2015). Secret, standardless surveillance authorities fail this step categorically, before any balancing begins.
   - ⚠️ **The single-tier trap:** blanket strict scrutiny over *all* personal information turns every tax audit, subpoena, and benefits cross-match into constitutional litigation. Tiering — "authority of law" (reasonableness) for ordinary collection, compelling-interest review for the sensitive core — mirrors distinctions courts already draw (content vs. metadata, sensitive vs. ordinary data), so existing doctrine carries the load instead of two decades of destabilizing test cases. Interlocks with candidate 6, which defines the sensitive core. The big-government evidence (Int'l Lesson 10): India upheld Aadhaar's welfare core and Japan upheld its national resident registry *under* exactly this kind of test — proof the tier structure preserves legitimate state functions while amputating overreach.
5. **Enforceability/redress** — dissolves the private-right-of-action stalemate rather than picking a side in it. Proposed formulation: *"Any person aggrieved by a violation of this article may obtain redress in the courts, including equitable relief. Congress and the States may provide further remedies."*
   - ⚠️ **The litigation-bonanza trap:** building automatic damages liability or evidence exclusion into the text re-codes the amendment as anti-police and re-fights the APRA private-right-of-action war at constitutional scale. The scoped version guarantees the thing statutes kept failing on — that the right is judicially cognizable at all (the courthouse door that *TransUnion v. Ramirez* standing doctrine currently narrows) — while leaving damages regimes, penalty schedules, and suppression rules to legislation, where they can be calibrated and revised.
6. **Heightened protection for intimate/sensitive categories** — stated generally, defined by legislation. Proposed formulation: *"The protection of this article applies with greatest force to information concerning a person's health, body, biometric and genetic identity, private communications, precise movements, and children, as further defined by law."*
   - ⚠️ **The frozen-list trap:** hard-coding an exhaustive category list into constitutional text ages badly (yesterday's texts never anticipated location pings; today's won't anticipate neural data), and category fights — is reproductive-health data in or out? — can re-code the whole amendment. An illustrative "including"-style list plus express legislative power to extend keeps the principle durable and moves the contested line-drawing into ordinary politics. Supplies the "sensitive core" that candidate 4's compelling-interest tier operates on.

7. **Enforcement power and savings clause** — the standard closing sections of every modern amendment, and what makes the principles/machinery split actually work. Proposed formulation: *"Nothing in this article shall abridge the freedom of speech or of the press. Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."*
   - **The savings clause (Int'l Lesson 11):** disarms the strongest ratification attack — *Google Spain*-style right-to-be-forgotten logic — before it's made. The amendment governs collection, aggregation, and use; it can never reach publication of truthful information.
   - **The enforcement clause (Int'l Lessons 4, 6):** the 14th/15th Amendment §5 pattern, and Brazil's "under the terms of the law" delegation. It keeps procedures out of constitutional text and doubles as the express constitutional authority for the commercial-side legislation candidate 3 delegates — mooting the Commerce Clause question.

**The package skeleton.** Read together, the seven candidates form a complete amendment architecture: coverage and aggregation principle (2), acquisition rule with one bright line (1), individual rights and purpose limitation (3), sensitive core (6), tiered review with quality-of-law (4), remedies (5), and savings + enforcement (7) — with all contested machinery deliberately pushed down to legislation.

**Inherently statutory (leave out of the amendment):** covered-entity thresholds and revenue tests; opt-in/opt-out mechanics and universal opt-out signals; data-broker registries and deletion dashboards; sectoral carve-outs (GLBA/HIPAA); enforcement architecture (FTC bureau vs. DPA, penalty schedules, damages caps); preemption mechanics (mooted — a constitutional right is inherently a floor); definition lists that must evolve with technology.

### Extends: Key Differences (what killed every bill) — the state statutory baseline and the private-right-of-action line

- **The state baseline is a no-PRA, AG-enforced, opt-out model.** By mid-2026, **23–24 states** have comprehensive consumer-privacy statutes (24 counting Florida's narrower law): California, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Iowa, Indiana, Tennessee, Texas, Florida, Maryland, Minnesota, Montana, Oregon, Delaware, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Kentucky, Nebraska, Rhode Island, plus Alabama, Louisiana, Oklahoma, and Vermont in 2026 — red and blue states alike. In nearly all of them enforcement is vested exclusively in the attorney general and there is **no private right of action** (California's breach-only PRA is the exception). ([Byte Back, June 2026](https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2026/06/u-s-state-privacy-law-landscape-expands-to-24-states-what-the-latest-legislative-wave-means-for-businesses/); [IAPP on Vermont](https://iapp.org/news/a/vermont-becomes-23rd-state-to-enact-consumer-privacy-law)) The Virginia-style template, not California's, is the actual national consensus — which is why the SECURE Data Act's ceiling preemption is contested even by GOP-led states.
- **Vermont is the cleanest natural experiment on the PRA.** Gov. Phil Scott (R) vetoed H.121 on June 13, 2024, citing its private right of action ("frivolous lawsuits") and novel provisions; the veto was sustained. The 2026 successor, S.71, passed once the PRA was removed and enforcement left with the AG — signed June 16, 2026 (after an initial veto reversed eight days later), effective Jan. 1, 2028. ([Vermont Public](https://www.vermontpublic.org/local-news/2024-06-13/gov-phil-scott-vetoes-data-privacy-bill); [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/vermont-becomes-23rd-state-to-enact-consumer-privacy-law); [Covington Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/state-privacy/vermont-data-privacy-bill-signed-into-law/))
- **The business community's stated red line.** The U.S. Chamber of Commerce told the Senate Commerce and House E&C committees (May 31, 2022) it would "strongly oppose legislation that fails to provide meaningful preemption or any proposal that creates a blanket private right of action," and led a 20-organization coalition letter (June 26, 2024) opposing APRA "as drafted" for creating "more than ten new private causes of action" while affirming support for a national privacy law. ([Chamber, 2022](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/data-privacy/u-s-chamber-warns-it-will-oppose-any-privacy-legislation-that-creates-a-blanket-private-right-of-action); [Chamber coalition letter, 2024](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/u-s-chamber-coalition-letter-opposing-h-r-8818-the-american-privacy-rights-act)) **Gate-check relevance (candidate 5):** the strongest counterargument to the redress clause is that "redress in the courts, including equitable relief" *is* a constitutional private right of action against private parties if candidate 3's commercial floor is read to bind them directly. The memo's answer — the clause guarantees justiciability and equitable relief, leaves damages to statute, and the commercial suite is a *legislative power* rather than a self-executing right — should be tested explicitly in the commissioned poll and in outreach to the Chamber-aligned side; the state record above (no PRA in ~23 of 24 laws) shows where that constituency's floor actually is.

### Extends: Amendment Framework — Challenges (the abortion shadow)

- **"Private life" is not a safe harbor from the autonomy reading.** Under ECHR Art. 8 — the "respect for private life" text the memo treats as the most transplant-ready model — the Grand Chamber in *A, B and C v. Ireland* (Dec. 16, 2010) held that while "Article 8 cannot be interpreted as conferring a right to abortion," access to abortion falls *within the scope* of private life (11–6 no violation for A and B; unanimous violation for C on procedural grounds). Any US text using "private life" will be read against that record by both sides. Reinforces the memo's choice to anchor candidate 2 to *intrusion into private life and information* rather than "decisions," and argues for the savings clause in candidate 7 to state expressly that the article confers no right respecting any particular conduct or decision. ([ECtHR legal summary, HUDOC](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/fre?i=002-680))

### Extends: Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

- **Candidate 7 / delegation — *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (June 28, 2024, 6–3)** overruled *Chevron*: courts now interpret ambiguous statutes independently rather than deferring to agencies. Consequence for the memo's "principles, not machinery" design: delegations of privacy machinery must run to *Congress* ("as further defined by law"), not to agency gap-filling; an enforcement clause modeled on the 14th/15th Amendment §5 pattern supplies the constitutional authority, but the FTC or any successor regulator will need express statutory text for each rule it writes. ([Cornell LII opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-451))
- **Candidate 6 / frozen-list trap — the list is already moving.** Colorado (H.B. 24-1058, enacted April 17, 2024) became the first state to add **neural data** to a comprehensive privacy law's sensitive-data tier; California followed (SB 1223, signed Sept. 2024, adding "neural data" to CCPA sensitive personal information); Vermont's 2026 law includes neural data among sensitive categories requiring explicit consent. Concrete support for the "including… as further defined by law" drafting: a category that did not exist in any 2018–2022 federal bill is now in three state statutes. ([Hunton on CO](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data); [Covington on CA SB 1223](https://www.insideprivacy.com/uncategorized/california-enacts-health-ai-bill-and-protections-for-neural-data/); [Covington on VT](https://www.insideprivacy.com/state-privacy/vermont-data-privacy-bill-signed-into-law/))
- **Candidate 1 watch item — the closest thing to direct warrant polling.** YouGov for Demand Progress Education Fund and FreedomWorks (a left–right sponsor pairing), fieldwork **Dec. 5–7, 2023**, n=800 US adults: 76% say agencies should be required to obtain a warrant before searching §702 data for Americans' communications (12% no); **80%** say Congress should pass a law requiring a warrant before government purchases data from brokers (10% no). Caveats the founder should weigh: the questionnaire carries an advocacy preamble, and the published codebook reports toplines only — **no party crosstabs are published**, so it does not satisfy the bipartisan-floor measurement on its own. ([DPEF release, Dec. 14, 2023](https://demandprogresseducationfund.org/new-polling-as-mass-surveillance-debate-reaches-final-stages-in-congress-americans-demonstrate-overwhelming-support-for-increased-privacy-protections/); [YouGov codebook](https://s3.amazonaws.com/demandprogress/documents/2023-12_warrantless_surviellance_polling_codebook.pdf))
- **Government-surveillance attitudes with party breakdowns — YouGov, fieldwork May 30–June 3, 2025, n=1,131 US adult citizens:** 56% call collecting and analyzing Americans' phone and internet data "an unnecessary intrusion" vs. 24% "justified" (Republicans more likely to call it justified, 36% vs. 21% of Democrats); 71% are concerned surveillance powers could be used "to target political opponents or suppress dissent" (very concerned: 55% D vs. 23% R); 84% (92% R / 80% D) accept monitoring suspected terrorists' online activity; 24% (33% D / 16% R) have self-censored online for fear of government monitoring. The partisan gaps track which party holds the executive — the veil-of-ignorance case for a rule that binds every administration. ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/52425-what-americans-think-about-privacy-united-states-government-surveillance-in-2025-poll))
