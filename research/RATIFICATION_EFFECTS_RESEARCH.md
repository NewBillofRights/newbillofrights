# Ratification Effects — What Happens to Existing Law When an Amendment Passes

*Research memo, 2026-08-18. Companion to the eight category memos, each of which now carries an
"Implementation: the law today and the day after" section applying this general law to its own
draft. Public page: `/amendments/after-ratification`.*

## 1. How an amendment becomes part of the Constitution

- **Article V.** Congress proposes an amendment by two-thirds of both Houses (or a convention
  called on application of two-thirds of the state legislatures proposes it); it becomes part of
  the Constitution when ratified by three-fourths of the states — 38 of 50 — by legislatures or by
  state conventions, as Congress directs. — [National Archives, "Constitutional Amendment
  Process"](https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/constitution)
- **No presidential role.** The President neither signs nor may veto a proposed amendment; the
  Supreme Court settled this in 1798 (the Eleventh Amendment was valid without presidential
  approval, an amendment being "a substantive act, unconnected with the ordinary business of
  legislation"). — [*Hollingsworth v. Virginia*, 3 U.S. 378 (1798), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/3/378)
- **Certification.** The Archivist of the United States, through the Office of the Federal
  Register, receives state ratifications and, on reaching 38, certifies the amendment by
  proclamation published in the Federal Register. — [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/constitution)
- **Speed varies enormously.** The Twenty-sixth Amendment was passed by Congress March 23, 1971
  and ratified July 1, 1971; the Twenty-seventh was proposed September 25, 1789 and ratified May 7,
  1992. — [National Archives, Amendments 11–27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27)

## 2. What happens to laws already on the books

Once ratified, an amendment is part of "the supreme Law of the Land" — effective on the date the last necessary state ratifies, not the date it is proclaimed: "the date of its consummation, and not that on which it is proclaimed, controls." — [*Dillon v. Gloss*, 256 U.S. 368 (1921), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/256/368) Existing federal statutes and
state constitutions and laws that conflict with it do not have to be repealed to lose force — but
they are not erased either. In practice:

- **Conflicting state law becomes unenforceable at once.** When the Nineteenth Amendment was
  challenged, the Court held it validly ratified and part of the Constitution, which invalidated
  state constitutional provisions limiting the vote to men; state ratification "transcends any
  limitations sought to be imposed by the people of a state." — [*Leser v. Garnett*, 258 U.S. 130
  (1922), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/258/130)
- **A federal statute that rests on a repealed provision falls with it — even mid-prosecution.**
  After the Twenty-first Amendment repealed the Eighteenth, prosecutions under the National
  Prohibition Act could not continue: "By the repeal the legislative will is expressed that no
  further proceedings be had under the act repealed." — [*United States v. Chambers*, 291 U.S. 217
  (1934), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/291/217)
- **The dead statute stays on the books until repealed.** Nothing removes conflicting text from
  the statute books automatically; it simply cannot be applied. Repeal (by Congress or a
  legislature) or a court judgment declaring the conflict does the housekeeping. Laws consistent
  with the amendment stand.
- **Retroactivity is set by the text.** *Hollingsworth* applied the Eleventh Amendment to pending
  as well as future cases; the Twenty-second Amendment, by contrast, exempted "any person holding
  the office of President when this Article was proposed by the Congress." Silence is resolved by
  the courts, which is why careful drafts say what they mean about existing officeholders,
  districts, or grants. — [*Hollingsworth*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/3/378);
  [National Archives, Amendments 11–27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27)

## 3. Self-executing text vs. text that needs a statute

Some amendment provisions work by themselves the day they are ratified (a prohibition or a
right that a court can apply); others create a power or a duty that only takes practical shape
when a legislature acts. Most modern amendments do both, and end with an **enforcement clause**:
"The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation" appears in
Amendments XIII, XIV, XV, XIX, XXIII, XXIV, and XXVI. — [National Archives, Amendments 11–27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27)

What enforcement legislation has looked like:

- **Thirteenth Amendment → Civil Rights Act of 1866.** Congress may legislate against the "badges
  and incidents of slavery," including private racial discrimination in property sales (42 U.S.C.
  § 1982 upheld). — [*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/392/409)
- **Fifteenth Amendment → Voting Rights Act of 1965.** "Congress may use any rational means to
  effectuate the constitutional prohibition of racial discrimination in voting." — [*South Carolina
  v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. 301 (1966), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/383/301)
- **The limit.** Enforcement legislation must remedy or prevent violations of what the amendment
  actually says — "congruence and proportionality between the injury to be prevented or remedied
  and the means adopted to that end" — not redefine the amendment. — [*City of Boerne v. Flores*,
  521 U.S. 507 (1997), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-2074.ZO.html)

Implication for drafting: an amendment that says "as provided by law" or "Congress shall have
power to enforce" is choosing to leave a piece to statute — the piece does not exist until the
statute does. An amendment that states a flat rule or prohibition applies the day it is certified.

## 4. Anticipatory, conforming, and "trigger" legislation

Legislatures do not have to wait for ratification to prepare for it.

- **Statute first, amendment to finish the job.** Congress lowered the voting age to 18 by statute
  in 1970; the Court upheld it for federal elections and struck it for state and local elections
  (*Oregon v. Mitchell*), leaving states facing two sets of voter records. Congress proposed the
  Twenty-sixth Amendment in March 1971 and the states ratified it in about 100 days — the fastest
  ratification of any amendment. — [*Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112 (1970), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/400/112);
  [Ronald Reagan Presidential Library (NARA), Amendment 26](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/education/lesson-plans/high-school/constitutional-amendments/constitutional-amendments-amendment-26);
  [Smithsonian Magazine, "How Young Activists Got 18-Year-Olds the Right to Vote in Record Time"](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-young-activists-got-18-year-olds-right-vote-record-time-180976261/)
- **States pre-positioning for the day after.** Pennsylvania created its Liquor Control Board by
  state law on November 29, 1933 — six days before the Twenty-first Amendment was ratified on
  December 5, 1933 — so that a state regulatory system was in place the moment national
  Prohibition ended. — [Pennsylvania House of Representatives Archives, "Pennsylvania. Liquor
  Control Board"](https://pahousearchives.klnpa.org/agents/corporate_entities/12); [National
  Archives, Amendments 11–27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27)
- **Contingent ("trigger") statutes.** A legislature can enact a law now that by its own terms
  takes effect only when a specified event occurs — here, certification of the amendment. The
  mechanism is ordinary; the substance is whatever the legislature chooses, which is why the
  category memos describe, per amendment, what conforming state or federal legislation would
  plausibly look like and which existing bills already resemble it.

## 5. Effective dates and transition rules written into the amendment

- Eighteenth Amendment §1: prohibition began "after one year from the ratification of this
  article." Twentieth Amendment §5: "Sections 1 and 2 shall take effect on the 15th day of October
  following the ratification of this article." Twenty-second: exempted the sitting President. —
  [National Archives, Amendments 11–27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27)
- The Foundation's drafts (research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md, as revised 2026-08-18) use the same tools: Term Limits §1
  counts no term "to which a person was elected before the ratification of this article" and §2 exempts
  Justices in office at ratification; Ethics §6 delays §1 one year; Redistricting §3 applies from districts
  established on the 2030 enumeration; Lobbying §5 reaches only those who leave office after ratification;
  Privacy §8 governs retention and use going forward; Pardon Reform §1 makes publication a condition of a pardon
  taking effect; Campaign Finance §6, Privacy §7, and Lobbying §4 carry enforcement clauses
  (Lobbying §4 additionally directs that the enforcement statute "shall provide civil and criminal
  penalties" and an independent investigator).

## 6. Litigation defines the edges

Every amendment's scope is worked out in cases over the years that follow; that is normal and not a
defect. The drafting choices that reduce it are precise definitions in the text, explicit
transition rules (§5), and enforcement clauses that let Congress fill detail by statute rather
than leaving it to inference (§3).
