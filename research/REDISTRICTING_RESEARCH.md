# Redistricting Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

Research supporting **Target Category 6 (Eliminate Mid-Decade Redistricting)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md); companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). Gathered 2026-08-11. The question researched here: can a constitutional ban on mid-decade redistricting — and gerrymandering limits generally — pass the foundation's full-spectrum test (MAGA Republicans through socialist Democrats), and what should the amendment text abstract?

**The headline findings:**

1. **The norm is dead.** Ten states enacted mid-decade congressional maps in 2025–26 — Texas, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, California, Utah, then post-*Callais* Florida, Tennessee, Louisiana, and Alabama — netting roughly +14 R / +6 D seats, in what has been called ["the largest coordinated attempt to redraw congressional districts between decennial censuses in modern American history"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_redistricting). Eight more states tried and failed or were blocked.
2. **The Supreme Court has explicitly left both doors open — and only constitutional text closes them.** [*LULAC v. Perry* (2006)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/548/399/) held that "neither the Constitution nor Congress" prohibits mid-decade redistricting; [*Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019)](https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/rucho-v-common-cause-2/) held partisan-gerrymandering claims nonjusticiable in federal court. An amendment is precisely the instrument that changes both.
3. **A once-per-decade timing rule survives *Rucho*'s "no judicially manageable standards" objection.** It is a bright-line calendar rule, already enforced by a state supreme court under identical constitutional language ([Colorado, *Salazar v. Davidson*, 2003](https://www.iconnectblog.com/u-s-federal-and-state-constitutional-limits-on-mid-decade-redistricting/)). A "fairness" standard may not survive it; the timing rule does.
4. **Every peer democracy already draws boundaries neutrally, on fixed schedules, with no partisan mid-cycle redraws** — the UK since 1944, Canada since 1964, New Zealand since 1887, plus Australia, Germany, Mexico, and India. The US is the outlier, not the pioneer.
5. **Statutory and initiative-level fixes are self-repealing.** Tennessee's statutory mid-decade ban did not stop its 2026 redraw; Missouri's Clean Missouri (62% in 2018) was repealed in 2020; Utah's Prop 4 was gutted by its legislature; even California's flagship commission was suspended by Prop 50 the moment the arms race demanded it. Durability requires constitutional entrenchment — the New Zealand lesson.
6. **The polling shows a principled consensus with situational defection** — only [9% say states should be allowed to draw partisan maps](https://today.yougov.com/politics) (Economist/YouGov, Aug 2025), yet 66% of Republicans backed the Texas plan and 63% of California Democrats backed Prop 50. That pattern is not a contradiction; it is the signature of a prisoner's dilemma — and the argument for a *mutual, simultaneous, national* constitutional rule.

## Amendment Framework

### Core provisions

A full-spectrum redistricting amendment has four candidate layers, in ascending order of ambition and descending order of consensus:

1. **The timing rule (core):** congressional (and optionally state legislative) districts are established once following each decennial census and may not be altered until the next census.
2. **The remedial exception:** courts retain power to order redraws to cure violations of the Constitution or federal law (malapportionment, Voting Rights Act) — without this, the amendment would freeze illegal maps.
3. **The anti-favoritism floor (optional):** no plan may be drawn to unduly favor or disfavor a political party — this is the *Rucho* reversal, and the layer with the most doctrinal risk.
4. **The neutral-authority principle (optional):** map-drawing by bodies insulated from the officeholders whose seats are drawn — the international norm and the [82%-support proposal (NBC News 2025)](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics), but also the layer with the most federalism friction.

### Challenges

- **Situational hypocrisy is rampant.** [66% of Republicans approved the Texas mid-decade plan; 63% of California Democrats backed the Prop 50 counter-gerrymander; only 18% of Democrats would keep California's commission if it cost their party seats](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). Any campaign will face "why should we disarm first?" from both directions.
- **The "mutual disarmament" framing is untested in polling.** The [Common Cause September 2025 finding](https://www.commoncause.org) that two-thirds — including majorities of Republicans and independents — back a nationwide gerrymandering/mid-decade ban is encouraging but *advocacy-commissioned* and should be independently replicated before it anchors strategy.
- **Timing rule vs. fairness rule is a real fork.** The timing rule is judicially manageable and symmetric but leaves decennial gerrymandering intact. The fairness rule attacks the disease but invites the *Rucho* manageability objection at constitutional altitude.
- **The entrenchment question:** an amendment ratified mid-decade either freezes the 2025–26 gerrymanders until 2030 (unacceptable to Democrats, who netted −8) or voids them retroactively (unacceptable to Republicans). Effective-date drafting is the whole ballgame — see trap notes below.

### Strategic read

The mutual-disarmament logic is the core of the full-spectrum case. Gerrymandering is a classic arms race: each party's voters oppose the weapon *in principle* (only 9% endorse partisan map-drawing) but demand its use *when the other side shoots first*. Texas → Prop 50 → Missouri/Ohio/NC → the post-*Callais* Southern wave is exactly the escalation spiral game theory predicts. No state, and no party, can unilaterally disarm: California's own commission — the national model — was suspended by its own Democratic voters the moment unilateral virtue became a five-seat liability. **A constitutional amendment is the only mechanism by which both parties give up the weapon simultaneously, everywhere, irrevocably.** That is not a Democratic frame or a Republican frame: in 2025 a Texas Democrat ([Veasey's Anti-Rigging Act](https://thefulcrum.us/governance-legislation/mid-decade-redistricting)) and a California Republican ([Kiley's H.R. 4358](https://kiley.house.gov/posts/rep-kiley-to-introduce-redistricting-legislation)) introduced substantively identical mid-decade bans within weeks of each other, each aimed at the *other* party's governor. The demand exists on both sides; it just never exists on both sides *of the same map*. A constitutional rule with a post-2030 effective date is the device that lets each side lock the door before knowing who the next intruder is — a Rawlsian veil-of-ignorance sell that maps cleanly onto MAGA "stop the riggers" instincts and progressive "protect democracy" instincts alike.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

**The one-person-one-vote arc is the proof of concept for constitutionalizing districting rules.** In *Colegrove v. Green* (1946) Justice Frankfurter warned courts away from the "political thicket" of districting. [*Baker v. Carr* (1962)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/369/186/) held malapportionment claims justiciable anyway, and [*Reynolds v. Sims* (1964)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/377/533/) (with *Wesberry v. Sanders* for Congress) imposed the equal-population rule nationwide. Within a decade, a bright-line numerical districting rule went from "unthinkably political" to universally enforced and universally accepted. That is the template: **bright-line districting rules can be constitutionalized and administered; vague fairness aspirations cannot.**

**The partisan-gerrymandering doctrine, case by case:**

- [*Gaffney v. Cummings* (1973)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/412/735/) — upheld Connecticut's deliberate *bipartisan* gerrymander; the Court blessed political line-drawing so long as it roughly mirrored statewide party strength.
- [*Vieth v. Jubelirer* (2004)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/541/267/) — a four-justice plurality declared partisan-gerrymandering claims nonjusticiable; Kennedy's concurrence kept the door ajar pending a manageable standard that never satisfied him.
- [*LULAC v. Perry* (2006)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/548/399/) — **the mid-decade Texas case, and the direct target of this amendment.** After Tom DeLay engineered Texas's 2003 mid-decade re-redistricting, the Court held that "neither the Constitution nor Congress has stated any explicit prohibition of mid-decade redistricting" ([CRS analysis](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RS22479.html)) and declined to treat mid-decade partisan timing as itself unconstitutional — while striking one district (TX-23) under VRA §2. The holding is an invitation: the Court said the prohibition doesn't exist, not that it couldn't.
- [*Arizona State Legislature v. AIRC* (2015)](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-129/arizona-state-legislature-v-arizona-independent-redistricting-commission/) — 5–4, the Elections Clause's "Legislature" includes the state's whole lawmaking process, including voter initiative; **independent commissions are constitutional.** Any amendment can build on this settled ground.
- [*Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019)](https://ballotpedia.org/Rucho_v._Common_Cause) — 5–4, partisan-gerrymandering claims are political questions "beyond the reach of the federal courts." Roberts's stated reason: no "judicially discoverable and manageable standards." [Kagan's dissent](https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2019/10/elena-kagan-audio-partisan-gerrymandering-dissent.html): courts "have coalesced around manageable judicial standards… They do not require — indeed, they do not permit — courts to rely on their own ideas of electoral fairness," and: "The people are sovereign… Election Day is what links the people to their representatives… And partisan gerrymandering can make it meaningless." *Rucho* expressly left state courts, state constitutions, and Congress free to act — an amendment supersedes it entirely.
- [*Moore v. Harper* (2023)](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10838) — 6–3, the "independent state legislature" theory rejected; state constitutions and courts *do* constrain legislatures' federal-election rules. Together with *AIRC*, this secures every state-level check an amendment would federalize.
- [*Louisiana v. Callais* (April 2026)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_redistricting) — partially dismantled VRA §2 majority-minority districting obligations, immediately triggering the second mid-decade wave (Florida +4R, Tennessee, Louisiana, Alabama). Lesson for drafting: the remedial-redraw exception must reference "this Constitution or laws of the United States" generically, not the VRA by name, because the statutory landscape underneath is unstable.

**The 2025–26 mid-decade war, tallied** (per [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Redistricting_ahead_of_the_2026_elections) and [Wikipedia's running compilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_redistricting)):

**State-law restrictions that already exist** — the drafting precedents: the [CRS fact sheet on mid-decade redistricting](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13082.html) and [I-CONnect's survey](https://www.iconnectblog.com/u-s-federal-and-state-constitutional-limits-on-mid-decade-redistricting/) identify roughly a dozen states with some restriction. The load-bearing examples: **Colorado** — in *People ex rel. Salazar v. Davidson* (2003) the state supreme court read Art. V's "following each census" language as *once* per decade and voided a Texas-style 2003 re-redistricting; **New York** — explicit constitutional ban on mid-decade congressional redistricting "unless modified pursuant to court order" (a ready-made federal template clause); **Tennessee** — a *statutory* ban ("districts may not be changed between apportionments") that proved worthless in May 2026 because legislatures can amend their own statutes; **North Carolina and Massachusetts** — constitutional bans for state-legislative maps only. The pattern to abstract: simple "following each census" text, read naturally by courts as a once-per-decade rule, works; statutes don't.

**Federal legislative vehicles** (CRS counts ~70 mid-decade-prohibition bills since 2005, nine in the 119th Congress alone):

- **[John Tanner Fairness and Independence in Redistricting Act](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/116/hr130)** — the historically closest-to-bipartisan template, introduced by Blue Dog Democrat Tanner from 2005 onward (H.R. 2642: 46 D + 2 R cosponsors): independent commissions plus a flat ban on re-redistricting "until after the next apportionment, unless ordered by a court." Reintroduced in the 119th as the [Tanner–Cooper FAIR Act, H.R. 5426](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5426/all-info).
- **[Freedom to Vote Act](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/freedom-vote-act-big-deal-redistricting)** (2021; Brennan Center and [CLC](https://campaignlegal.org/update/what-freedom-vote-act-means-partisan-gerrymandering) summaries — *both advocacy sources*) — the Democratic template: a statutory partisan-gerrymandering ban enforced by quantitative thresholds in D.C. federal court, supplying exactly the "manageable standard" *Rucho* said was missing, plus process/transparency rules. Instructive as the maximal statutory design — and as a reminder that it failed to a filibuster, twice.
- **The 2025 crop:** [Veasey's Anti-Rigging Act](https://thefulcrum.us/governance-legislation/mid-decade-redistricting) (D-TX, July 2025); **[Kiley's H.R. 4358](https://abc7news.com/post/democrats-republicans-introduce-bills-ban-mid-decade-congressional-redistricting-nationwide/17851661/)** (introduced as R-CA, Aug 2025; [sitting as an independent by mid-2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/18/nx-s1-5823700/rep-kevin-kiley-i-calif-on-his-bill-to-stop-mid-cycle-redistricting-efforts)) — banning mid-decade redistricting and nullifying mid-decade maps unless court-mandated; the [Padilla–Lofgren Redistricting Reform Act of 2025 (S. 2885/H.R. 5449)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2885/text) — mid-decade ban plus mandatory independent commissions; the [Gonzalez SAD Act](https://gonzalez.house.gov/media/press-releases/congressman-vicente-gonzalez-introduces-bill-prohibit-mid-decade-redistricting) and [Davis RESET Act](https://dondavis.house.gov/media/press-releases/congressman-don-davis-introduces-legislation-hit-reset-mid-decade); plus a recurring Coretta Scott King Mid-Decade Redistricting Prohibition Act. None will pass this Congress — which is itself the argument that this belongs in the amendment pipeline, not the statutory one.

### Prior Amendment Proposals

Constitutional (as opposed to statutory) gerrymandering amendments have been proposed repeatedly but thinly at the federal level — H.J.Res. proposals for independent commissions and once-per-decade rules recur without ever reaching a floor vote; the serious drafting energy has gone into statutes (Tanner, FTVA) precisely because Article V seemed unreachable. The state record is the real precedent library: voter-initiated *constitutional* amendments creating commissions or districting criteria passed in Arizona (2000), Florida (Fair Districts, 2010), California (2008/2010), Ohio (2015, 2018), Colorado, Michigan, Missouri, Utah (2018), Virginia (2020), New York (2014). The abstraction: when districting rules are put to voters as constitutional text, they pass — usually by landslides (see next section) — and the *contested* question is never the principle, only the durability.

## State Models

**The independent-commission states — AZ, CA, CO, MI, MT, WA (+ ID, NJ, HI variants):**

- **Arizona** (Prop 106, 2000): first citizen-initiated independent commission; survived *AIRC* (2015) and *Harris* (2016) at the Supreme Court.
- **California** (Props 11/20, 2008–2010): the flagship — 14 citizens, partisan-balance quotas, selection by lottery from a vetted pool. **And in November 2025 its own electorate suspended its congressional map by a 29-point margin** the moment countering Texas required it. The single most important datum in this file: *unilateral state-level reform is unstable under arms-race conditions.* Prop 50 sunsets back to the commission after 2030 — the "temporary emergency" framing was essential to its passage.
- **Colorado** (Amendments Y & Z, 2018: [71.2% and 70.8%](https://ballotpedia.org/Colorado_Amendment_Y,_Independent_Commission_for_Congressional_Redistricting_Amendment_(2018))) — legislatively referred, genuinely bipartisan campaign; the ceiling case for consensus.
- **Michigan** ([Prop 2, 2018: 61.3%](https://ballotpedia.org/Michigan_Proposal_2,_Independent_Redistricting_Commission_Initiative_(2018))) — Voters Not Politicians, a pure volunteer petition drive; 13 citizens (4 R, 4 D, 5 unaffiliated) selected by mailed lottery.
- **Montana and Washington**: older bipartisan commissions, low-drama.

**The instructive failures:**

- **Ohio** — a full curriculum in one state. May 2018's legislatively referred congressional reform [passed with 74.9%](https://ballotpedia.org/Ohio_Issue_1,_Congressional_Redistricting_Procedures_Amendment_(May_2018)): bipartisan supermajority required for a 10-year map, with a *4-year map* as the penalty for a party-line vote. The GOP took the 4-year penalty map in 2021 — which meant Ohio's constitution *scheduled* a redraw for 2025, converting the penalty into a mid-decade opportunity that delivered +2 R in the middle of the arms race. Then [November 2024's Issue 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Ohio_Issue_1) — a citizens'-commission fix — [failed 53.7–46.3](https://ohiocapitaljournal.com/2024/11/05/ohio-voters-reject-issue-1-leaving-politicians-in-control-over-map-making-process-ap-projects/) after the sitting Ballot Board wrote ballot language describing the anti-gerrymandering measure as *requiring* gerrymandering. *(Detail, added 2026-08-17: the five-member Ballot Board — chaired by the Secretary of State, Republican-controlled — approved that language on a [3–2 party-line vote](https://spectrumnews1.com/oh/columbus/news/2024/09/19/ohio-officials-approve-issue-1-ballot-language), two days after the state Supreme Court [ordered two of eight disputed sections rewritten](https://www.statenews.org/government-politics/2024-09-18/ballot-language-for-issue-1-is-set-but-backers-of-the-redistricting-plan-say-the-wording-is-unfair) while upholding the other six; the measure's backers sued, calling the language "the most biased, inaccurate, deceptive, and unconstitutional" the state had seen ([News 5 Cleveland](https://www.news5cleveland.com/news/local-news/ohio-ballot-board-approves-secretary-laroses-redistricting-amendment-language-citizens-not-politicians-set-to-file-lawsuit); case study: [Kettering Foundation](https://kettering.org/ohios-issue-1-a-case-study-in-ballot-manipulation/); overview: [Wikipedia, 2024 Ohio Issue 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Ohio_Issue_1)). Lesson for our own drafting: whoever controls ballot summary language controls the frame — a reform that reaches the ballot through incumbent-controlled machinery must expect a hostile summary.)* Lessons: (1) never build a mandatory mid-decade redraw into a reform design; (2) incumbent-controlled ballot machinery can kill reform framing; (3) 75% support for the principle does not survive a hostile summary.
- **Missouri** — [Clean Missouri passed with ~62% in 2018](https://ballotpedia.org/Missouri_Amendment_1,_Lobbying,_Campaign_Finance,_and_Redistricting_Initiative_(2018)) (nonpartisan state demographer + fairness formula); the legislature put repeal on the 2020 ballot bundled with lobbying-gift and campaign-finance sweeteners, and [voters repealed it ~51–49](https://missouriindependent.com/2020/11/04/voters-repeal-clean-missouri-redistricting-plan-they-enacted-in-2018/). Then Missouri redrew mid-decade in 2025 anyway. Lesson: initiative-level reform is one hostile referendum away from death.
- **Utah** — Prop 4 (2018, 50.3%) created an advisory commission; the legislature repealed it in 2020; the Utah Supreme Court revived it in 2024 (legislatures may not eviscerate government-reform initiatives), producing the **court-ordered November 2025 map** — the cycle's one clean example of the *remedial* mid-decade redraw an amendment must preserve.
- **Virginia** (2020 amendment, ~66%): hybrid legislator-citizen commission that deadlocked in 2021, throwing maps to the state supreme court — a reminder that commission *design* (tie-breaking especially) is where reforms succeed or fail, and an argument for keeping design details out of constitutional text.

**Ballot-share summary:** commission/anti-gerrymandering measures scored 56% (AZ 2000), 61% (MI), 63% (FL 2010), 66% (VA), 71% (CO ×2), 75% (OH May 2018), 62% (MO 2018) — routinely clearing both-party majorities in red, blue, and purple states. That is full-spectrum-test evidence at the ballot box, stronger than any survey: **when framed as a rules-of-the-game question rather than a partisan one, this reform wins landslides everywhere it isn't sabotaged.**

## International Models

Every peer democracy assigns boundary-drawing to a neutral body on a fixed statutory schedule. None permits partisan mid-cycle redraws. Founding dates, structures, and lessons:

### United Kingdom — Boundary Commissions (since 1944)

Four permanent commissions (England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland) created by the House of Commons (Redistribution of Seats) Act 1944; the Speaker is nominal chair, the working deputy chair is a High Court judge, and members are barred from party politics. Reviews run on a fixed cycle (now every eight years) targeting electorates within ±5% of quota. Critically, the [Parliamentary Constituencies Act 2020 removed Parliament's own vote on the outcome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_Boundaries_Readjustment_Act) — recommendations now take effect automatically by Order in Council, because MPs had repeatedly stalled unfavorable reviews. *Lesson: even a neutral commission fails if the politicians it draws keep a veto; the 2020 reform is the endpoint the US amendment should anticipate.*

### Canada — Electoral Boundaries Readjustment Act (1964)

Before 1964, the House of Commons drew its own maps after each census, "prone to partisan influence" — the era of Macdonald's notorious 1882 "hiving the Grits" gerrymander. The [EBRA (Royal Assent Nov. 20, 1964)](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure-and-practice-4/ch04-3-e.html) created ten independent provincial commissions per census, each **chaired by a judge designated by the province's chief justice**, with two other impartial members named by the Speaker (historically professors, clerks, non-partisan public servants). MPs may file objections; commissions may ignore them. Once every ten years, period. *Lesson: a Westminster majority-party system voluntarily disarmed sixty years ago and no party has seriously tried to re-arm — mutual disarmament, once made, is stable.*

### Australia — AEC Redistributions (modern form 1983–84)

The [Australian Electoral Commission conducts "redistributions"](https://www.aec.gov.au/redistributions/about/what-is-a-redistribution.html) through a Redistribution Committee (Electoral Commissioner, state Australian Electoral Officer, Surveyor-General, Auditor-General). **Redistributions are triggered automatically by rule, not by political choice**: (1) a change in a state's seat entitlement, (2) malapportionment beyond tolerance, or (3) seven years since the last one. Since the 1983 reforms, Parliament cannot amend or reject the outcome. *Lesson: the triggers are the constitution of the system — when timing is automatic, "should we redraw now?" stops being a partisan weapon entirely. This is the purest expression of what a once-per-decade rule abstracts.*

### Germany — Wahlkreiskommission (+ MMP)

A standing [constituency commission appointed by the Federal President](https://aceproject.org/ace-en/topics/bd/annex/bdy/bdy_de/mobile_browsing/onePag), chaired by the President of the Federal Statistical Office, reports each term; deviation above 25% from average population *mandates* redrawing (15% triggers a should-redraw). The Federal Constitutional Court reviews electoral law aggressively. But the deeper German insight is structural: under mixed-member proportional representation, **the party composition of the Bundestag is set by the list vote, so district lines cannot manufacture a majority** — gerrymandering a German constituency wins you almost nothing. *Lesson: electoral-system design can make the gerrymandering problem nearly disappear (see the PR note below).*

### Mexico — INE (independent 1990/1996; INE since 2014)

Mexico's [300 federal districts are drawn by the National Electoral Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_electoral_districts_of_Mexico) after each census: a technical committee of geographers, demographers, and mathematicians runs a published **optimization algorithm** against fixed criteria; parties sit at the table, comment, and propose — [but have no vote](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1665-20372016000200331&lng=es&nrm=iso&tlng=en). *Lesson: a young democracy emerging from one-party rule solved districting with transparency + algorithm + party voice-without-vote; "consulted but not deciding" is a replicable formula.*

### India — Delimitation Commission (1952; frozen since 1976)

Ad hoc commissions chaired by a retired Supreme Court judge with the Chief Election Commissioner drew boundaries after each census — until the [42nd Amendment (1976) froze seat apportionment at 1971 census figures](https://vidhilegalpolicy.in/blog/the-impact-of-constituency-freeze/), extended by the 84th Amendment (2001) until the first census after 2026, to avoid punishing southern states that succeeded at family planning. Fifty years on, [a vote in Kerala can be worth double one in Uttar Pradesh, and the 2026 delimitation fight is a federal crisis](https://www.theindiaforum.in/public-policy/delimitation-democracy-and-federalism-options-and-solutions). *Lesson — the cautionary one: a once-per-decade rule must never harden into a no-redistricting rule. Freezing maps trades a partisan pathology for a malapportionment pathology. The amendment must mandate the decennial redraw, not merely cap redraws at one.*

### New Zealand — Representation Commission (since 1887)

The [Representation Act 1887 transferred boundary-drawing from MPs' committees to a permanent Representation Commission](https://www.elections.org.nz/voting-system/history-vote/electoral-timeline) — the world's oldest continuous independent boundary authority. Its composition is ex officio and professional (Surveyor-General, Government Statistician, Chief Electoral Officer, Chair of Local Government Commission) plus exactly one government and one opposition nominee, redrawing after each five-yearly census. The [Electoral Act 1956 *entrenched* the Commission's makeup and the boundary rules](https://ojs.victoria.ac.nz/jnzs/article/download/5431/4807/7650): amendable only by **75% of Parliament or a public referendum** — in a country with no written constitution. *Lesson: New Zealand understood in 1956 that boundary rules are the kind of law a bare majority must not be able to touch. That is precisely the Article V argument.*

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Neutrality comes from office, not virtue.** Every durable model staffs commissions ex officio (judges, statisticians, surveyors-general, auditors) or by lottery — never by appointment of the officeholders whose districts are being drawn. The chair is a judge or chief statistician in Canada, UK, Germany, India, NZ.
2. **Automatic timing is the load-bearing wall.** Australia's three triggers, Canada's decennial cycle, the UK's eight-year clock: when the *calendar* decides when maps are drawn, the single most abusable discretionary choice — *when* to strike — is abolished. A once-per-decade rule is the minimal American translation of this.
3. **Remove the politicians' veto over the output.** The UK needed a second reform (2020) and Australia a third (1983) to make commission maps self-executing. Design for the endgame: recommendations take effect by operation of law.
4. **Entrench, or watch it repealed.** New Zealand's 75%-supermajority entrenchment (1956) vs. the American counterexamples: Tennessee's statute, Clean Missouri, Utah Prop 4 — all overridden by simple majorities. Only constitutional text survives a determined legislature.
5. **Bright-line numeric rules travel; vague fairness rules don't.** Germany's 25% deviation trigger, the UK's ±5% quota, "once following each census" — courts everywhere administer these without becoming map-drawers. No peer system asks judges to adjudicate "too partisan" at large; they adjudicate numbers and calendars.
6. **Mutual disarmament, once made, is stable.** Canada (1964), the UK (1944), NZ (1887): in each case the governing party gave up a weapon its successors never seriously tried to recover. The equilibrium after disarmament is self-enforcing because re-arming requires visibly breaking a revered neutral institution.
7. **Never let the timing rule freeze apportionment.** India's 1976 freeze is the fifty-year warning: mandate the decennial redraw affirmatively; a rule that only *limits* redistricting can be twisted into a rule *against* redistricting.
8. **Transparency plus algorithm disciplines even contested processes.** Mexico's published optimization criteria and party-comment-without-vote structure show that legitimacy can be engineered procedurally in a low-trust environment — relevant to a polarized US.
9. **Electoral-system design is the deeper cure.** Germany's MMP (and NZ's post-1996 MMP) make district lines nearly irrelevant to partisan control. Single-member plurality districts are the precondition for gerrymandering; proportional systems mostly dissolve the problem. This file's amendment treats the symptom cluster within the single-member world — the foundation's separate **"Proportional representation?" candidate** ([CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md)) is where the disease-level cure lives, and the two should be cross-referenced, not merged.
10. **The US is the outlier.** Not one peer democracy lets sitting partisans redraw their own districts, and none permits discretionary mid-cycle redraws. "Every democracy we compare ourselves to already did this" is itself a full-spectrum argument — it reads as common sense to the right and as democratic best practice to the left.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. The three below clear it — the timing rule rests on the 9%-support-for-partisan-maps consensus and 56–75% ballot results across red, blue, and purple states; the effective-date and preamble clauses are structural companions. Two candidates are held back at the end.*

1. **The once-per-decade rule (the core provision).**
   Proposed formulation: *"Districts for the election of Representatives in Congress, and for the election of members of any State legislature, shall be established once following each decennial enumeration, and shall not thereafter be altered until the next enumeration, except as a court of competent jurisdiction shall require to remedy a violation of this Constitution, of the laws of the United States, or of the constitution or laws of the State."* *(Revised 2026-08-17: the state-constitution clause was added after the corpus review showed the original text would bar state courts from ordering the mid-decade remedy they used in Pennsylvania (2018) and New York (2022) — see Review Additions.)*
   - Directly overrules *LULAC v. Perry*'s "no explicit prohibition" holding with an explicit prohibition; adopts the exact "following each census" formula Colorado's court enforced in *Salazar* and New York's "unless modified pursuant to court order" template.
   - ⚠️ **Court-capacity trap (the *Rucho* objection, answered):** *Rucho* refused to police gerrymandering for want of "judicially manageable standards" — but a calendar rule *is* manageable; a court need only ask "is this the first map since the census?" Keep the core provision purely temporal and the manageability objection evaporates. Resist the urge to smuggle fairness language into this clause.
   - ⚠️ **Emergency-exception trap:** courts routinely order mid-decade redraws to cure malapportionment and VRA violations (Utah 2025 is the clean example; *Callais*-era litigation will produce more). Text without the remedial exception freezes illegal maps; text with a loose exception invites collusive "friendly lawsuits" engineered to reopen maps. The formulation above threads it: only a *court* may require alteration, and only to *remedy a violation* — a stipulated consent decree without an adjudicated violation doesn't qualify, and drafters' notes should say so.
   - ⚠️ **The India trap:** phrase as "shall be established once following each enumeration" (a mandate) not merely "shall not be redrawn more than once" (a cap), so the decennial redraw is compulsory and the rule can never ossify into a boundary freeze.

2. **The effective-date / anti-entrenchment provision.**
   Proposed formulation: *"This article shall apply to all districts established following the first decennial enumeration conducted after its ratification; no district established or altered other than in conformity with this article shall thereafter be given effect."*
   - Resolves the entrenchment question — **does the amendment freeze the 2025–26 gerrymanders in place until 2030?** — by making the clean prospective choice: everyone's mid-decade maps, red and blue alike, die together at the next census, and the rule binds from 2030 forward. This is the veil-of-ignorance design that makes simultaneous disarmament sellable to both parties.
   - ⚠️ **Retroactivity trap:** the alternative (voiding current mid-decade maps immediately, as Kiley's H.R. 4358 would) is symmetric on paper (+14 R / +6 D voided) but asymmetric in practice and guarantees one party fights ratification to the death. Prospective-only application costs four years of patience and buys the coalition. Do not litigate 2025 inside the amendment.

3. **The voter-primacy declaration (interpretive preamble).**
   Proposed formulation: *"The right of the people to choose their representatives being prior to the power of representatives to choose their voters, districts shall be drawn to serve the equal suffrage of the people and not the advantage of any incumbent or party."*
   - Constitutionalizes the *Reynolds* line and Kagan's sovereignty framing ("Election Day is what links the people to their representatives"); gives courts an interpretive lodestar without creating a freestanding cause of action; supplies the campaign its slogan — "voters pick their politicians; politicians don't pick their voters" — which polls as pure consensus (the 9%/82% numbers).
   - ⚠️ **Dead-letter trap:** preambular rights language that courts treat as hortatory can *legitimize* narrow readings of the operative clauses ("the preamble shows they knew how to say more"). Keep it short, and make clear in the record that the operative provisions — not this clause — carry the enforcement load.

**Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar:**

- **The anti-favoritism floor** (*"No districting plan shall be drawn with the intent, or have the effect, of unduly favoring or disfavoring any political party…"*) — the *Rucho* reversal. Only about half of Republicans call gerrymandering a "major problem" (AP-NORC 2023), the standard tracks the Democratic Freedom to Vote Act template, and "unduly" hands its meaning to the Court that wrote *Rucho*. This is where ratification opposition would concentrate. Path back: survey work proving a symmetric fairness standard holds a Republican majority — or hold it for a second amendment.
- **The neutral-authority principle** (*"Each State shall vest the establishment of districts in a body whose members are not, and are not appointed by, officers or candidates whose districts the body establishes…"*) — commissions poll 82% overall but Republican support is thinner (57% in 2021; 38–19 trust preference in 2025), stated support is soft under partisan pressure (only 18% of Democrats would keep California's commission at the cost of seats), and Ohio's 2024 commission measure lost 54–46. Real-world ballot evidence is mixed where polling is rosy. Path back: replicated polling on the "who may not draw" (rather than "who must") framing, which avoids the federalism wedge.

**The package skeleton.** Minimal viable amendment = **Principle 1 (once-per-decade rule with remedial exception) + Principle 2 (prospective effective date)** — two sentences, judicially self-executing, symmetric, poll-tested in principle at 90%+, already road-tested as text in Colorado and New York. Principle 3 (preamble) is cheap and message-bearing. The held-back layers (anti-favoritism floor, neutral authority) return only with polling proof. Strategic default: lead with the minimal package — it is the version a Texas Republican and a California Democrat have *already both introduced* as statutes.

**Inherently statutory:** commission composition, selection lotteries, and tie-breakers; quantitative partisan-fairness metrics and thresholds (efficiency gap, mean-median, the FTVA machinery); transparency, public-hearing, and data-publication requirements; single-member-district mandates (2 U.S.C. §2c) and any move toward multi-member/proportional systems; VRA-interplay rules and racial-fairness enforcement post-*Callais*; litigation venue, three-judge panels, and expedited-review procedures; census methodology and prisoner-reallocation rules; state-legislative-map details beyond the timing rule; effective-date transition mechanics and map-reversion procedures.

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion. Items are grouped by the memo section they extend.*

### Extends: US Legal & Legislative Landscape

- **Congress already ran bright-line districting rules for seventy years — the *Rucho* "unmanageable" objection has a statutory counter-history.** From the Apportionment Act of 1842 through the Act of 1911, Congress imposed districting standards by statute — contiguity, compactness, "as nearly as practicable an equal number of inhabitants," and "no district electing more than one Representative" (the 1911 formulation). The Supreme Court in [*Wood v. Broom*, 287 U.S. 1 (1932)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/287/1.html) recited that "substantially the same provisions" appeared in the Acts of 1842, 1872, 1882, 1891, 1901 and 1911, and held that they lapsed only because the 1929 Reapportionment Act "omitted the requirements as to the compactness, contiguity, and equality in population" — a deliberate omission, since amendments to restore them failed on the floor. Lesson for the memo's core argument: numeric and structural districting rules were federal law for most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and were administered without courts becoming map-drawers; they disappeared by legislative choice, not judicial incapacity. That is the same "bright lines travel; vague fairness doesn't" pattern the memo draws from *Reynolds* — and a reminder that statutory rules vanish when a Congress declines to re-enact them.
- ***Allen v. Milligan* (June 8, 2023) — the modern baseline for the remedial-redraw exception.** A 5–4 Court (Roberts, joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Jackson and, in most parts, Kavanaugh) affirmed a preliminary injunction against Alabama's 2021 congressional map for likely violating VRA §2 and retained the *Gingles* framework ([CRS LSB11002](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11002); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_v._Milligan)). Alabama then re-enacted a map with a single majority-Black district (signed July 21, 2023); the three-judge court rejected it (Sept. 5, 2023, "deeply troubled" by the failure to comply) and imposed a special master's map with two Black-opportunity districts on Oct. 5, 2023 for the 2024 election. Why it matters here: it is the cycle's clearest example of a *court-compelled* mid-decade redraw over the legislature's resistance — exactly the case the memo's remedial exception must preserve — and, read alongside *Callais* three years later, it shows how fast the statutory floor beneath that exception can move. (Alabama's post-*Callais* motion to reconsider the special-master map is what triggered its 2026 special session.)
- ***Louisiana v. Callais* — the particulars the memo's *Callais* bullet lacks.** Argued Oct. 15, 2025; decided **April 29, 2026, 6–3**, Alito writing ([opinion PDF, supremecourt.gov](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-109_21o3.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_v._Callais); [CRS LSB11431](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11431)). The Court held Louisiana's second majority-Black district an unconstitutional racial gerrymander and that "compliance with section 2 … could not justify the state's use of race-based redistricting," while raising the burden on §2 plaintiffs (intentional discrimination; alternative maps meeting all state objectives). Aftermath as verified: Louisiana's governor suspended primaries April 30 for a redraw; Florida passed its redistricting bill April 29 (four additional GOP-leaning seats); Tennessee eliminated its lone majority-minority district; Alabama called a special session after the Court granted reconsideration of the special-master map. Confirms the memo's drafting note that the remedial exception must not name the VRA.
- ***Harper v. Hall* (N.C.) — the case that proves the "friendly-court" trap runs in both directions.** On Feb. 4, 2022 the North Carolina Supreme Court (4–3 Democratic majority) struck the state's maps as partisan gerrymanders under the state constitution and a court-adopted interim congressional map produced a 7–7 delegation in November 2022. After the 2022 elections flipped the court to 5–2 Republican, the new majority granted rehearing (Feb. 3, 2023) and on **April 28, 2023 reversed itself in full**, holding partisan-gerrymandering claims nonjusticiable under the state constitution and remanding for new maps ([State Court Report tracker](https://statecourtreport.org/case-tracker/harper-v-hall); [Loyola All About Redistricting](https://redistricting.lls.edu/case/moore-v-harper/)). The legislature unveiled a new congressional map on Oct. 18, 2023 (versions projected at 10–4 or 11–3 R vs. the 7–7 baseline — [Democracy Docket](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/north-carolina-republicans-unveil-new-congressional-and-legislative-maps/)) and Republicans won [10 of 14 seats in 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_North_Carolina). Then came the October 2025 redraw the memo tallies — North Carolina's *third* congressional map of the decade. Lesson for Principle 1: a "court may require alteration" exception is only as neutral as the courts; the memo's insistence that the exception be tied to an *adjudicated violation* (not a change of judicial heart) is the load-bearing safeguard.
- **Georgia 2005 — the mid-decade precedent between DeLay's Texas (2003) and the 2025 wave.** After Republicans won unified control of Georgia in 2004, the legislature's redistricting committees approved an identical replacement congressional map on Feb. 25, 2005, discarding the 2001 Democratic-drawn map that Republicans said "splits an egregious number of counties and communities of interest in an effort to make partisan gains" ([Roll Call, Feb. 25, 2005](https://rollcall.com/2005/02/25/georgia-map-all-but-done/)). Both parties' abuses in one episode: a Democratic 2001 gerrymander answered by a Republican mid-decade re-redistricting — and, per Roll Call, a map that mainly shored up incumbents rather than flipping seats. Useful as evidence that mid-decade redraws are a recurring tool, not a 2025 invention, and that Congress declined to act after *LULAC* despite Texas, Colorado, and Georgia in the same half-decade.
- **State-constitutional courts have ordered mid-decade *congressional* redraws against both parties — and the memo's proposed text would arguably not cover them (bears on Principle 1).** In [*League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth* (Pa. Jan. 22, 2018, 4–3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Women_Voters_of_Pennsylvania_v._Commonwealth_of_Pennsylvania) the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held the 2011 Republican congressional map "clearly, plainly and palpably" violated the state's Free and Equal Elections Clause, imposed its own map on Feb. 19, 2018 after the legislature missed the deadline (the U.S. Supreme Court denied stays Feb. 5 and March 19), and the delegation went from a Republican supermajority to 9–9. In [*Harkenrider v. Hochul* (N.Y. Apr. 27, 2022, 4–3)](https://thearp.org/litigation/harkenrider-v-hochul/) the Court of Appeals struck the Democratic legislature's congressional and state-senate maps both for bypassing the 2014 commission procedure and as drawn "with impermissible partisan bias," and a special master's map was adopted May 21, 2022. Ohio's supreme court struck the commission's maps [seven times in 2022 (five legislative sets, two congressional)](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/timeline-ohios-gerrymandered-maps-how-ohio-politicians-defied-court) — and a federal three-judge panel [ordered the state to run 2022 legislative elections on a map the state court had invalidated](https://www.statenews.org/government-politics/2022-05-27/federal-court-intervenes-in-ohio-redistricting-orders-state-to-implement-unconstitutional-district-maps) (2–1, Chief Judge Marbley dissenting: "If there was any hope of the Commission fulfilling its constitutional duty, this federal panel quashed it"). Drafting consequence: the memo's Principle 1 text permits alteration only "to remedy a violation of this Constitution or of the laws of the United States." Read literally, that clause would bar a state court from ordering a mid-decade redraw to cure a violation of the *state* constitution — the very check *Moore v. Harper* preserved and the memo says the amendment should build on. Founder to consider adding "or of the constitution or laws of the State" (with the same adjudicated-violation limiter). Also a cautionary datum for the entrenchment lesson: Ohio's constitutional text (Issues 1 of 2015 and 2018) was on the books throughout and did not prevent the 2022 elections from being run on invalidated maps.
- **2025–26 war — verified updates to the tally table.** *Texas:* on **April 28, 2026** the Supreme Court summarily reversed the three-judge court's November 2025 preliminary injunction (Sotomayor, Kagan, Jackson dissenting), so the +5 R map governs 2026 ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/04/supreme-court-reinstates-texas-congressional-map-previously-rejected-over-racial-gerrymandering/)). *Missouri:* referendum petitioners filed >300,000 signatures on Dec. 9, 2025 (106,384–115,720 required — [Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/12/11/referendum-to-repeal-missouris-new-congressional-map-submits-signatures-for-2026-ballot/)); the Missouri Supreme Court held unanimously on [May 12, 2026](https://www.stlpr.org/government-politics-issues/2026-05-12/in-blow-to-democrats-missouri-supreme-court-says-map-that-targets-rep-cleaver-is-in-effect) that filing a referendum petition does not suspend the map and on [May 27](https://redistrictingonline.org/2026/05/28/missouris-congressional-map-survives-another-legal-challenge-but-the-referendum-fight-is-still-alive/) that the governor's special-session call was valid; on **Aug. 4, 2026 the Secretary of State declared the referendum "insufficient" as unconstitutional**, saying no number of valid signatures could qualify it, and People Not Politicians sued the same evening ([ABC17](https://abc17news.com/politics/your-voice-your-vote/2026/08/04/secretary-of-state-declares-referendum-on-congressional-map-insufficient/)). *Virginia:* the Democratic legislature's amendment authorizing a "temporary" mid-decade redraw passed the April 21, 2026 referendum [51.7–48.3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Virginia_redistricting_referendum) (a map projected to flip four Republican-held seats), and the Supreme Court of Virginia [voided the result 4–3 on May 8, 2026](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/05/08/virginia-supreme-court-voids-election-results-for-virginia-redistricting-referendum-blocking-implementation-of-a-map-that-would-have-shifted-four-congressional-seats-towards-democrats/) because the first legislative vote (Oct. 31, 2025) came after early voting had begun, violating Art. XII §1's intervening-general-election requirement. Virginia is the cycle's clearest *Democratic* mid-decade attempt and pairs with the Republican wave; it also shows a 66%-approved commission amendment (2020) coming within one procedural ruling of being bypassed by a simple-majority-referred amendment.

### Extends: State Models

- **Missouri — the record is more damaging to the "constitutional text" thesis than the memo states.** Clean Missouri (Amendment 1, 2018, 62%) was a *constitutional* amendment; it was undone by Amendment 3 (2020), a *legislatively referred constitutional amendment* (SJR 38: Senate 22–9, House 98–56, near-party-line) that voters approved with 51%. A Cole County judge had to [rewrite the legislature's ballot title](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2020/08/19/missouri-judge-rewrites-ballot-title-for-amendment-3-that-would-repeal-2018-redistricting-amendment/) because it concealed that the measure's "central purpose … is to effectively repeal Amendment 1" — the same incumbent-controlled-summary problem the memo documents in Ohio 2024. Lesson: in states where the legislature can refer an amendment by simple majority and voters ratify by simple majority, constitutional text is one hostile referendum away from repeal — the entrenchment variable is the *amendment threshold*, not the constitutional label. See flagged correction below.
- **Florida's Fair Districts Amendment — the one U.S. test of a constitutional anti-favoritism standard, with a mixed record (bears on the held-back anti-favoritism floor).** Amendment 6 (2010) passed with [62.9%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Districts_Amendment) and wrote into Art. III §20 that no plan or district "shall be drawn with the intent to favor or disfavor a political party or an incumbent." In [*League of Women Voters of Florida v. Detzner* (July 9, 2015)](https://thearp.org/litigation/league-women-voters-florida-v-detzner/) the Florida Supreme Court found the 2012 congressional map tainted by unconstitutional partisan intent, ordered eight districts redrawn, and — after the legislature failed to agree — approved a plaintiffs' map on Dec. 2, 2015. The *Rucho* majority itself cited this as proof that text can supply a standard: "Provisions in state statutes and state constitutions can provide standards and guidance for state courts to apply … We do not understand how the dissent can maintain that a provision saying that no districting plan 'shall be drawn with the intent to favor or disfavor a political party' provides little guidance" ([*Rucho*, Part V, via LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/18-422)). The counter-datum: on [July 17, 2025](https://statecourtreport.org/case-tracker/black-voters-matter-v-byrd) a reshaped Florida Supreme Court upheld the DeSantis-drawn 2022 map (20–8 R) against a Fair Districts non-diminishment challenge, and the legislature enacted a further mid-decade map on April 29, 2026 with the amendment still on the books. Gate-check reading: Florida shows an "intent to favor" standard *is* judicially manageable and won 63% at the ballot — which weakens the *Rucho*-manageability objection the memo raises against the anti-favoritism floor — but also that its enforcement tracks the composition of the court, which is the memo's other objection ("hands its meaning to the Court"). It does not by itself move the floor out of held-back status; it does sharpen the "path back" (poll the Florida wording, which has a real ballot record).

### Extends: Amendment Framework — Challenges (polling)

- **The Common Cause "mutual disarmament" finding now has an independent replication with party breakdowns.** [Marquette Law School Poll, national survey, Nov. 5–12, 2025](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/MLSPSC29PressReleaseNationalIssues.pdf) (n = 1,052 adults, ±3.3; SSRS probability panel), question naming *both* Texas and California: "Do you favor or oppose states, outside the normal ten-year census cycle, redrawing their congressional districts to make them as advantageous as possible for the party with the majority in the state?" — **oppose 71 / favor 28**; Republicans 70–30, independents 68–31, Democrats 73–27. The September 15–24, 2025 wave (before Prop 50) had Republicans 62–38 and Democrats 80–20 — Republican opposition *rose* eight points and Democratic opposition *fell* seven after California joined the arms race. That movement is direct evidence for the memo's prisoner's-dilemma reading, and a bipartisan floor of ~70% on a symmetric question is the strongest independent number in the file for the timing rule.
- **But the floor is softer when the question is framed as a rule choice rather than a condemnation (gate-relevant caveat).** A [Noble Predictive Insights poll for The Center Square, June 1–4, 2026](https://www.newslinelocal.com/p/poll-most-voters-oppose-mid-decade) (n = 2,585 RV) offered a binary: lines "should only be redrawn once every 10 years after the U.S. Census, except when required by a court" versus states "should be allowed to redraw before the next Census if they believe current maps are unfair or outdated." Overall 50–33 for once-a-decade; **Republicans 42–40**, Democrats 57–28, independents 46–23 (31% unsure). Caveats: a media-commissioned poll, not one of the major public pollsters, and the "unfair or outdated" alternative is loaded. Still, it is the only poll found that tests the memo's *actual* Principle 1 wording (once per census, court exception), and it puts the Republican bipartisan floor at a plurality rather than the 70–90% the "partisan maps" framing yields. The memo's line that the minimal package is "poll-tested in principle at 90%+" should be read as principle-level; the rule-level number needs commissioned replication before it anchors strategy.

### Extends: International Abstraction Lessons

- **Lesson 4 refinement — "entrench" means a supermajority threshold, not merely constitutional placement.** New Zealand's 1956 entrenchment works because it requires 75% of Parliament or a referendum. The American counterexamples verified above are constitutional provisions defeated at simple-majority thresholds: Missouri's Amendment 1 (2018) repealed by a simple-majority-referred Amendment 3 (2020, 51%); Ohio's 2015/2018 amendments unenforced through seven adverse rulings; Virginia's 2020 commission amendment nearly bypassed by a simple-majority-referred 2026 amendment that passed 51.7% and fell only on procedure. Article V's two-thirds/three-fourths structure is the American analogue of New Zealand's 75% — which is the honest reason the foundation's federal route can deliver durability that state constitutions have not.

## Implementation: the law today and the day after (2026-08-18)

*Amendment-specific companion to [RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md](RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md) (public page `/amendments/after-ratification`). The general law — supremacy, self-execution, enforcement clauses, trigger statutes, effective dates — is stated there and applied here to the Redistricting draft (PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md §6: Sections 1–3). Every new URL below was fetched and read on 2026-08-18; existing memo URLs are reused as marked.*

### 1. Federal law today

**Constitutional text.** Art. I §2 cl. 3 requires the "actual Enumeration … within every subsequent term of ten years"; Art. I §4 cl. 1 (the Elections Clause) provides that the "times, places and manner of holding elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each state by the legislature thereof; but the Congress may at any time by law make or alter such regulations" ([LII, Article I](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei)). Nothing in the text says when, or how often, a state may draw the districts inside it. Under *Arizona State Legislature v. AIRC* (2015) "the legislature thereof" includes the state's whole lawmaking process, including initiative-created commissions ([Harvard Law Review](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-129/arizona-state-legislature-v-arizona-independent-redistricting-commission/) — existing), and under *Moore v. Harper* (2023) state constitutions and courts constrain the legislature's exercise of that power ([CRS LSB10838](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10838) — existing).

**Controlling doctrine the amendment would displace.** *LULAC v. Perry*, 548 U.S. 399 (2006): "neither the Constitution nor Congress has stated any explicit prohibition of mid-decade redistricting" ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/548/399/); [CRS RS22479](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RS22479.html) — both existing). CRS's March 2026 fact sheet restates the current rule flatly: "Mid-decade redistricting is prohibited by neither the U.S. Constitution nor federal law" ([CRS IF13082](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13082.html) — existing). Section 2 of the draft supplies the "explicit prohibition" *LULAC* found missing; the case's reasoning on that point becomes obsolete the moment the article applies. Section 2 would not, by itself, disturb *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019), which held that "partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts" while noting that "provisions in state statutes and state constitutions can provide standards and guidance for state courts to apply" and that "Congress has regularly exercised its Elections Clause power, including to address partisan gerrymandering" ([LII, 18-422](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/18-422) — existing). The draft's Section 2 is a calendar rule, not a fairness rule; whether Section 1's directive ("districts shall be drawn to serve the equal suffrage of the people and not the advantage of any incumbent or party") is itself justiciable is left to the courts (see §3(f) below).

**Federal rules the amendment leaves untouched (and that Section 2's court exception preserves).**
- Equal population: *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964) — Art. I §2 requires that "as nearly as is practicable one man's vote in a congressional election is to be worth as much as another's" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/376/1)); *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964) for state legislatures ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/377/533/) — existing).
- Single-member districts: 2 U.S.C. §2c (Pub. L. 90-196, Dec. 14, 1967) — in each state entitled to more than one Representative "there shall be established by law a number of districts equal to the number of Representatives" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/2/2c)).
- Apportionment fallback: 2 U.S.C. §2a(c) — until a state redistricts after an apportionment, added seats are "elected from the State at large and the other Representatives from the districts then prescribed by the law of such State" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/2/2a)). This is the statute that governs the gap between census and first map; it interacts with the draft's "established once following each decennial enumeration" language (see §3(f)).
- Voting Rights Act §2, 52 U.S.C. §10301(a): no "standard, practice, or procedure" may be imposed "in a manner which results in a denial or abridgement of the right of any citizen … to vote on account of race or color" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/52/10301)); as construed in *Allen v. Milligan* (2023) ([CRS LSB11002](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11002) — existing) and narrowed in *Louisiana v. Callais* (Apr. 29, 2026) ([opinion PDF](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-109_21o3.pdf) — existing). A §2 violation is a violation of "the laws of the United States" within Section 2's exception, so court-ordered §2 remedies remain available whatever the statute's future shape — the reason the draft names no statute.
- Litigation channel: 28 U.S.C. §2284(a) — a three-judge district court "shall be convened … when an action is filed challenging the constitutionality of the apportionment of congressional districts or the apportionment of any statewide legislative body" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2284)). A claim that a mid-decade map violates the new article is such a challenge; the existing statute would carry it (with direct appeal to the Supreme Court) without new legislation.
- Federal–state allocation of remedial work: *Growe v. Emison*, 507 U.S. 25 (1993) — "reapportionment is primarily the duty and responsibility of the State through its legislature or other body, rather than of a federal court," and federal courts must neither "affirmatively obstruct state reapportionment nor permit federal litigation to be used to impede it" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/507/25)). *Upham v. Seamon*, 456 U.S. 37 (1982) — a court's "modifications of a state plan are limited to those necessary to cure any constitutional or statutory defect" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/456/37)). Both bear directly on how far a court-ordered "alteration" under Section 2's exception may go.

**No federal statute conflicts.** No Act of Congress authorizes or forbids mid-decade redistricting, so the amendment overrides no federal statute; it removes a discretionary state power that federal law was silent about. Congress's Elections Clause power to legislate about congressional districting survives (Section 2 binds Congress as well as the states as to *when* districts may be altered — see §3(f)).

### 2. State law today

**Counts.** NCSL (updated May 8, 2026): "At least 10 states explicitly prohibit mid-decade redistricting" — it lists Alabama, Alaska, Kansas, Missouri, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Tennessee and Utah — but "Only New York and Utah explicitly prohibit congressional mid-decade redistricting. The rest address state legislative redistricting only." "At least six additional states prohibit mid-decade redistricting in some contexts as a result of court decisions" (California, Colorado, Nebraska, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Wisconsin). "A few states explicitly allow the practice" — Ohio (which *requires* a mid-decade redraw when a map lacks bipartisan support), Texas (*Abbott v. MALC*, legislative), Louisiana (attorney-general opinion, legislative), Washington and Mississippi ("at any point in time"), Wyoming (congressional districts "from time to time"). "Most states do not explicitly address mid-decade redistricting at all." ([NCSL, Mid-Decade Redistricting](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/mid-decade-redistricting)). *Discrepancy to note:* CRS IF13082 (existing) reads Tennessee's statute as providing that "[congressional] districts may not be changed between apportionments," while NCSL's line credits only New York and Utah with an explicit congressional prohibition; the memo's earlier "roughly a dozen states" phrasing is consistent with NCSL's ten-plus-six but neither CRS nor I-CONnect (existing) states a total. Commission counts, for context (NCSL, Dec. 2021, pre-Prop 50): commissions have primary responsibility for congressional lines in ten states and legislative lines in fifteen ([NCSL, Creation of Redistricting Commissions](https://www.ncsl.org/redistricting-and-census/creation-of-redistricting-commissions)).

**Provisions closest to the draft (would stand, and supplied its wording).**
- New York Const. Art. III §4(e): "A reapportionment plan and the districts contained in such plan shall be in force until the effective date of a plan based upon the subsequent federal decennial census taken in a year ending in zero unless modified pursuant to court order," and the commission process governs "except to the extent that a court is required to order the adoption of, or changes to, a redistricting plan as a remedy for a violation of law" ([NY Senate, CNS A3 §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CNS/A3S4)). This is the template for Section 2's exception.
- Colorado — *People ex rel. Salazar v. Davidson* (2003): "following each census" read as once per decade; New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio classed as "hybrid/conditional" ([I-CONnect](https://www.iconnectblog.com/u-s-federal-and-state-constitutional-limits-on-mid-decade-redistricting/) — existing).
- The other NCSL-listed prohibitions (Alaska's "shall be effective … until after the official reporting of the next decennial census" is NCSL's example) are consistent with the article and unaffected by it.

**Provisions that would conflict and become unenforceable once the article applies.**
- Ohio Const. Art. XIX (congressional) and Art. XI (legislative): a congressional plan passed by a 3/5 supermajority or bipartisan commission vote "is valid for ten years," while "maps passed by normal legislation are valid for two general elections" — a state-mandated mid-decade replacement that is not a court remedy. Ohio's Oct. 31, 2025 congressional redraw was this mechanism operating: the 2022 partisan-vote map "was only valid through the 2024 elections and required replacement" ([Loyola, All About Redistricting — Ohio](https://redistricting.lls.edu/state/ohio/)). Under Section 2 a four-year map's scheduled replacement would be an alteration "not … required" by a court and, under Section 3, could not "be given effect"; the ten-year track survives.
- State provisions or opinions expressly *permitting* discretionary mid-decade redraws (Texas, Louisiana, Washington, Mississippi, Wyoming per NCSL above) would be inoperative to that extent — not repealed, but incapable of authorizing a redraw the article forbids.
- Temporary authorizations of the Virginia/California kind: Virginia's April 21, 2026 amendment authorizing legislative congressional redistricting between Jan. 1, 2025 and Oct. 31, 2030 "in the event that any State … conducts a redistricting" ([Va. Dept. of Elections](https://www.elections.virginia.gov/election-law/proposed-amendment-for-april-2026-special-election/)) was voided by the state supreme court on May 8, 2026 on procedural grounds ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/05/08/virginia-supreme-court-voids-election-results-for-virginia-redistricting-referendum-blocking-implementation-of-a-map-that-would-have-shifted-four-congressional-seats-towards-democrats/) — existing); California's Prop 50 map sunsets to the commission after 2030 ([Wikipedia, 2025–26 redistricting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_redistricting) — existing). Any such provision still in force when the article applies would be inoperative for the post-census maps.
- **What stands:** every state's decennial process (legislature, commission, or hybrid); state districting criteria (compactness, communities of interest, Florida's "intent to favor" clause); state-court remedies for state-constitutional violations, which Section 2 expressly preserves — the mid-decade orders in Pennsylvania (2018, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Women_Voters_of_Pennsylvania_v._Commonwealth_of_Pennsylvania) — existing) and New York (2022, [ARP, *Harkenrider*](https://thearp.org/litigation/harkenrider-v-hochul/) — existing) are the models; and local-government districting, which the article does not reach.

### 3. The day after ratification

**(a) What becomes unenforceable, and when.** On the day of certification itself, nothing. Section 3 makes the article apply "to all districts established following the first decennial enumeration conducted after its ratification," so every map in force at ratification — including the ten states' 2025–26 mid-decade maps tallied above ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Redistricting_ahead_of_the_2026_elections) — existing) — stands until replaced after that census. From the first post-census maps onward: (i) any legislative or commission alteration of a congressional or state-legislative map that is not required by a court to remedy a violation "shall not … be given effect" — self-executing, no statute needed, enforceable by injunction in the ordinary way; (ii) state constitutional or statutory provisions that mandate or permit such alterations (Ohio's two-election maps; the permissive provisions NCSL lists) become inoperative to that extent. They stay on the books until repealed or declared inoperative by a court, per the general rule (RATIFICATION_EFFECTS §2). *(Founder note: this is the same "everyone's maps expire together" design the page already describes; the implementation point is that it defers all operative effect to the post-census cycle.)*

**(b) What stands.** All of §1's federal rules (equal population, §2c, §2a(c), VRA §2 as construed, §2284); every state's decennial process and criteria; court-ordered remedial redraws under federal law or state constitutions/laws; state prohibitions already stricter than the article; local redistricting.

**(c) The draft's own clauses.** *Section 2* is the operative rule and is self-executing: a flat prohibition with a single, court-only exception ("except as a court of competent jurisdiction shall require to remedy a violation of this Constitution, of the laws of the United States, or of the constitution or laws of the State"). *Section 3* is the effective-date and enforcement clause combined: applicability from "the first decennial enumeration conducted after its ratification," and the sanction that "no district established or altered other than in conformity with this article shall thereafter be given effect." *Section 1* is interpretive. The draft carries **no congressional enforcement clause** ("Congress shall have power to enforce…"); nothing in it says "as provided by law," so no piece waits on a statute.

**(d) Enabling legislation.** None is required for the rule to bind. Congress retains its Elections Clause power over congressional districts and could legislate procedure (venue, expedited review, data-publication or transparency rules, a definition of "technical corrections") consistent with the article; for *state legislative* districts the draft gives Congress no new power, so enforcement of that half rests on the courts alone. Existing bills show what a conforming statute looks like — both introduced in the 119th Congress before ratification and each within Congress's existing Elections Clause power: **H.R. 4358, Anti-Rigging Act of 2025** (Veasey and six Texas cosponsors, introduced July 10, 2025): a state redistricted after an apportionment may not be redistricted again until the next apportionment unless "a court requires the State to conduct such subsequent redistricting to comply with the Constitution or to enforce the Voting Rights Act of 1965"; applies to redistricting after the 2020 census ([govinfo text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr4358ih/html/BILLS-119hr4358ih.htm)). **H.R. 4889** (Kiley, introduced Aug. 5, 2025): same prohibition and court-order exception, applied to redistricting "after the November 2024 election" ([govinfo text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr4889ih/html/BILLS-119hr4889ih.htm)). CRS counts ten such bills in the 119th Congress (H.R. 4358, 4632, 4889, 5426, 5449/S. 2885, 5837, 5879, 7167, 7219) and roughly seventy since the mid-2000s ([CRS IF13082](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13082.html) — existing); the Padilla–Lofgren S. 2885/H.R. 5449 ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2885/text) — existing) and Tanner–Cooper H.R. 5426 ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5426/all-info) — existing) add commission mandates the draft does not. Note that the statutory versions name the VRA; the draft deliberately does not.

**(e) State trigger / conforming legislation.** The mechanism (a law that by its terms takes effect on a specified event — RATIFICATION_EFFECTS §4) is available to any state that wants its process ready for the first post-ratification census: e.g., repeal or suspend a two-election-map provision, or codify "once following each census … unless modified pursuant to court order" in state law. No state has yet enacted legislation contingent on ratification of a *federal* redistricting amendment (none identified). The subject does have a recent example of contingent districting legislation at the state level: Virginia's General Assembly enacted a congressional map that "would take effect only if the constitutional amendment is approved by the voters" at the April 21, 2026 referendum ([Va. Dept. of Elections](https://www.elections.virginia.gov/election-law/proposed-amendment-for-april-2026-special-election/)); the amendment passed 51.7–48.3 and both were voided May 8, 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Virginia_redistricting_referendum) — existing). That instance ran in the opposite direction from the draft (authorizing a mid-decade redraw), but it is the mechanism.

**(f) Litigation questions the wording leaves to the courts** (candid list; the "by law" hooks are none, the exception is one):
1. **"Established once following each decennial enumeration."** When a state fails to enact a map and a court draws the first one (the *Growe*/§2a(c) scenario), is that map "established," so that a later legislative map in the same decade is a forbidden "alteration"? The text does not say. Read with the India-trap purpose ("established once" is a mandate), the natural reading is yes; a legislature will argue otherwise.
2. **Scope of a court-ordered alteration.** "Except as a court … shall require to remedy a violation": does a remedial order reopen only the defect (the *Upham* rule for court-drawn plans) or license the map-drawing body to redraw the whole plan once the door is open? Section 2's "as … shall require" points to the former; expect the argument.
3. **Consent decrees.** Whether a stipulated judgment entered without an adjudicated violation is a court "requir[ing]" alteration "to remedy a violation." The memo's drafting note says no; the text says only what is quoted.
4. **"Altered."** Whether ministerial changes (precinct-line conformity, annexations, correcting a drafting error) are alterations. Several states already distinguish "technical corrections"; the article does not.
5. **Section 3 timing.** "The first decennial enumeration conducted after its ratification": if ratification falls after Census Day of a decade (April 1, 2030) but before that census's maps are drawn, is the 2030 enumeration "conducted after" ratification? On the literal reading no, and the article would first bind the 2040 maps. Ratification before the 2030 enumeration avoids the question; a later ratification hands it to the courts.
6. **Section 1's status.** Whether "districts shall be drawn to serve the equal suffrage of the people and not the advantage of any incumbent or party" creates a justiciable claim (reopening the *Rucho* manageability debate at constitutional altitude) or is interpretive only. The page and memo say interpretive; the text is phrased as a directive.
7. **Congress and "at any time."** The Elections Clause lets Congress "at any time by law make or alter" state regulations. Whether Congress could itself require or perform a mid-decade congressional redraw (e.g., by amending §2c) is doubtful under Section 2, which excepts only court-required remedies; the two clauses would have to be reconciled.
8. **"Districts … for the election of members of any State legislature."** Application to multi-member and floterial districts, to a state that elects legislators from fixed county units, and (by exclusion) to local bodies; and whether federal courts, absent an enforcement clause, will apply Section 3's "shall not be given effect" by enjoining an election on a non-conforming map close to Election Day, or defer to state courts under *Growe*.
