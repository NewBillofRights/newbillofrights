# Fusion Voting — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled nationally — no independent national survey with a party breakdown has ever asked the mechanism; the one independent poll that asks it directly is a single state (56% overall, **55% R / 61% D / 62% I**, Fairleigh Dickinson, New Jersey, Feb 2023), the closest national proxies are themselves partisan-coded in opposite directions (Gallup 43% R / 58% D 2025; Pew 29% R / 46% D Nov 2025), and both non-polling tests that matter here are open — recommendation: **hold as a candidate (gate: a national both-parties poll of the mechanism, plus an answer to the existential-problem test)**.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

**The mechanism.** Fusion voting — also called electoral fusion or cross-nomination — lets more than one party nominate the same candidate, with the candidate appearing on each nominating party's ballot line and the votes cast on those lines added together. A voter who will not pull a major-party lever can vote for that candidate on a minor party's line instead, and the minor party's separate vote total becomes a visible, countable measure of its contribution. It was the ordinary practice of American elections through the 19th century — the Republican Party itself emerged out of anti-slavery cross-nominations — and it disappeared in a wave of state statutes around 1900 whose purpose was to break third-party alliances with the Democratic Party in the North and West and, in the South, coalitions between newly enfranchised Black voters and poor white farmers ([Wikipedia, *Electoral fusion in the United States*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_fusion_in_the_United_States); [Protect Democracy](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/fusion-voting-explained/)).

**Where it survives.** New York and Connecticut are the two states where fusion is legal and in active use with separate party lines; Oregon and Vermont use dual-labeling forms, California allows it in presidential elections, and Pennsylvania and Maryland permit it in certain contests including judicial ones. Most states ban it, and bans have continued to be enacted — South Dakota (1999), Delaware (2011), South Carolina (2022 — [S.B. 108, passed May 12, 2022](https://ballot-access.org/2022/05/13/south-carolina-bill-banning-fusion-passes-on-last-day-of-session/)). Fusion has changed outcomes on both sides of the spectrum: New York's American Labor Party line for Franklin Roosevelt in 1936–1944, New York's Conservative Party line, Connecticut's 2010 governor's race decided within the Working Families Party's margin, and a 2016 presidential candidate appearing on California's ballot under both a major-party and a minor-party label ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_fusion_in_the_United_States); [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/what-we-know-about-fusion-voting/introduction/)).

**The amendment variants.** No fusion amendment has been introduced in Congress. Constructed from the state reform texts and the litigation prayers for relief, an amendment would take one of two forms: **(a)** a right of a qualified party to nominate any consenting candidate, and of a voter to vote for that candidate on that party's line, with the votes aggregated; or **(b)** a narrower prohibition on state laws forbidding a candidate from accepting more than one nomination. Form (a) is the operative version, since (b) leaves ballot-design workarounds intact. Both would have to save state authority over ballot access, party qualification thresholds, and counting procedure "by law," per §6.2 of the method.

**How it entered our pipeline, and overlap.** Fusion voting is one of the founder's own candidate entries — **Michael Hamilton**, listed in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) under "Candidate Ideas (under consideration)" and in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) as "**Fusion voting?**" with no note; [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) carries it as **Unpolled**, grouped with fair single-member districting and proportional representation, with Protect Democracy's explainers as the named proxy. No target amendment overlaps it: fusion governs who may nominate and how ballot lines are counted, and the nearest neighbor, Target 6 (Redistricting), addresses district boundaries rather than the party structure competing within them. The two adjacent candidates — proportional representation and fair single-member districting — share fusion's diagnosis but not its remedy, and nothing here carries over to them.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — the mechanism itself**

- **Fairleigh Dickinson University Poll, New Jersey adults, Feb. 1–6, 2023 (n=808, ±4.6 pts).** The only independent poll located that asks the mechanism directly, and it publishes a party split. Wording: *"Right now, a candidate can only be nominated by one party… Some have proposed that candidates can be nominated by more than one party, which was legal in New Jersey in the past, but is currently banned… Should the state allow third parties to nominate the same person for an office as one of the major parties?"* — **56% support / 32% oppose / 12% don't know**; by party **55% R / 61% D / 62% I**; by gender 62% men / 51% women — [FDU Poll](https://www.fdu.edu/news/fdu-poll-majority-in-new-jersey-support-fusion-ticket-laws/). This is a state sample, not a national one, and New Jersey is a state where fusion was a live legal controversy at the time of fielding; it cannot carry a national verdict. It is nonetheless the best single piece of evidence in the file, and its shape matters: a 6-point R–D gap, which is among the narrowest party gaps anywhere in this research corpus.

**Litigation record — the why-an-amendment evidence, and a live natural experiment**

- ***Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party*, 520 U.S. 351 (April 28, 1997), 6–3** (Rehnquist for the Court; Stevens, joined by Ginsburg and in part Souter, dissenting; Souter separately). The Court upheld Minnesota's fusion ban, holding that "the burdens Minnesota imposes on the Party's First and Fourteenth Amendment associational rights — though not trivial — are not severe," that they are "justified by 'correspondingly weighty' valid state interests in ballot integrity and political stability," and — the line that governs this category — that "the Constitution permits the Minnesota Legislature to decide that political stability is best served through a healthy two party system" ([Cornell LII, opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1608.ZO.html); [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1608.ZS.html)). The Court also noted the state's interest "does not permit it to completely insulate the two party system from minor parties' or independent candidates' competition and influence."
- **New Jersey — *In re Tom Malinowski* (Nos. A-3542-21, A-3543-21).** The Moderate Party sought to cross-nominate a sitting congressman who was also the Democratic nominee; the Secretary of State refused under the state's anti-fusion statutes. The **Appellate Division upheld the ban on Feb. 26, 2025**, reasoning in part that the anti-fusion statutes predated the 1947 constitutional convention and that the delegates "considered but rejected three proposals that would have allowed fusion ballots," and indicating that changing the rule is a legislative rather than a judicial matter. The **New Jersey Supreme Court denied certification in December 2025**, leaving the ruling in place — [State Court Report](https://statecourtreport.org/case-tracker/re-tom-malinowski); [New Jersey Globe](https://newjerseyglobe.com/judiciary/n-j-supreme-court-wont-hear-fusion-voting-case/); [Protect Democracy](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/challenging-constitutionality-nj-anti-fusion-laws/) (advocacy party to the case).
- **Kansas — *United Kansas Inc. v. Schwab*, No. 128896 (Kansas Court of Appeals, Aug. 14, 2026).** United Kansas cross-nominated two state-House candidates who were unopposed Democratic primary candidates; the Secretary of State required them to renounce all but one nomination. A three-judge panel **affirmed dismissal**, holding that although the statutes touch expression and assembly rights under §§3 and 11 of the Kansas Constitution Bill of Rights, the burden is not severe because the party has other avenues to communicate, and that the statutes serve important state interests in "preserving election integrity and preventing voter confusion." United Kansas has said it will appeal — [Kansas Reflector](https://kansasreflector.com/2026/08/14/kansas-court-of-appeals-agrees-with-order-forbidding-cross-party-nominations-fusion-voting/); [WIBW](https://www.wibw.com/2026/08/14/united-kansas-party-appeal-decision-maintaining-state-anti-fusion-voting-laws/).
- **Wisconsin — *United Wisconsin v. Wisconsin Elections Commission*, No. 2025CV001438 (Dane County Circuit Court, filed April 28, 2025).** Still open; summary judgment moved April 10, 2026, with six amicus briefs and the plaintiffs' reply filed Aug. 7, 2026. The plaintiff set is explicitly cross-partisan — it includes a former Republican state Senate majority leader and a former Republican legislator who later served on the Court of Appeals — and the amici include a bipartisan former-members-of-Congress brief and a joint brief from the state's Libertarian and Working Families parties — [Law Forward](https://www.lawforward.org/united-wisconsin-v-wec/) (advocacy counsel); [Wisconsin Examiner](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2026/04/13/lawsuit-seeks-to-declare-wisconsin-fusion-voting-ban-unconstitutional/).
- **Michigan — two suits filed mid-May 2026** by the Common Sense Party and the Libertarian Party of Michigan, in the Court of Claims and Ingham County Circuit Court, on association, speech, and state right-to-vote grounds. **No ruling as of this writing** — [Michigan Advance](https://michiganadvance.com/2026/06/02/minor-parties-in-michigan-are-suing-to-allow-fusion-voting-calling-current-law-anti-competition/); [Election Law Blog](https://electionlawblog.org/?p=156300).

**National proxies — labeled as proxies; none is evidence for the fusion mechanism**

- **Gallup — "a third major U.S. political party is needed"** (asked against "the two major parties are doing an adequate job"), three consecutive September readings: **Sept. 2–16, 2025** (n=1,000 phone / 2,136 web) — **62% overall, 43% R / 58% D / 74% I** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/696521/americans-need-third-party-offer-soft-support.aspx)); **Sept. 3–15, 2024** (n=1,007) — **58%, 48% R / 53% D / 69% I** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/651278/support-third-political-party-dips.aspx)); **Sept. 1–23, 2023** (n=1,016) — **63%, 58% R / 46% D / 75% I** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/512135/support-third-political-party.aspx)).
- **Pew Research Center, Nov. 17–30, 2025 (n=10,357 adults, American Trends Panel).** Wish there were more parties to choose from: **37% overall — 29% R and Republican leaners / 46% D and Democratic leaners**; among leaning independents, 42% R-leaning / 56% D-leaning; 44% of adults under 50 vs. 25% of those 65+. Pew reports the partisan split is "similar to the last time we asked this question in 2023" — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/15/nearly-4-in-10-americans-wish-they-had-options-beyond-the-republican-and-democratic-parties/).
- **New America, *What We Know About Fusion Voting*** — the research synthesis on fusion's actual effects. Fusion "creates incentives for the emergence of minor parties" and gives them leverage when they deliver decisive votes, but that is "increasingly rare as districts have become less competitive," and the claim that fusion reduces system-wide polarization is "more speculative," with "more questions than concrete answers." It cites no fusion-specific polling — [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/what-we-know-about-fusion-voting/introduction/).

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The proxies point in opposite directions and neither is about fusion.** Gallup's "third party needed" reads 62% overall in 2025; Pew's "wish there were more parties," fielded within ten weeks of it, reads 37%. A 25-point spread between two first-rate pollsters on adjacent questions is a wording effect, and it is the clearest possible demonstration that neither number can be transferred onto a specific ballot mechanism (§11 trap: broad-battery numbers are not evidence for a specific mechanism).
- **The proxy is regime-coded, and the flip is on the record.** Gallup's Republican reading ran 58% (2023) → 48% (2024) → 43% (2025) while the Democratic reading ran 46% → 53% → 58%. The two lines crossed and swapped positions in two years, tracking which party holds the presidency — Gallup itself notes partisans' support varies with which party controls the White House. This is the same failure mode that made filibuster reform unstable (§4.3).
- **Support for the diagnosis is soft even where it is high.** In the same Gallup 2025 survey, only 15% say they are "very likely" to vote for a third-party candidate, 54% would switch to a major-party candidate if their preferred third-party candidate could not win, 57% express high concern about wasting their vote, and Gallup puts committed third-party voters at 11% of adults. Whatever the 62% measures, it is not a constituency.
- **The one direct reading is a state, and a salient one.** FDU's 56% was fielded in New Jersey in February 2023, two months after the Moderate Party's litigation was filed there. Salience cuts both ways — respondents may have had context most Americans lack, and the number may not travel.
- **Never asked anywhere:** whether Americans support a *constitutional* fusion right as opposed to a state-law change; whether support survives when trade-offs are named (longer ballots, a candidate's name appearing more than once, minor parties trading nominations for concessions); and whether the number holds when the example party is one the respondent dislikes. No national pollster — Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, or UMD PPC — has been found asking the fusion mechanism at any point.
- **Advocacy concentration.** Nearly every non-academic source in the fusion record is produced by an organization litigating for fusion (Protect Democracy, Law Forward, Center for Ballot Freedom) or by parties to the suits. The court rulings and the pollsters' own pages are the exceptions, and they carry this memo.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **Unpolled.** No independent national poll with a party breakdown asks the proposal. The best available direct reading is **55% (Republicans)** in one state — FDU, New Jersey, Feb. 2023 (56% overall, 61% D, 62% I) — which would fall in the **Moderate** band (45–59) if it were national, and is reported here as an indication, not a floor. The closest national proxies are "a third party is needed" (Gallup Sept. 2025: **43% R** floor) and "wish there were more parties" (Pew Nov. 2025: **29% R** floor); both are Weak at the floor, both are about the diagnosis rather than the remedy, and the Gallup floor swaps parties with the presidency. Per §4.1 the verdict is **Unpolled**, and per §3 an unpolled category with the gate named is a candidate.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — **at risk on the proxies, not on the mechanism.** Gallup's 62% conceals 43% R; Pew's 37% conceals 29% R. The one mechanism reading has a 6-point party gap and no such split. Which pattern a national fusion poll would reproduce is exactly the unknown.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **untested, and the live risk.** No survey has named a single cost. Gallup's soft-support findings (15% "very likely," 54% would switch, 11% committed) show the adjacent sentiment collapsing the moment a consequence is attached — the pattern that killed the balanced budget.
  - *Staleness* — **applies to the only direct evidence.** FDU is February 2023 fieldwork, three and a half years old, never re-asked.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **demonstrated on the proxy, unknown on the mechanism.** Gallup's R and D lines crossed between 2023 and 2025. The mechanism is formally neutral, but "would a new party help or hurt us right now" is precisely the calculation that regime-codes an answer.
  - *Framing dependency* — **unknown;** only one wording has ever been fielded. *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **not applicable in the usual form:** there is no discrete abuse, only a structural condition, which is itself a finding (see the existential test below).
  - *Clearly cleared:* nothing yet, because nothing has been measured nationally. The FDU reading is the one indication that a fusion question need not split by party the way the proxies do — one state, one wording.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **open, and the harder of the two tests.** The case for: anti-fusion laws were enacted to suppress electoral competition, they are a load-bearing support of a two-party structure that a majority tells Gallup is not representing them, and a system in which voters have no exit is more brittle under stress. The case against: this is a claim about the quality of party competition, not a mechanism by which the constitutional order fails — the country has run for a century with fusion banned in most states and legal in New York and Connecticut, and no one has shown a threat-level difference between them. New America's synthesis calls the depolarization claim "more speculative," with "more questions than concrete answers." On the standard that removed civic-information/AI and parked good-government data, fusion currently reads as a party-mechanics reform with a plausible democratic-health argument behind it, not a demonstrated threat.
  - *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes by construction, and the litigation record corroborates it.** Fusion is directionally neutral: it works identically for a party to the right of the Republican Party and a party to the left of the Democratic Party, and New York's history supplies both (a Conservative Party line and a Working Families Party line on the same ballots). The plaintiff coalitions are cross-partisan on their face — Wisconsin's includes a former Republican state Senate majority leader, and its amici include both the Libertarian and Working Families parties. It is the rare candidate that satisfies the veil of ignorance without a drafting workaround. It is also unpolled — symmetry by construction is an argument, not a measurement.
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **partial pass, and the weakest link.** The affirmative answer is answer 1 in §5.3, in an unusual form: *Timmons* forecloses the federal constitutional route, so only an amendment can establish a nationwide fusion right, and the 2025–2026 state rulings show the state-constitutional route failing too (New Jersey affirmed and certification denied; Kansas affirmed). But the counter is strong on its own terms: **fusion is legal today by ordinary state statute in New York and Connecticut, and *Timmons* holds only that states are not *required* to permit it.** A state legislature can restore fusion without touching the Constitution — the statutory path, which §5.3 sends to the statutory list. The amendment answer therefore reduces to a claim about *uniformity and durability*: that a right existing at the sufferance of fifty legislatures, which they have repealed as recently as 2022, is not secure. That is the same argument the corpus declined for *Thornton*-style uniformity clauses in the term-limits package.

**Is the framing that polls the thing we would draft?** Not yet, and the gap is precise. The FDU wording asks whether *a state* should allow cross-nomination. The amendment would establish a fusion right *against* the states, nationwide, permanently — a different question, and one on which the record cuts against assuming support transfers: state legislatures of both parties have banned fusion, three of them since 1999, which is behavior rather than opinion. There is no abuse-framing/remedy-framing gap of the kind that split DOJ independence and the FCC, because there is no discrete abuse to frame; the diagnosis framings (Gallup, Pew) and the remedy framing (FDU) measure different things, and only the remedy framing has produced a cross-party majority. That is an argument for polling the remedy, not for treating the diagnosis numbers as support.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** A national poll, from an independent pollster with published party crosstabs, asking the mechanism in forward-looking, both-parties wording — for example: *"Some states allow more than one party to nominate the same candidate, so that a candidate can appear on more than one party's line and the votes are added together. Would you favor or oppose a constitutional amendment guaranteeing that right in every state — including for a new party to the right of the Republican Party and a new party to the left of the Democratic Party?"* — with an **R floor of 60% or better**, and holding at **≥55% R** on a follow-up that names the costs (ballots grow longer; the same candidate's name appears more than once; small parties may trade their nomination for policy concessions). Second, independent of the polling: a **state-constitutional ruling striking an anti-fusion law**, or a fusion-restoring statute enacted with cross-party majorities in a state that currently bans it. Wisconsin (fully briefed as of Aug. 7, 2026) and the Kansas Supreme Court appeal are the live opportunities, and a cross-party legislative vote would count under §4.2 while sharpening the why-an-amendment problem.

**Evidence that would move it out.** A national poll of the mechanism with an **R or D floor under 45%**, or one showing the FDU-style majority collapsing under named trade-offs, would make this a rejection on the full-spectrum and depth tests. Separately: if a state restores fusion by statute or state-constitutional ruling and the practice takes hold without federal action, that strengthens the statutory path and the category should move to the **statutory list** rather than to a target — a §5.3 outcome, not a polling one. A finding that fusion's effects are as modest as New America's synthesis suggests would close the existential-problem test against it.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate — gate: a national both-parties poll of the mechanism, plus an answer to the existential-problem test.** It cannot be accepted, because §4.1 has no number to work with: the only direct reading is one state, three years old, and would be Moderate rather than Strong even if it were national (§2, §3). It should not be rejected, because it is the rare candidate that passes the symmetry test by construction rather than by drafting, with cross-partisan plaintiff coalitions in Wisconsin and New Jersey as real-world corroboration (§3), and because the one time the remedy was asked the party gap was six points, among the narrowest in this corpus (§4). The two holds are separable: the polling gap is closable by commissioning the wording above, but the existential-problem test is a judgment the evidence will not force, and on the standard that parked good-government data and removed civic-information/AI, fusion currently reads as a party-mechanics reform. If the answer to §5.1 is no, this becomes a rejection on the existential test regardless of what the polling returns — so that call is worth making before any survey money is spent. Founder decides.

## Site summary

Let more than one party nominate the same candidate, with the votes added together — banned in most states since about 1900 and upheld by *Timmons* (1997). Symmetric by construction and cross-partisan in the courtroom, but nationally unpolled: the only direct poll is one state (55% R / 61% D, FDU 2023), and the national proxies flip parties with the presidency.
