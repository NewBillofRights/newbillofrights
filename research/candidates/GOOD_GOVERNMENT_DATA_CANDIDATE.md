# Good-Government Data — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled as a mandate — no independent national poll asks whether the Constitution should require accurate, nonpartisan official statistics; the closest evidence runs the wrong way, because trust in federal statistics is the most cleanly regime-coded number in the whole corpus (67% R / 22% D trust federal economic data in Feb–Mar 2026, with the Democratic figure down from 54% in March 2025, and the parties' positions reversed in 2022) — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: a mechanism-specific poll of a publication-and-integrity duty clearing a ≥60% floor, plus an answer to why-an-amendment that the Evidence Act does not already supply).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The idea entered the pipeline from **Michael Hamilton**, logged in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) as "Good government data (inspired by Steve Ballmer / USAFacts)?" and carried in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) as "Good government data (Steve Ballmer / USAFacts-style)?". USAFacts is a nonpartisan nonprofit that compiles federal, state, and local government figures on what government takes in, spends, and does; the amendment question is whether that function should rest on a constitutional footing rather than on a statute and an appropriation.

Nobody has drafted it. Reconstructed from the idea and from what the statutory and state analogues actually do, it has three possible shapes:

1. **A publication duty** — the government must publish, on a regular schedule, accurate and complete accounts of revenues, expenditures, population, employment, and program results. This is the USAFacts shape, and it is the one closest to text the Constitution already has.
2. **An integrity-and-independence guarantee** — official statistics must be produced by professionally independent agencies, free from political direction as to their substance, conclusions, or timing. This is the shape the 2025–26 events point at, and the shape of the pending bill described below.
3. **A right of access** — a personal right to government records and data, enforceable by suit. This is the state-constitutional shape (Florida Art. I §24, California Art. I §3(b)), and it belongs to the rejected civic-information category rather than this one; see the overlap note at the end of this section.

**What the Constitution already contains.** Two clauses do part of this work. The Enumeration Clause requires that "the actual Enumeration shall be made … within every subsequent term of ten years" ([Art. I §2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei)) — a constitutional statistical mandate that has run since 1790. And the Statement and Account Clause requires that "a regular statement and account of receipts and expenditures of all public money shall be published from time to time" ([Art. I §9](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei)) — a constitutional publication duty covering exactly the fiscal half of the USAFacts idea. The relevant limit is not that the duty is missing but that it is unenforceable by the public: in [*United States v. Richardson*, 418 U.S. 166 (1974)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/418/166/), the Court held that a taxpayer challenging the CIA's statutory exemption from itemized accounting lacked standing, because his claim was a "generalized grievance" with no "logical nexus" between his taxpayer status and the asserted failure. So the honest version of this proposal is narrower than it first appears: the amendment-grade addition is not a *duty to publish* — that exists — but *enforceability, scope, and integrity*.

**The statutory landscape.** The [Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act of 2018 (H.R. 4174, Pub. L. 115-435)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/house-bill/4174) already imposes, in 44 U.S.C. §3563, the exact duty an amendment would carry. Each statistical agency "shall — (A) produce and disseminate relevant and timely statistical information; (B) conduct credible and accurate statistical activities; (C) conduct objective statistical activities," with "objective" defined as "accurate, clear, complete, and unbiased" ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/COMPS-15356/pdf/COMPS-15356.pdf)). It passed the House **356–17** on December 21, 2018 and the Senate by unanimous consent ([Clerk, Roll 484](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2018484); [SSA bulletin](https://www.ssa.gov/legislation/legis_bulletin_021519.html)) — §4.2 evidence, but evidence that Congress *can* self-bind here, which is the problem for §5.3.

**The pending bill.** The [Statistical Agency Integrity and Independence Act of 2025, H.R. 4907](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/4907/history), introduced August 2025, would permit removal of the head of the Census Bureau, BLS, NCES, or BJS only for cause — "proven inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance" — and bar removal based on "the substance, conclusions, or timing" of any statistical release ([sponsor's release](https://whitesides.house.gov/2025/08/05/rep-george-whitesides-introduces-bill-protect-impartiality-and-independence/); [Census Project](https://thecensusproject.org/2026/01/15/statistical-agency-integrity-and-independence-act-h-r-4907/)). Its sponsor and every cosponsor located belong to one party — a §5.2 signal, treated as one in §3.

**Overlap.** The right-of-access shape (3) is already adjudicated: [CIVIC_INFORMATION_REJECTED.md](../rejected/CIVIC_INFORMATION_REJECTED.md) rejected the informing-citizens category on the existential-problem and why-an-amendment tests, and expressly contemplates that if this candidate is ever pursued, the transparency material would ride with it. This memo therefore evaluates only shapes (1) and (2) — the *integrity of official statistics*, which is a different proposition from *informing citizens*. Civic information is about what the public knows; this is about whether the numbers the government publishes about itself are true. No target category covers either.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — these measure trust, not the proposal, which is unpolled**

- **Economist/YouGov, Feb. 17 – Mar. 1, 2026 (n=1,073 adult citizens).** Only **23%** say all (3%) or most (20%) statistics reported by the government are reliable and accurate, down from **36% in March 2025**; 34% say a small portion, 13% none. Trust in "data reported about the economy by the federal government": **40% trust (35% somewhat, 5% completely) / 52% distrust** — by party, **67% R / 22% D trust**, with the Democratic figure **down from 54% in March 2025**. Democrats are far likelier than Republicans to say *none* of the government's statistics are reliable (**20% D / 3% R**). On individual series, the share saying the figure is *underestimated* runs Democrats 62% / Republicans 26% on unemployment, 59% / 28% on inflation, 57% / 25% on COVID-19 deaths, 42% / 11% on ocean temperatures; on COVID-19 deaths *overestimated* the direction reverses, 38% R / 13% D — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/54316-government-agency-americans-see-trustworthy-national-park-service-ice). **This is the decisive evidence in the memo**, and it is decisive against, not for.
- **Economist/YouGov, April 4–7, 2022 (n=1,000 adult citizens).** The same question set under the previous administration: none of the twelve government statistics polled was rated trustworthy by a majority; "Democrats are more likely to trust than to distrust each of the statistics," while "Republicans are more likely to distrust than to trust each of the statistics." Net trust on national vote counts ran **−51 among Republicans / +60 among Democrats** — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/42099-democrats-more-likely-trust-government-statistics). Paired with the 2026 wave, this is a documented flip: each party distrusts the official numbers when the other party holds the executive branch.
- **Economist/YouGov, Aug. 9–11, 2025 (n=1,635 adult citizens).** 39% say the government undercounts the unemployed and 22% say the numbers are accurate; by party, **49% D / 28% R** say undercounting — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/52753-donald-trump-approval-gerrymandering-texas-redistricting-jeffrey-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-inflation-economy-unemployment-august-9-11-2025-economist-yougov-poll). An earlier wave (Aug. 1–4, 2025, n=1,702) put trust in federal economic data at **42% / 46% distrust**, down from 53% in March 2025 — [news write-up](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/slightly-more-americans-distrust-federal-153014569.html) (the pollster's own page for that wave was not located).
- **Pew Research Center, Nov. 17 – Dec. 15, 2014 (n=3,212, American Trends Panel).** On open government data: **53%** say data initiatives could make officials more accountable and 56% say they could help journalists cover government better; Pew reports Democrats more optimistic across these items, at **58% D / 45% R** on the officials-accountable item, with gaps of 6–9 points — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2015/04/21/users-views-on-potential-impacts-of-open-data-and-open-government/); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-do-americans-view-open-government-data/). See the discrepancy note below.
- **AP-NORC / USAFacts "State of the Facts," Oct. 15–28, 2019 (n=1,032, AmeriSpeak probability panel).** At least 8 in 10 of each party agree facts must be "accurate, complete, and transparent," and 6 in 10 or more of each say facts must be "based in data" — the strongest genuinely bipartisan reading in the file, and a *valence* item, not a mandate. Same survey: Democrats trust scientists 72% vs. 40% R, academics 57% vs. 30%, media 23% vs. 11% — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/state-of-the-facts/).
- **AP-NORC / USAFacts "State of the Facts," July 29 – Aug. 8, 2024 (n=1,019).** **49%** say information from the federal government is influenced by the president's political beliefs and 48% say the same of members of Congress; only 10% say policymakers' decisions are always or often grounded in factual information (no party breakdown published) — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/state-of-the-facts-2024/).

**Legislative votes**

- **Evidence Act, H.R. 4174 — House 356–17 (Dec. 21, 2018), Senate by unanimous consent, signed Jan. 14, 2019.** A large cross-party majority for the statutory duty of accurate, objective, timely official statistics — [Clerk, Roll 484](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2018484).
- **H.R. 4907 (2025), statistical-agency removal protections** — sponsor and all located cosponsors from one party; referred to committee, no floor action — [congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/4907/history).

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **Echelon Insights for Results for America, February 2026 (n=1,038 likely voters).** Broad support for evidence-based policymaking: **91% R / 90% D**; 92% say examining evidence of what works ensures "government is actually making progress solving problems"; 85% say they are likelier to vote for officials whose policies "have been shown to work through evidence"; by 58%–37% voters prefer identifying the best programs to expand over identifying wasteful programs to cut — [Results for America](https://results4america.org/evidence-based-policymaking-poll/). Results for America advocates for evidence-based policy; the fieldwork is Echelon's. This is the 70–90% valence topline the corpus predicted, and it is a *disposition*, not a mandate.
- **USAFacts / Harris Poll, published July 14, 2017 (n=2,521; sponsor-commissioned).** 90% say data is critical to believing information; 81% say information must come from a nonpartisan source to be seen as factual; 76% feel information about government expenditures and outcomes is biased; 77% rate civic education fair or poor; 88% prefer facts and figures to anecdotes (no party breakdown published; no fieldwork dates published) — [USAFacts release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/usafacts-releases-inaugural-state-of-the-facts-poll-300488327.html).

**Nuances & caveats**

- **Nothing above asks the question.** Not one item measures favor/oppose on a constitutional duty to publish accurate, nonpartisan statistics. Every number is either a *trust* reading (do you believe the numbers?) or a *valence* reading (should facts be accurate?). Under §11, neither can serve as a floor.
- **The corpus's "66% said open data improves accountability" does not match the source.** Pew gives 53% for making officials more accountable and 56% for helping journalists; the 66% is the Brookings write-up's *combined* share supporting at least one of the two goals — a cumulative figure, which §11 flags by name — and the split beneath it is 45% R / 58% D. Logged as a discrepancy.
- **The valence/mandate gap is the whole story.** 91% R and 90% D want evidence-based policymaking (Echelon 2026) while 67% R and 22% D trust the actual numbers (YouGov 2026). Agreement that statistics *should* be accurate coexists with a 45-point split over whether they *are*.
- **Staleness on the transparency proxies.** The Pew open-data reading is from 2014 fieldwork; the USAFacts topline from 2017 with no published fieldwork dates and no party breakdown; the strongest bipartisan valence item from 2019. The only fresh numbers with party splits are the trust readings, and they are the ones that fail.
- **Regime coding is not a risk here; it is the measured result.** Republicans distrusted every one of twelve government statistics in 2022; Democrats now say "none are reliable" at nearly seven times the Republican rate, and their economic-data trust fell 32 points in eleven months. Both parties have occupied both positions.
- **What has never been asked:** support for a constitutional publication duty; support for removal protections for statistical-agency heads; whether either survives a costs-named framing; and whether support holds when respondents are asked to bind an administration of their own party.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor: Unpolled.** No independent national poll with a party breakdown asks whether the Constitution should require accurate, nonpartisan official statistics, or whether statistical-agency heads should be protected from political removal. The closest proxies, none of which can serve as a floor: 8-in-10 of both parties that facts must be accurate, complete, and transparent (AP-NORC 2019 — a valence item); 90% R / 90% D for evidence-based policymaking (Echelon 2026 — advocacy, and a disposition rather than a mandate); 45% R / 58% D that open data would make officials more accountable (Pew 2014 — the only proxy with a published party split on a data-specific item, and it is Moderate/Weak, thirteen points apart and twelve years old). Verdict per §4.1: **Unpolled**.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - **Incumbent / regime coding — the governing failure mode, and it is measured rather than merely feared.** Trust in federal economic data: 67% R / 22% D (Feb–Mar 2026), with the Democratic reading down from 54% in March 2025; the parties' positions were reversed in 2022, when Republicans were likelier to distrust each of twelve statistics. The corpus's rule is that "a floor measured while 'your side' holds the power flips when the other side holds it" (§11) — here the flip is documented in the same pollster's own series, in both directions. Any floor measured today on statistical integrity would be measured against one administration and would not survive the next.
  - **High-topline / low-floor — at risk, and the risk is now specific.** The predicted 70–90% topline is real (91/90 in Echelon 2026), but it sits on the valence framing. The one data-specific item with a party split lands at 45% R, which is Weak. Whether the mandate framing carries a Strong floor or a Weak one is exactly what has not been tested.
  - **Framing dependency — at risk and unresolved.** A publication duty and an independence guarantee are different proposals with plausibly different floors; the second resembles civil-service independence, which the corpus already classifies as regime-coded (Republican support for a nonpartisan civil service falling 87→66, [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md)).
  - **Depth (trade-off collapse) — untested.** No survey has named a cost: enforceability against the executive, insulation of agency heads from elected accountability, or the price of the collection itself.
  - **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — likely, on the pattern.** Roughly half say federal information is politically influenced (49%, AP-NORC 2024) — the abuse half; the structural remedy is unmeasured, and the sole remedy bill carries cosponsors from one party only.
  - **Staleness — cleared for the trust readings** (Feb–Mar 2026 fieldwork), **not for the transparency proxies** (2014–2019).
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - **Existential-problem test (§5.1): open, and this is the deciding test.** [SELECTION_METHOD §5.1](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) already parks this category here, on the same ground that rejected civic information: a good-government nicety, not a threat requiring a rule that binds future majorities forever. The 2025–26 record supplies the strongest argument yet for the other side, and it is new since the category was filed: a statistical-agency head was dismissed in August 2025 following a jobs report the administration called rigged, and independent analysis estimates the resulting uncertainty shock reduced GDP by roughly $20 billion ([CEPR/VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/when-trust-official-statistics-declines), via [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/05/businesses-investors-flying-blind-when-trust-government-statistics-declines)); and a 2019 Supreme Court decision found the stated justification for a census question "contrived" and pretextual ([*Department of Commerce v. New York*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/18-966)). The case *for* passing is that self-government cannot correct itself if the numbers by which performance is judged are subject to political direction — closer to a rule-of-law problem than a service-quality one. The case *against* is that the country has run on statute here since 1790 without the counting itself failing. **Not decided here; §5.1 is a founder call, and the recommendation turns on it.**
  - **Symmetry test (§5.2): passes by construction, fails in current reception.** A duty of accurate publication and a for-cause removal rule bind every administration identically and are natural veil-of-ignorance rules — the 27th-Amendment shape the corpus prefers (§6.2). But the only legislative vehicle has single-party cosponsorship, the abuse examples in circulation run to one administration, and the trust data show the out-party is always the distrusting party. Pairing is available and should be used if the category advances — the 2022 series records Republicans distrusting every federal statistic under the prior administration, exactly as Democrats do now — but no proposal has been framed that way.
  - **Why-an-amendment test (§5.3): fails on the present record.** None of the four accepted answers applies. *Courts struck the statutes* — no. *The Court exempted itself* — no. *Congress structurally cannot self-bind* — it plainly can, and did: the Evidence Act imposes the duty of "credible and accurate" and "objective" statistics, 356–17. *The text is silent* — it is not; the Enumeration Clause commands a decennial count and the Statement and Account Clause commands publication of receipts and expenditures. The one real gap is enforcement: *Richardson* leaves the Statement and Account Clause without a plaintiff, so the existing text is a duty no one can sue on. That is a genuine defect and the strongest available answer, but it argues for a *standing* clause — and the corpus's pattern (§6.4) is that enforcement machinery is always the unpolled or partisan-coded part. Absent evidence that the statutory route has failed, this remains statutory.

**No framing clears the bar honestly.** The two that post supermajorities (facts-should-be-accurate; evidence-based policymaking) are valence statements no one opposes and no constitutional text would change — a different proposal wearing the name. The only framing measured on the actual subject matter with a party split comes in at 45% R. And the two framings that are the real proposal have never been asked. The abuse-vs-remedy gap is visible in outline — about half the country believes federal information is politically shaped, and the sole remedy in Congress attracts one party's cosponsors — but the remedy side is a blank, not a low number.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

- **Evidence that would move it in.** A single independent national poll (Pew, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, or foundation-commissioned on one of their panels) asking both framings, forward-looking and mechanism-specific, with published party crosstabs:
  1. *Publication duty* — "The Constitution should require the federal government to publish accurate and complete accounts of what it takes in, what it spends, and the size and condition of the population, on a regular schedule set by law." Threshold: **≥60% floor** in the less-supportive party.
  2. *Integrity guarantee, written behind the veil* — "The head of a federal statistical agency should be removable only for cause, and never because of the substance, conclusions, or timing of a statistical release — this rule would apply to every future president of either party." Threshold: **≥60% floor**, and the wording must name "any future president" so the number is not read against the current one (§11).
  3. *Depth check* — re-ask (2) after naming the cost ("even if that means the president cannot replace an agency head whose numbers he believes are wrong"), and require the floor to stay out of the Weak band.
  Corroborating §4.2 evidence: a cross-party floor vote on H.R. 4907 or a successor, or a state constitutional amendment on official-statistics integrity passing by a wide margin in a state of the opposite partisan lean.
- **Evidence that would move it out.** Either of two results makes this a rejection. First, if the publication-duty and integrity questions come back Weak, or the floor swaps parties between waves under different administrations, it fails the **regime-coding** failure mode (§4.3) — the test that has held civil-service independence and filibuster reform out of the target list, and the flip is already documented here in a way it is not for those. Second, if the founder resolves §5.1 as it was resolved for civic information — a good-government improvement rather than a threat to the country's future — it fails the **existential-problem test** and should move to Rejected, since the §5.3 answer is already weak.
- **Recommendation: hold as a candidate.** Gate: *a mechanism-specific poll showing a ≥60% floor on a publication-or-integrity duty framed to bind any future administration, plus a why-an-amendment answer the Evidence Act does not already supply.* Reasoning: the category cannot be accepted, because the thing that would be drafted has never been polled and the only party-split proxy on the subject is 45% R (§2, §4); it should not yet be rejected, because the 2025–26 record is newer than the 2026-08-06 filing and supplies an existential argument that did not exist then (§3). Two findings weigh against holding. The why-an-amendment test currently fails outright: Congress already imposed this exact duty by statute, 356–17, and the text is not silent — it commands both the census and the publication of receipts and expenditures, leaving only *Richardson*'s enforcement gap as the amendment-grade remainder (§3). And the regime-coding evidence is the most complete in the corpus — a two-directional flip in one pollster's own series — so a Strong floor measured in any single year would not be trustworthy. If the specified poll comes back Weak, or §5.1 is resolved as it was for civic information, this should move to Rejected rather than continue to sit. Founder decides.
