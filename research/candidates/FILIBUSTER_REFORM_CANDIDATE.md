# Filibuster Reform — Candidate Category Memo

*Superseded 2026-08-18: rejected 2026-08-18 — see [../rejected/FILIBUSTER_REFORM_REJECTED.md](../rejected/FILIBUSTER_REFORM_REJECTED.md). This candidate memo is retained as the gate-stage record.*

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** 8% (Republicans) on "get rid of the filibuster entirely," Monmouth Jan 2022, against 30% of Democrats — and the same measure read 10% R / 30% D in April 2021 while a Democratic majority held the chamber — Weak, and the floor is regime-coded rather than stable — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: a forward-looking, veil-of-ignorance poll of a *threshold rule* — not "keep or kill" — showing ≥45% in both parties).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The filibuster is not in the Constitution. It is a consequence of Senate Rule XXII, which since 1975 has required "three-fifths of the Senators duly chosen and sworn" — 60 votes — to invoke cloture and end debate on most matters; the Senate adopted its first cloture rule in 1917 at two-thirds of senators voting ([Senate historical office](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/filibusters-cloture.htm)). Rule XXII carries a higher bar for changing the rules themselves. The rule's own text, in paragraph 2, puts the two thresholds in a single sentence: cloture carries if the question is "decided in the affirmative by three-fifths of the Senators duly chosen and sworn — **except on a measure or motion to amend the Senate rules, in which case the necessary affirmative vote shall be two-thirds of the Senators present and voting**" ([Standing Rules of the Senate, Rule XXII ¶2, U.S. Senate Manual, S. Doc. 110-1, via GPO/govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SMAN-110/html/SMAN-110-pg20-2.htm)) — 67 if all senators participate ([CRS RL32684](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32684.html)). That asymmetry is why the rule is in practice changed not by amending it but by a majority overruling the chair — the procedure the same report describes, in which a presiding officer rules that cloture may be invoked by simple majority and a majority sustains the new precedent.

Four distinct proposals travel under the category name, and they are not variants of one idea:

1. **Abolition** — eliminate the 60-vote threshold so legislation passes by simple majority. This is the version pollsters ask about, and it requires no amendment: a Senate majority can do it in an afternoon, as the chamber twice demonstrated for nominations (below).
2. **Reform short of abolition** — restore the "talking" filibuster requiring senators to hold the floor; shift the burden from 60 to sustain cloture to 41 to sustain a filibuster; or carve out categories of legislation.
3. **Constitutionalizing simple-majority rule** — an amendment forbidding either chamber from requiring more than a majority to pass legislation, entrenching the abolition permanently.
4. **The inversion** — raise the bar to *kill* rather than to pass: require 67% to block a bill rather than 60% to advance one. Contributed by **u/smcstechtips** in the r/PoliticalDiscussion thread, also raised by **u/alabasterskim** and **u/johnnyslick** ([REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md)). Unlike the others it is symmetric on its face — and has never been polled.

The category entered our pipeline from the founder's own candidate list — **Michael Hamilton**, "Filibuster reform?" ([AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)) — and was reinforced by the Reddit extraction. No bill or resolution to constitutionalize any version could be verified; the live congressional activity is entirely about the Senate rule, not the Constitution.

**Overlap with existing targets.** Nothing here is covered by a target amendment, but the filibuster is the *mechanism* by which two targets' statutory predecessors died: the DISCLOSE Act was filibustered in 2010, 2012, and 2022 ([CAMPAIGN_FINANCE_RESEARCH.md](../CAMPAIGN_FINANCE_RESEARCH.md)) and the Freedom to Vote Act's redistricting provisions twice ([REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md](../REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md)). The corpus cites those filibusters in the opposite direction — as evidence that the blocking problem is institutional and warrants a constitutional fix to the underlying right, not as an argument for changing the Senate rule. This category is a separate question about procedure, evaluated here on its own.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Monmouth University, Jan. 20–24, 2022 (n=794 adults).** "Do you think the Senate should get rid of the filibuster entirely, keep it but reform how it can be used, or keep it as is with no changes?" — **eliminate 17% / reform 35% / keep as is 43% / don't know 5%.** Party crosstab from the report's own tables: **Republicans 8% eliminate, 21% reform, 65% keep as is; independents 16% / 39% / 42%; Democrats 30% / 44% / 19%** ([Monmouth release](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_012722/); [full report PDF, Q22 crosstab p. 7](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/documents/monmouthpoll_us_012722.pdf/)). Fieldwork ran during a Democratic Senate majority, four days after the failed rules-change vote.
- **Monmouth University, April 8–12, 2021 (n=800 adults).** Identical wording — **38% keep as is / 38% reform / 19% eliminate / 6% don't know.** By party: **Republicans 64% / 22% / 10%; independents 41% / 38% / 16%; Democrats 14% / 49% / 30%** ([Monmouth release](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_042921/)). Also a Democratic majority.
- **Economist/YouGov, May 22–25, 2021 (n=1,500 adult citizens).** Whether the filibuster is a good or bad thing: **Republicans 68% good / 32% bad; Democrats 32% good / 68% bad** — an exact mirror image. The article's headline finding is that "Party is all that matters," and its explanatory sentence is the one this memo turns on: "Those partisan positions always flip when the other party is in control because the rule often keeps the majority party from passing certain legislation" ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/36290-supporting-senate-filibuster-poll)).
- **Economist/YouGov, March 27–30, 2021 (n=1,500 adult citizens).** Reform rather than abolition: returning to a "talking" filibuster drew **54% overall — 57% R / 54% D**, the only filibuster measure located in this search on which both parties' majorities agree. On making it harder to block legislation by mere threat: **35% R support / 34% oppose, against 60% of Democrats in favor.** On a voting-rights carve-out, **53% of Republicans opposed** ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/35050-how-should-filibuster-be-reformed-poll)).
- **Rasmussen Reports, Jan. 10–11, 2022 (n=1,000 likely voters).** "Do you favor or oppose changing the rules to eliminate the filibuster?" — **40% support / 48% oppose / 12% unsure.** By party: **66% of Democrats support elimination; 74% of Republicans oppose it; 51% of unaffiliated voters oppose** ([Rasmussen](https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/public_surveys/48_oppose_eliminating_filibuster)).
- **Rasmussen Reports, Aug. 19–21, 2025 (n=1,127 likely voters).** Same pollster, same wording — "In the Senate, a filibuster can prevent a vote from taking place unless 60 senators agree. Some want to change the Senate rules so that a vote must be held whenever a majority of senators agree. Do you favor or oppose changing the rules to eliminate the filibuster?" — now **39% support / 44% oppose / 16% not sure** under a Republican majority. Rasmussen's own summary: "Opposition to ending the U.S. Senate's filibuster rule has softened, now that Republicans hold the majority in the upper chamber of Congress" ([Rasmussen](https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/trump_administration_second_term/39_support_eliminating_senate_s_filibuster_rule); [question wording](https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/questions/questions/august_2025/questions_filibuster_august_19_21_2025)). **The party crosstabs for this wave remain unverified.** Retried 2026-08-18: the release itself is public and confirms the topline and fieldwork above, but Rasmussen publishes demographic crosstabs only to Platinum subscribers ("Platinum Members can review demographic crosstabs and a full history of our data"), so no party split for this wave is available at any public URL. The 2022→2025 comparison is therefore stated at the topline only.

**Legislative votes — the strongest evidence in this category**

The roll calls are more probative than the polling, because they record what senators do when the rule actually binds them, and they run in both directions with near-perfect party symmetry.

- **Nov. 21, 2013 — Roll Call 242 (113th Cong.).** On the decision of the chair, in the appeal establishing simple-majority cloture for executive and most judicial nominations: **48 yea / 52 nay, chair not sustained.** All 48 yeas were Republicans; the 52 nays were 50 Democrats plus 2 independents ([Senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1131/vote_113_1_00242.htm)).
- **April 6, 2017 — Roll Call 109 (115th Cong.).** The same procedure, extending simple-majority cloture to Supreme Court nominations: **48 yea / 52 nay, chair not sustained** — this time all 48 yeas Democrats and independents, all 52 nays Republicans ([Senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1151/vote_115_1_00109.htm)).
- **Jan. 19, 2022 — Roll Call 10 (117th Cong.).** On the decision of the chair relating to the legislative filibuster, on H.R. 5746 — the attempted talking-filibuster carve-out: **52 yea / 48 nay, chair sustained**, so the change failed. The 52 were 50 Republicans plus 2 Democrats; the 48 were 46 Democrats and 2 independents ([Senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1172/vote_117_2_00010.htm)).

Two identical 48–52 votes four years apart with the parties exactly exchanged is as clean a demonstration of regime-coding as the corpus contains. Neither party has ever supplied a cross-party majority for changing the threshold; each supplied a party-line majority when it held the chamber and the change served it.

**Institutional data**

- Cloture motions filed rose from 39 in the 94th Congress (1975–76) to **286 filed, 284 voted, 240 invoked in the 119th (2025–26)**; the 117th recorded 336 filed ([Senate cloture counts](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/cloture/clotureCounts.htm)). The 60-vote threshold now applies routinely rather than exceptionally — the premise both sides' arguments rest on, and the one figure here that is not contested.

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **Navigator Research / Global Strategy Group, March 7–11, 2024 (n=1,000 RV plus oversamples).** Whether "getting rid of the loophole that allows a small minority of U.S. senators to block legislation that a majority of senators support" would have a positive impact: **60% positive / 14% negative — Republicans 51% / 20%; independents 49% / 15%; Democrats 71% / 7%** ([Navigator](https://navigatorresearch.org/three-in-four-americans-feel-getting-rid-of-the-filibuster-would-have-a-positive-impact/)). Navigator is a Democratic-aligned research project. Two cautions beyond sponsorship: the item measures anticipated *impact*, not support, and the wording ("loophole," "small minority... block legislation that a majority... support") argues the case inside the question. The same series read 43% positive in May 2022. **Corpus discrepancy:** [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) dates this "Navigator, Jan 2026"; the post is dated March 28, 2024 and the fieldwork is March 7–11, 2024. The 51% R figure is accurate; the date is not.
- **Navigator Research, March 11–15, 2021 (n=1,001 RV).** After reading a definition, **52% supported abolishing the filibuster** — no party breakdown published ([Navigator](https://navigatorresearch.org/majority-of-americans-support-abolishing-the-filibuster/)).

**Nuances & caveats**

- **Nothing independent and recent exists.** The last independent national poll with a published party breakdown on the core proposal that could be verified is Monmouth, January 2022 — more than four years stale, and taken under the *opposite* Senate configuration from today's. The Rasmussen August 2025 wave is the newest independent topline, and its crosstabs are paywalled.
- **The major reform batteries do not ask.** Pew's flagship survey of political-system change tests term limits, age limits, voter ID, automatic registration, court expansion, the Electoral College, House size, and Senate apportionment — and no filibuster item ([Pew, Sept. 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/)). The Cato/Morning Consult constitutional-amendment battery of June 25–26, 2026 (n=2,253) tests eight ideas and contains no filibuster, cloture, or supermajority item ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/how-americans-would-change-the-constitution-new-poll-12152648)). UMD PPC's catalog of 200+ positions with bipartisan majority support contains no Senate-procedure item ([PPC](https://publicconsultation.org/united-states/over-200-policy-positions-supported-by-bipartisan-majorities/)). Pollsters ask about the filibuster when a specific bill is being blocked and stop when it is not — which is precisely why every reading is regime-coded.
- **Salience is low.** In Navigator's March 2024 wave, 61% said they understood what the filibuster is, but only 21% said they understood it "very well."
- **Respondents hold inconsistent positions.** In the same January 2022 Monmouth survey, 55% said establishing national election guidelines matters more for democracy than preserving processes like the filibuster, while 44% supported and 46% opposed allowing the filibuster to be used to stop exactly such a bill — Republicans at 57% support for that use, Democrats at 72% opposed. The abstract and applied preferences point opposite ways within one sample.
- **"Reform" consistently outpolls "eliminate."** In both Monmouth waves, keep-with-reform plus keep-as-is exceed 75%, and elimination never exceeds 19%.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **8% (Republicans)** on "get rid of the filibuster entirely," Monmouth Jan. 2022 → **Weak** per §4.1, by the widest margin of any category in the corpus. The April 2021 reading was 10% R; Rasmussen's 2022 wave puts 74% of Republicans in opposition. The floor for the *opposite* proposal — entrenching the supermajority requirement — is the mirror: 19% of Democrats wanted to keep it as is in January 2022, and 68% called it a bad thing in May 2021. **Both directions fail, on the same data.** That is the distinguishing feature: it is not that we lack a majority for the reform, it is that each of the two available reforms is opposed by roughly nine in ten of one party.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **fails, and this is the decisive finding.** Same pollster, same question, parties exchanged: Rasmussen recorded 48% opposed to elimination under a Democratic majority (Jan. 2022) and 44% opposed under a Republican majority (Aug. 2025), attributing the softening explicitly to the change in control. The roll calls are cleaner still: 48–52 in 2013 with Republicans as the 48, 48–52 in 2017 with Democrats as the 48. Advocacy data rotates the same way — Republican "positive impact" for elimination stood at 51% in March 2024, while the party held the Senate minority. A floor measured under one configuration is not the floor that survives the next election: the §11 trap in its purest form.
  - *Framing dependency* — **applies.** One framing clears with matching majorities (talking filibuster, 57% R / 54% D) and it is not abolition; it is a procedural burden-shift a Senate majority could adopt tomorrow and that no one proposes to constitutionalize.
  - *Staleness* — **applies.** Every independent reading with a party breakdown is from 2021–2022, under the opposite Senate configuration.
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — applies to the advocacy framing only: Navigator's 60% overall conceals no dramatic party split (51/49/71), but measures impact rather than support, while the independent favor/oppose question lands at 17% overall.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **untested.** No pollster has run a forward-looking version ("if the other party held the majority") against a current-configuration version — the single experiment this category most needs.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **cleared, in an unusual way.** There is no cross-party agreement on the abuse to begin with; each party describes the other's use of the rule as the problem. The gap between shared diagnosis and contested remedy never opens, because the diagnosis itself is contested.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem* — **open.** The case that it threatens the country's future: a chamber that cannot legislate on 60 votes pushes lawmaking into executive orders and reconciliation, so power migrates to the presidency and to procedures designed for budgets, and the branch the Constitution made primary atrophies — cloture motions rising from 39 to 286 per Congress is offered as the measure. The case that it does not: the filibuster is a rule the Senate writes for itself under Article I, §5, has been changed by simple majority twice in thirteen years, and its presence or absence changes which laws pass, not whether the republic has a functioning constitutional structure — a preference about outcomes wearing procedural clothes. Both arguments are made in good faith; the polling cannot adjudicate between them, and this memo takes no side.
  - *Symmetry* — **fails as ordinarily framed; the second decisive finding.** Abolition and entrenchment each advantage whichever party holds the majority, and every recorded vote and poll reading rotates with control. A rule written behind the veil of ignorance is one where the author does not know which side it binds next; here everyone knows exactly, and the 2013/2017 roll-call pair shows both parties acting on that knowledge in turn. The one framing that might survive is the inversion (u/smcstechtips: a supermajority to *kill* rather than to pass), which by construction does not favor the majority — and it has never been drafted or polled.
  - *Why-an-amendment* — **passes formally, and cuts both ways — which is the point.** The Senate's rulemaking power under Article I, §5 is exactly why an amendment is the only durable instrument: a rule change stands only until the next majority reverses it by the same simple-majority appeal, as 2013 and 2017 demonstrate. So *only* an amendment could permanently abolish the filibuster — and equally, only an amendment could permanently entrench it. But §5.3's four accepted answers point elsewhere: no court has struck a statute here, the Court has not exempted itself, Congress can self-bind on this and repeatedly has, and the text is not silent — Article I, §5 affirmatively commits the question to each chamber. An amendment is the only *permanent* instrument, not the only instrument that can reach the problem.

Only one framing clears the bar honestly, and it is the one we would not draft. The talking filibuster reaches matching majorities (57% R / 54% D) because it is not a threshold change at all — it restores a procedural cost on the objecting senator while leaving the 60-vote requirement intact. Constitutionalizing it would put floor-management mechanics into the Constitution, colliding with the drafting doctrine in §6.2 ("dollar amounts, thresholds, procedures, agency designs go to Congress"). It is also a rules change the Senate could adopt by majority vote — and did vote on, and reject, in January 2022.

The abuse-framing/remedy-framing gap that structures most candidate categories does not appear here, and the reason is instructive. In the FBI/DOJ and FCC candidates, both parties condemn the abuse and split on the fix. Here there is no shared abuse: the January 2022 Monmouth internals show Republicans at 57% supporting the filibuster's use to stop a specific bill and Democrats at 72% opposing that same use. What one party calls the abuse the other calls the protection — a category without a common premise, not one with a contested remedy.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

- **Evidence that would move it in.** A forward-looking, veil-of-ignorance question naming neither party and no pending bill, put to an independent pollster with published crosstabs — for example: *"Should the Constitution require that a bill can pass the Senate with a simple majority, no matter which party holds the majority — or should it allow the Senate to require more than a majority?"* — with a parallel form testing the inversion (*"a supermajority should be required to block a bill from receiving a vote"*). The threshold: **≥45% in both parties (Moderate) on the same design, holding across two waves fielded under different Senate majorities.** Two waves is not a formality; it is the only way to distinguish a preference from a regime effect, and this is the category where the corpus already knows the difference matters. A second route: a rules change drawing majorities of *both* caucuses — which has never happened in any of the three recorded appeals — would count as §4.2 evidence in a way no poll can.
- **Evidence that would move it out.** A re-ask under the current Republican majority returning Democratic elimination support above 60% and Republican support in the teens, mirroring 2021–22 with the parties exchanged. That would convert the regime-coding finding from a strong inference into a measured fact and make this a rejection on the **symmetry test** (§5.2), with the polling floor as corroboration. On present evidence that is the more likely outcome.
- **Recommendation: hold as a candidate**, gate: *a two-wave, forward-looking poll of a threshold rule showing ≥45% in both parties*. Rejection is defensible today — 8% R on the core proposal is the weakest reading in the corpus (§3) and symmetry fails as the category is ordinarily framed (§5.2). Three things argue for holding instead. The failing framings are all abolition-or-entrenchment, and the one structurally symmetric design — the inversion at §1.4 — has never been drafted or asked, so the category has not been measured in the form that could pass (§4). The existential-problem argument is genuinely open rather than settled against (§3). And holding costs nothing: this rides on the survey spec, and one well-designed question resolves it. If the founder prefers to move it now, the clean rejection is on **symmetry**, not polling — the numbers are a symptom, and the 2013/2017 roll-call pair is the finding.
