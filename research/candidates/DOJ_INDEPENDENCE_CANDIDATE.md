# FBI / DOJ Independence — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** 39–44% (Republicans) on the only structural remedy ever polled — judicial review of a special counsel's firing, Monmouth 2018 — Weak on the remedy while the *diagnosis* now runs 53% R / 55% I / 58% D (Marquette, May 2026); the category is held by the abuse-vs-remedy gap and by the fact that no pollster has asked about constitutional DOJ independence in eight years — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: a 2026 independent poll of the for-cause/removal remedy in forward-looking wording clearing 60% R).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The category asks whether the Constitution should insulate federal criminal law enforcement from presidential direction. Three variants appear in the record, and they are not interchangeable:

- **(a) Structural independence for the Department itself.** The founder's draft in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) ("Insulating Law Enforcement," Michael Hamilton, 2026-08-06): "establish an independent constitutional status for the Department of Justice and Special Prosecutors, stripping the president of unilateral authority to fire top law-enforcement officers without cause or Senate concurrence."
- **(b) Removal protection only.** A for-cause standard for the Attorney General, FBI Director, special counsels, and inspectors general, with dismissal reviewable by a panel of federal judges. The founder's two drafts in this family — "Codifying Special Counsel Protections" and "DOJ Firewall Laws" (binding public logs of White House communications about active investigations) — are **flagged in AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md as statutory, not constitutional**. Section 3 explains why that flag is now questionable in a way that cuts *toward* an amendment.
- **(c) Conduct rules rather than structural ones.** A prohibition on directing an investigation or prosecution of a named person for a purpose unrelated to law enforcement — a rule about what the President may order, not about who may be fired.

**Pipeline entry.** Founder seed candidate (Michael Hamilton), logged in [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) as "FBI/DOJ independence?" and in AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md under "Executive-accountability drafts." Independently reinvented in the public-thread mining: u/musashisamurai (44 points — "DOJ becomes an independent agency") and u/Journey2Jess (3 points — "move DOJ out from under the executive"), per [REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md).

**What an existing target already covers.** Nothing in the slate reaches prosecutorial direction. Target 7 (executive compliance with SCOTUS, [EXECUTIVE_COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md](../EXECUTIVE_COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md)) binds the executive to *court orders* — it says nothing about who the Attorney General answers to before a case reaches a court. Target 8 (pardon reform, [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../PARDON_RESEARCH.md)) constrains the disposal of prosecutions after the fact, not their initiation. The remainder to evaluate here is therefore the whole of it.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — the remedy**

- **Monmouth University Poll, Jan. 28–30, 2018 (n=806 adults).** "Would you support or oppose requiring the approval of a panel of federal judges before any special counsel could be fired by the president or attorney general?" — **62% support / 29% oppose — 44% R (47% oppose) / 65% I / 76% D** — [Monmouth](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/MonmouthPoll_US_020118_B/). The highest Republican number ever recorded on a structural insulation mechanism — and a plurality-oppose result.
- **Monmouth University Poll, April 26–30, 2018 (n=803 adults).** A softer, post-hoc version: "In the event a special counsel is fired, would you support or oppose allowing that counsel to file an appeal with a panel of federal judges who would then decide if there is just cause for the firing" — **57% support / 27% oppose — 39% R (43% oppose) / 58% I / 71% D** — [Monmouth](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_050118/). Weakening the mechanism from *ex ante* approval to *ex post* appeal lowered the Republican floor five points rather than raising it.
- **CBS News / SSRS, Nov. 15–18, 2018 (n=1,103 adults).** Whether Congress should pass legislation to protect the special counsel from being fired — **52% support — 33% R (67% opposed) / 75% D**, independents in the majority — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/americans-divide-along-partisan-lines-over-protecting-special-counsel-cbs-news-poll/).

These three are the entire national record on the remedy, all of it 2018 fieldwork.

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — the diagnosis**

- **Marquette Law School Poll, May 20–26, 2026 (n=1,001 adults, SSRS Opinion Panel).** Whether the Justice Department has filed unjustified criminal cases against the President's political opponents — **55% unjustified / 45% justified — 53% R / 55% I / 58% D**, which Marquette calls "little difference in partisan perception," unchanged overall since November 2025 — [Marquette release PDF](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32PressRelease_CourtIssues.pdf). In the same survey, the 2022–24 cases brought against the current President split sharply: **45% unjustified overall, 77% R calling them unjustified vs. 86% D calling them justified**. One sample, two prosecutions, a 5-point party gap on one and a 63-point gap on the other.
- **Marquette Law School Poll, Nov. 5–12, 2025 (n=1,052 adults).** Same DOJ item: **55% unjustified — 48% R / 47% I / 65% D**; the 2023–24 cases against the President: 42% unjustified — **77% R / 29% I / 10% D** — [Marquette](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/11/20/mlspsc29-court/). Between November and May the Republican share calling the *current* cases unjustified rose 48→53 while the Democratic share fell 65→58.
- **Quinnipiac University, Oct. 16–20, 2025 (n=1,327 RV, live-interviewer RDD).** "Do you think President Trump is using the U.S. Justice Department to file unjustified criminal charges against his political opponents, or do you think the charges against his political opponents are justified under the law?" — **52% unjustified / 38% justified — 6% R / 58% I / 92% D**; 83% of Republicans say the charges are justified — [Quinnipiac release PDF](https://poll.qu.edu/images/polling/us/us10222025_ubgr49.pdf). Same season, same apparent subject as Marquette's item, and a **47-point** gap in the Republican number — treated in §4 as a wording finding, not a contradiction to be averaged away.
- **Pew Research Center, Aug. 4–10, 2025 (n=3,554 adults; DOJ item asked of n=1,784).** Favorability of the Justice Department: **39% favorable / 46% unfavorable**, versus 43/44 in July 2024 and 49/41 in March 2023. By party, **51% of Republicans and Republican leaners now rate the DOJ favorably, up 18 points in a year; 28% of Democrats and Democratic leaners, down 27 points** — [Pew report PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/08/PP_2025.8.27_agency-favorability-report.pdf), [summary](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/08/27/republicans-views-of-justice-department-fbi-rebound-as-democrats-views-shift-more-negative/). Pew reports views of the FBI "shifted to a similar degree," and that in its pre-2020 telephone series partisan views of Justice "tended to become more positive when the presidency was held by their party." A 45-point party swap in twelve months with no change in the institution's design.

**Adjacent structural evidence (labeled: not this mechanism)**

- **Marquette, May 20–26, 2026** on statutory removal protections at multimember agencies (the question underlying *Trump v. Slaughter*): **73% say statutes can set removal requirements — 54% R / 72% I / 93% D**, up from 71% (39% R / 89% D) in November 2025 — same [release PDF](https://law.marquette.edu/assets/community/poll/MLSPSC32/MLSPSC32PressRelease_CourtIssues.pdf). A Republican majority for for-cause protection in general is a signal, but the question is about regulatory commissioners, not prosecutors, and §11 forbids attaching a general-battery number to a specific mechanism.
- **PRRI, Aug. 15–Sept. 8, 2025 (n=5,543).** "When decisions by Congress or the Supreme Court hold our country back, the president should be able to ignore them" — 16% agree / **78% disagree (24% R / 14% I / 10% D agree)** — [PRRI](https://prri.org/research/trumps-unprecedented-actions-deepen-asymmetric-divides/). Principle-level, not prosecutorial.

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **States United Democracy Center / YouGov, May 19–29, 2026 (n=1,537 adults).** Agree or disagree that the President "should be able to direct the Justice Department to pursue charges against his political opponents" — **60% disagree / 16% agree — 36% R disagree (30% agree) / 53% I / 86% D**; the release notes Republican agreement is down 6 points from May 2025 — [States United](https://statesunited.org/resources/justice-department-survey/). Advocacy-selected question, YouGov fieldwork. This is the corpus's current headline number and its 36% is the corpus's stated floor.
- **States United / YouGov, June 17–20, 2025 (n=1,515 adults).** Roughly 1 in 5 approve of the president using the Justice Department for political ends; the position "fail[s] to get majority support across Democrats, independents, and Republicans" — [States United](https://statesunited.org/resources/politicized-prosecutions-rule-of-law/) (no party percentages published per item).
- **Yale Youth Poll, March 9–23, 2026 (n=3,429 RV, under-35 oversample).** Whether the President should have authority to "direct the Department of Justice to investigate political opponents" — **58% disagree overall; 37% of Republicans agree**, under 10% of Democrats — [Yale](https://youthpoll.yale.edu/spring-2026-results).

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The remedy is effectively unpolled.** Searched 2026-08-18 and not found: any 2019–2026 national item from Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, or UMD PPC asking whether the Attorney General should be independent of the President, whether the FBI Director should be removable only for cause, or whether prosecutors should be protected from removal. AP-NORC's nearest item asks about unilateral action generally and never mentions prosecution (March 21–25, 2024 — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/few-adults-like-the-idea-of-unilateral-action-by-presidents/)).
- **The 36% floor in the corpus is a disagreement share on an advocacy question, not support for a remedy.** It measures how many Republicans reject a stated presidential power; nobody has asked them to endorse a rule limiting it.
- **Every diagnosis number uses named-incumbent wording.** Marquette, Quinnipiac, States United, and Yale all name a sitting officeholder. Under §11 ("same principle, different incumbent") none measures what a forward-looking rule would poll.
- **Marquette's own instrument contains the flip.** 77% of Republicans call the 2022–24 cases unjustified; 86% of Democrats call those same cases justified; on cases filed since, the parties are within five points of each other. The party saying the Department is being misused is the party out of power at the time of fieldwork, and both have occupied that position inside four years.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **39–44% (Republicans)** on judicial review of a special counsel's removal, Monmouth Jan. and April 2018 → **Weak** per §4.1, on eight-year-old data; the only other remedy reading is 33% R (CBS/SSRS, Nov. 2018). Stated more honestly: **Unpolled — no independent national poll since 2018 asks any version of the structural remedy with a party breakdown**, and the corpus's 36% is an advocacy-question disagreement share, not a remedy floor. The diagnosis would score Moderate on one pollster's wording (53% R, Marquette May 2026) and Weak on another's (6% R, Quinnipiac Oct. 2025).
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — the defining failure here.** Marquette finds 53% R / 58% D agreeing unjustified cases have been filed (May 2026); the three polls that ever asked about a preventive mechanism found 44%, 39%, and 33% R.
  - **Incumbent / regime coding — present and measured.** Pew's DOJ favorability moved 33→51 among Republicans and 55→28 among Democrats in twelve months with no change to the Department's structure. Marquette's 77/86 split on the prior-administration cases versus a 53/58 near-tie on the current ones is the same phenomenon inside one instrument. A floor measured now would not survive a change of administration.
  - **Framing dependency — severe and unresolved.** Quinnipiac (6% R) and Marquette (48–53% R) fielded ostensibly the same diagnosis in the same season and differ by roughly 47 points among Republicans. Until that gap is understood, no diagnosis number can carry a verdict.
  - **Staleness — total on the remedy side.** All remedy data is 2018.
  - **High-topline / low-floor — present on the advocacy questions.** 60% overall conceals 36% R (States United, May 2026); 58% conceals 37% R agreeing with the power (Yale, March 2026).
  - **Depth (trade-off collapse) — never tested.** No poll has asked whether an insulated Department is acceptable when the cost is named.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - **Existential-problem — passes, and more clearly than most of the slate.** The power to initiate a criminal prosecution against a named person is the sharpest coercive instrument the state holds, and the record now contains prosecutions that each party's identifiers call unjustified when aimed at their own side (Marquette, both waves). A design under which that instrument follows the presidency is a rule-of-law problem in the mission's sense, not a policy preference.
  - **Symmetry — passes by construction, strained in reception.** A for-cause standard binds every future president identically, and the abuse record pairs on its face: the 2022–24 cases (77% R call them unjustified) and the cases filed since (58% D call them unjustified) are two halves of one pairing from a single Marquette instrument. But each party currently reads "independence" as protection for the prosecutors it fears losing rather than restraint on the president it holds — which is what the 47-point wording gap and the Pew favorability swap register.
  - **Why-an-amendment — passes decisively, and this is the finding that changed since 2026-08-06.** The founder's drafts flag special-counsel protection and DOJ firewall rules as *statutory*; current doctrine says a statute may not be able to do this work. In [*Trump v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_e2pg.pdf), 603 U.S. 593 (July 1, 2024), the Court held that "[i]nvestigative and prosecutorial decisionmaking is 'the special province of the Executive Branch,'" that the Executive has "exclusive authority and absolute discretion" over which crimes to investigate, and that allegations investigations were "sham[s] or proposed for an improper purpose do not divest the President of exclusive authority over the investigative and prosecutorial functions of the Justice Department and its officials" — conduct within a "conclusive and preclusive" authority that "Congress cannot act on, and courts cannot examine." On removal, [*Trump v. Slaughter*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf), No. 25–332 (decided June 29, 2026), held the FTC's for-cause provision "contrary to the separation of powers" and, of the 1935 precedent that sustained such protections for ninety-one years, wrote: "If anything more is left of *Humphrey's*, we overrule it," leaving open only entities outside the President's "general administrative control" — it named the Federal Reserve, not prosecutors. Read with [*Seila Law LLC v. CFPB*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/19-7), 591 U.S. 197 (2020), and [*Myers v. United States*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/272/52), 272 U.S. 52 (1926) — which held Congress may not condition removal of an executive officer on Senate consent, the exact mechanism in draft (a) — the statutory route is now the *least* available option here. [*Morrison v. Olson*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/487/654), 487 U.S. 654 (1988), upholding for-cause removal of an independent counsel 7–1, is the contrary authority; its statute lapsed in 1999 and its reasoning is in tension with the 2020–2026 line. This is §5.3 answer #1 (courts struck the statutes) and #4 (the text is silent) at once.

A category can be held on §4 while passing all of §5, and that is this category's position: the constitutional case is the strongest part of the file and the evidence case is the thinnest.

## 4. Framings tested

| Framing | Best number | Source | Clears? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Judges must approve before a special counsel is fired | 62% (44R/65I/76D) | Monmouth, Jan. 2018 | No — Weak, plurality R opposition, stale |
| Fired counsel may appeal to a judicial panel | 57% (39R/58I/71D) | Monmouth, Apr. 2018 | No — softening the mechanism lowered the R floor |
| Congress should legislate protection for the special counsel | 52% (33R/75D) | CBS/SSRS, Nov. 2018 | No — Weak |
| President should not direct DOJ to charge opponents | 60% disagree (36R/53I/86D) | States United/YouGov, May 2026 (advocacy) | No — and it is a disagreement share, not remedy support |
| President should not direct DOJ to investigate opponents | 58% disagree (37% R agree) | Yale Youth Poll, Mar. 2026 | No — same shape, different house |
| DOJ has filed unjustified cases against opponents | 55% (53R/55I/58D) | Marquette, May 2026 | Diagnosis clears; not a remedy |
| Same subject, incumbent named as actor | 52% (6R/58I/92D) | Quinnipiac, Oct. 2025 | No — the 47-pt R gap against Marquette is the finding |
| Statutes may set for-cause removal requirements (agencies) | 73% (54R/72I/93D) | Marquette, May 2026 | Adjacent — clears, but is about commissioners |
| Constitutional independence for DOJ / AG removable only for cause or with Senate concurrence | — | never asked | Unknown |

Two things follow. First, **the abuse framing and the remedy framing are not the same proposal wearing different clothes**: Monmouth's 2018 pair shows that when the mechanism is named, Republican support sits at 39–44%. Second, **the diagnosis framing is not stable enough to build on**. Marquette asks whether "the Justice Department has filed unjustified criminal cases"; Quinnipiac asks whether the President "is using the U.S. Justice Department to file unjustified criminal charges." Naming the President as the actor moves Republicans from 53% to 6%. That is a wording finding of the same species as "fixed terms" versus "term limits" in the judiciary category but an order of magnitude larger, and it means any survey we commission must field both actor-named and institution-named versions or produce a number that cannot be defended.

Conspicuously absent is the one question a drafting decision would need: whether the Attorney General and FBI Director should be removable only for cause, asked forward-looking about *any* president. Eight years of pollster attention to this subject have produced no reading of it.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** An independent national poll (Pew, Marquette, AP-NORC, Monmouth, Economist/YouGov, or UMD PPC deliberative) with 2026-or-later fieldwork asking, in forward-looking both-parties wording: *"Should any future president be able to remove the Attorney General or FBI Director only for cause — such as misconduct or neglect of duty — rather than at will?"* and separately *"Should any future president be prohibited from directing the Justice Department to investigate or prosecute a specific person for reasons unrelated to law enforcement?"* Both fielded with an actor-named control arm to price the 47-point wording effect, and a costs-named follow-up ("even if that means a president cannot remove prosecutors voters disapprove of"). **Threshold: ≥60% Republican support on the conduct-rule version and ≥55% on the removal version, surviving the costs-named follow-up.** Corroborating evidence would count: a state constitutional amendment insulating a state attorney general adopted by a cross-party ballot majority, or a roll-call in either chamber on DOJ-independence legislation carrying majorities of both parties.

**Evidence that would move it out.** If the commissioned poll reproduces the 2018 pattern — Republican support for the removal remedy in the 30s or low 40s while the diagnosis polls in the 50s — the category fails §4.3's abuse-vs-remedy and framing-dependency tests on fresh data and should be rejected in the same terms as the self-pardon ban, with the path back written as a conduct-rule reframe. Second disqualifier: if forward-looking wording ("any future president") drops *both* parties below the incumbent-named readings, the category is regime-coded to the core and joins filibuster reform as structurally unstable.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate,** with the gate restated from "36R on remedy" to **"remedy unpolled since 2018; commission it."** Section 3 finds the why-an-amendment test passed more strongly here than in any other candidate examined — *Trump v. Slaughter* closed the statutory route in June 2026 and *Trump v. United States* placed prosecutorial direction inside an authority Congress "cannot act on" — and the existential-problem test passed on the mission's own terms, so this is not a category failing on principle. It fails, for now, on measurement: the only remedy data is eight years old and Weak (39–44% R), the newest headline number is an advocacy-worded disagreement share, and two independent pollsters differ by 47 points among Republicans on the diagnosis (§2, §4). Because §4 shows the abuse framing does not carry the remedy, promoting on the 55% diagnosis would be the exact trap §11 warns against. This belongs at the top of the commissioned-survey list rather than in the slate; if the removal question clears the threshold above, the natural home is a **companion article to Target 7** rather than a standalone amendment, since executive compliance with courts and executive non-direction of prosecutors are two halves of one rule-of-law proposition. Founder decides.
