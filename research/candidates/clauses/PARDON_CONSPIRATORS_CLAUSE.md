# No Clemency for the President's Own Conspirators — Clause Memo (Limited Presidential Pardon Reform)
*Held back in [research/PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../../PARDON_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a candidate clause of the Limited Presidential Pardon Reform amendment from 2026-08-18. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** Floor **43%** (Republicans, YouGov May 13–15, 2026, independent) — **Weak; the number moved down, not up, when the advocacy poll was replaced by an independent one** — what holds it is that every published number measures a relationship while the drafted clause measures a mechanism no one has asked about — **candidate clause (gate: forward-looking, both-parties wording on the coordination mechanism, not the "allies" relationship).**

## 1. The clause

The drafted text, from [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) "Held back":

> *"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon for an offense committed at the direction of, or in knowing coordination with, the President, or an offense of which the President was an intended beneficiary."*

**What it would add to the ratified article.** The article's four operative rules reach *who* the recipient is (a relative, §3), *what* the transaction was (something of value, §4), and *how* the grant was made (published with reasons, §1; named and specified, §2). None reaches the case where the recipient is no relation, took no money, and committed the offense **on the President's behalf**. This is the only drafted provision that turns on the President's relationship to the *crime* rather than to the *person*. Its lineage is the Cohen amendment, introduced every Congress since October 2017, currently [H.J.Res.13 (119th Cong., Jan. 9, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13); its founding-era pedigree is Edmund Randolph's September 15, 1787 motion to except treason — "The President may himself be guilty. The Traytors may be his own instruments" — which failed 2–8–1 ([Madison's notes](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_915.asp)).

**Why the parent memo held it back.** The associates ban sat at *exactly* 50% with Republicans — the Weak/Moderate boundary — on advocacy-commissioned polling only; and the clause carries the acute **review-standard trap**, since "at the direction of" requires a factfinder to determine what the President directed. The memo called it "the first provision to trade away"; [§6.4](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) records the same at the principle-level gate.

## 2. The evidence

**Best evidence — the independent replication the gate asked for, and it lands below the bar.** YouGov's standalone *Presidential Pardons* survey, fielded **May 13–15, 2026** among **1,127 U.S. adult citizens** ([toplines and crosstabs PDF](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results_P22Pm9s.pdf)), asked *"Do you think it is appropriate or inappropriate for a president to pardon…"* across six recipient categories. This is a non-advocacy instrument with published party crosstabs — precisely the evidence the parent memo's path back named. The "inappropriate" rows:

| Recipient (Q5) | Overall | Rep | Ind | Dem | Floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Themself | 76% | 64% | 76% | 88% | **64%** |
| Their campaign donors | 71% | 57% | 71% | 86% | **57%** |
| Their family members | 71% | 57% | 74% | 81% | **57%** |
| Their administration's officials | 63% | 44% | 65% | 81% | **44%** |
| Their staff | 61% | 42% | 65% | 79% | **42%** |
| **Their political allies** | **61%** | **43%** | **63%** | **79%** | **43%** |

*(Crosstab tables 5A–5F, "Party ID" panel.)*

The three items nearest this clause — administration officials, staff, political allies — cluster at **42–44% Republican**, i.e. **Weak** under [§4.1](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md). The advocacy reading the parent memo used, 69% overall with **50% R / 84% D** on pardons of "staff" ([YouGov for Protect Democracy, May–June 2024](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/survey-jan-6-pardon-opposition/); n=1,200 validated voters in 43 battleground districts, ±3.3), is now bracketed by an independent national sample returning 42% R on the same relationship. Both are YouGov; the differences are sample frame (battleground validated voters vs. national adult citizens), wording, and two years of fieldwork.

**Second, the mechanism has still never been asked.** Every number above measures a *relationship* — staff, officials, allies. The drafted clause turns instead on whether the offense was committed at the President's direction, a materially different proposition: the same instrument found only **20% "a great deal"** say a president should weigh a "personal relationship with the person convicted," against **47% "not at all"** (Q4). The public rejects personal-relationship clemency in the abstract while splitting on the named categories. No published survey asks about direction or coordination.

**Third, the anchors in the same instrument** (Q6, "support or oppose"; support = strongly + somewhat):

- Requiring pardon details including reasons be made public — **79%** (79R / 75I / 83D). Floor **75%** — the first independent number on the never-polled Principle 1.
- Prohibiting a president from pardoning themself — **72%** (61R / 71I / 85D). Floor **61%**.
- Prohibiting a president from pardoning a family member — **64%** (57R / 62I / 74D). Floor **57%**.

Within one instrument and one sample, the transparency rule and the self-pardon ban clear or approach the Strong bar while the associates items sit fifteen to twenty points lower. The clause is not marginally behind the package; it is last.

**Fourth, the live-conduct readings are worse and cannot supply a floor.** Monmouth (Dec. 5–10, 2024, n=1,006, ±3.9) asked about a Republican president's first-term pardons of associates and a relative by marriage: 29% approve / 56% disapprove, with disapproval at **32% among Republicans**, 58% independents, 74% Democrats. The mirror item in the same poll — a Democratic president's pardon of his own son — drew 32% approval overall and **65% among Democrats** ([report](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_121124/) for both item wordings). Each party's voters approved their own side's grant. These judge named grants by named presidents, not a rule; [§11](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) ("same principle, different incumbent") forbids reading a floor off them. They are cited here only as the symmetric abuse pair.

### Nuances & caveats

- **The two readings are not a clean 50-vs-43 comparison.** Protect Democracy's item is a *battleground-district validated-voter* sample; the May 2026 item is a *national adult-citizen* sample. Neither is wrong — they are different universes, both YouGov, two years apart. The honest statement is that the clause has two readings, 50% R and 42–44% R, and the non-advocacy one is lower.
- **"Not sure" is doing real work.** On the political-allies item, 21% of respondents and 21% of Republicans answered "not sure" — the highest uncertainty of the six categories. A forced-choice or mechanism-specific framing would likely move it, so 43% should not be read as a ceiling.
- **The review-standard trap is unchanged, and is the substance.** A court applying "at the direction of, or in knowing coordination with" must find facts about presidential intent — the defect [§6.2](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) cites for preferring categorical exclusions over motive tests. *Trump v. United States* (2024) sharpens it: footnote 3 bars a prosecutor from admitting "testimony or private records of the President or his advisers probing the official act itself" ([opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_e2pg.pdf)). A clause enforceable only by proving what the President directed, in a legal environment restricting evidence of what the President directed, has an enforcement gap written in before ratification.
- **A drafting answer exists and is untested.** The second limb — "an offense of which the President was an intended beneficiary" — is closer to a transaction test than a motive test and could be severed and drafted alone. It has never been drafted separately or asked about.
- **Comparative practice supports the principle but supplies no floor.** Brazil's Supreme Federal Tribunal annulled a president's pardon of a political ally **8–2** on May 10, 2023 for "desvio de finalidade" ([Brasil de Fato](https://www.brasildefato.com.br/2023/05/10/stf-cancela-perdao-da-pena-que-bolsonaro-deu-a-ex-deputado-federal-daniel-silveira/)); peer democracies make self-protective clemency structurally impossible or voidable. None of that is a US bipartisan floor.

## 3. The gate result

**Floor: 43%** (Republicans, political-allies item, YouGov May 2026) — **Weak** under [§4.1](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md), with the two adjacent items at 44% and 42%. The gate the parent memo set — "independent polling above the bar" — has now been run, and the clause failed it. Under the method as written, a Weak floor "has been disqualifying every time it appeared."

**Failure modes ([§4.3](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)):**

- **High-topline / low-floor — failed.** 61% overall against a 43% Republican floor: an 18-point topline-to-floor gap and a 36-point party gap. The canonical pattern the metric exists to catch.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — failed, in an unusual direction.** Elsewhere both parties condemn the abuse and split on the remedy. Here the *remedy* framings poll better than the *relationship* framings: prohibiting self-pardons draws 61% R as a policy, while calling a pardon of the president's allies "inappropriate" draws 43% R. The relationship language, not the rule language, is where Republicans fall away.
- **Incumbent / regime coding — at risk, undetermined.** The May 2026 fieldwork sits inside one administration's active use of the power, and the Monmouth pair shows each party's voters approving their own president's grants. Forward-looking wording has never been fielded here, so the effect's size is unknown — it is the likeliest explanation for the 2024-to-2026 gap and the first thing a new instrument must control for.
- **Framing dependency — untested and material.** Six recipient framings exist; the drafted mechanism has none. A 22-point spread between "themself" (64% R) and "their staff" (42% R) inside one instrument is direct evidence this family of questions is framing-sensitive.
- **Depth — untested.** No survey has put the counterargument ("a court would have to decide what the president directed") to respondents.
- **Staleness — cleared.** The decisive reading is three months old.

**Non-polling tests ([§5](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)):**

- **Why-an-amendment — passes, as the parent's.** The clause inherits the article's answer: the pardon power is constitutional text, and *Trump v. United States* named it among the "conclusive and preclusive" powers, so a statute can regulate conduct around a pardon but cannot make one void. Bauer and Goldsmith's contrary view — that Congress may criminalize pardons used "in a corrupt scheme to obstruct a judicial proceeding" ([Lawfare, Feb. 26, 2020](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/how-reform-pardon-power)) — applies to this clause more forcefully than to the others, since the conduct it targets is closest to obstruction. If the clause is ever dropped, that is the honest place to send it.
- **Symmetry — passes on construction, at risk on packaging.** The rule binds every future president and reaches conduct available to either party. But its only legislative vehicle, the Cohen amendment, has been a single-caucus artifact since 2017 with no hearing, no markup, and no significant cross-party cosponsors. A symmetric pairing exists — one president's grants to associates, another's to his own family, measured in the same Monmouth instrument — but it is not the pairing the clause is publicly associated with.
- **Existential-problem — passes.** A president who can immunize those who commit crimes at his direction holds, in effect, a personal police power. That is Randolph's objection, not a good-government nicety.

## 4. Poll question

Fielded by an independent pollster in forward-looking, both-parties wording, in the same instrument as the self-pardon item (the corpus's designated first commissioned poll) so the two are comparable within respondents. The core item asks about the **mechanism**, which no published survey has done, not the relationship, which six published items already measure.

**Core wording (verbatim):**

> *"Suppose a future president — of either party — asks someone to commit a federal crime on the president's behalf, and that person is later convicted of it. Should the president be allowed to pardon that person, or should the Constitution prohibit a president from pardoning someone for a crime the president directed them to commit?"*

**Follow-ups, in order:**

1. *Costs named (the depth test):* "Supporters of this rule say no president should be able to protect people who broke the law on his orders. Opponents say a judge or jury would have to decide what a president secretly directed, which puts courts in the middle of the president's decisions and could tie up ordinary pardons in lawsuits. Having heard both, do you support or oppose the rule?"
2. *Severed second limb (the transaction-test variant):* "Should the Constitution prohibit a president from granting a pardon when the president is a direct beneficiary of the offense being pardoned?"
3. *Relationship replication, for comparability with the existing series:* "Do you think it is appropriate or inappropriate for a president to pardon their political allies?" — the verbatim YouGov May 2026 item, carried unchanged as the anchor.
4. *Symmetry probe:* the same core item asked twice in split samples, once naming no president and once as "including presidents you voted for," to size the regime-coding effect the 2024→2026 movement suggests.

**Threshold: ≥60% in both parties on the core item, holding at ≥55% in both parties after the costs-named follow-up.** Anything below 60% but at or above 45% in both parties keeps the clause a candidate and moves the drafting effort to the severed second limb (follow-up 2), which must clear the same ≥60% bar on its own before it is drafted. A repeat Weak reading — either party below 45% on both the core item and the severed limb — moves the clause from candidate to rejected, with the path back being the statutory route Bauer and Goldsmith describe rather than a re-poll.

## 5. Recommendation

**Keep as a candidate clause, but record that its gate has been run once and failed, and narrow what is being gated.** The parent memo's condition was "independent polling above the bar." Independent polling now exists and puts the Republican floor at 42–44% across three adjacent framings — below the advocacy reading, not above it — which under the method's own rule is disqualifying. This is not yet a rejection only because every published number measures a *relationship* while the drafted clause measures a *mechanism*, and the corpus has never asked the question the clause actually poses. That single unasked question is now the whole gate.

Two operational consequences. The parent memo's "first provision to trade away" should be treated as settled rather than provisional — nothing in the new evidence argues for drafting it in, and the same instrument shows the transparency and self-pardon provisions clearing bars this clause is nowhere near. And if a commissioned poll returns another Weak floor on the mechanism wording, move it to a rejected clause with an explicit path to companion legislation rather than leaving it a candidate whose gate has failed twice.
