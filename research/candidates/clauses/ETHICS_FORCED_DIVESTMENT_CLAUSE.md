# Forced Sale of Existing Holdings — Clause Memo (Ethics)

*Held back in [research/ETHICS_RESEARCH.md](../../ETHICS_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a rejected clause (69% "goes too far", Bloomberg 2016, no party split — verify; substantiate; path back or none) clause of the Ethics amendment from 2026-08-18. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled by party — the one direct measurement is a 69/26 topline against the mechanism with no party breakdown published — the safe harbor is what carries the parent amendment's 86–87% floor, and removing it converts an anti-self-dealing rule into a wealth test — **rejected clause (test: a both-parties, forward-looking favor/oppose on sale-versus-blind-trust clearing ≥60% in both parties, which no existing evidence suggests is reachable).**

## 1. The clause

There is no drafted text for this clause: it is defined by a subtraction. Section 1 of the ethics article as it stands reads:

> **Section 1.** No person holding the office of President or Vice President, no Senator or Representative, and no Justice or judge of the United States (in this article, "covered officers") shall, during their continuance in office, purchase, sell, or hold any individual security, digital asset, or contract of speculation whose value their official conduct may affect, **other than through a diversified fund or qualified blind trust as Congress shall by law provide.**

The clause under consideration would strike the bolded safe harbor and require covered officers to *sell* covered holdings outright, converting the proceeds into neutral assets. Section 6 gives Section 1 a one-year effective date and a one-year compliance window for anyone later assuming a covered office — the article's "divestment window." Under the present text an officer satisfies it by sale **or** by moving holdings into a blind trust or diversified fund; the forced-sale variant would close the second route.

The parent memo held it back at the drafting stage rather than the gate stage. [ETHICS_RESEARCH.md](../../ETHICS_RESEARCH.md) Principle 1 records it as the **wealth-penalty framing trap**: forced sale "polled as 'goes too far' for 69% in 2016 … the blind-trust/diversified-fund safe harbor is what separates 'no self-dealing' (wildly popular) from 'punish successful people for serving' (a real attack line, and a genuine deterrent to non-wealthy candidates who own a business). Keep the safe harbor in the constitutional text, not just the implementing statute." The safe harbor is the one piece of Section 1 mechanics the corpus insists on constitutionalizing rather than delegating.

## 2. The evidence

**The one direct measurement.** Bloomberg News National Poll, conducted by Selzer & Co., **December 2–5, 2016**, n=999 U.S. adults, margin of error ±3.1 points, weighted by age, race, and education. The item, verbatim from the published topline:

> "Donald Trump has said he plans to take himself completely out of business operations as part of becoming president. His adult children may be involved both in running the business and in serving as advisers to him as president. Do you think Donald Trump should sell all his businesses so that neither he nor his family could potentially profit from actions he takes as president, or does that go too far?"
> **26 Should sell businesses · 69 That goes too far · 5 Not sure**

The preceding item in the same instrument is the contrast that makes the finding: "Do you think Donald Trump does or does not need to choose between being president and being a businessman, to avoid the appearance of a conflict?" — **67 does need to choose / 27 does not / 6 not sure**. The same respondents, in the same call, endorsed the *principle* of separation by 67% and rejected the *sale mechanism* by 69%. That is a 40-point gap between problem and remedy inside a single survey, and it is the cleanest instance of the abuse-vs-remedy pattern (§4.3) anywhere in the ethics corpus.

**No party breakdown exists.** The published topline reports party identification as a sample-composition item (27 Republican / 11 lean R / 19 totally independent / 8 lean D / 29 Democrat / 2 other / 4 refused) but publishes **no crosstab for the divestment question**; neither NPR's nor CBS's coverage reports one. A search conducted 2026-08-18 for any independent national poll — Marquette, Pew, AP-NORC, Gallup, YouGov — putting forced sale, or sale-versus-blind-trust, to respondents with party breakdowns since 2016 returned nothing. **That is the finding: the mechanism has been measured once, ten years ago, in person-named wording, with no party split published, and never since.**

**What the safe-harbor version polls.** The clause matters only in contrast to what it would replace, and the replacement is the strongest-polling proposal in the platform. The trading ban with the blind-trust/diversified-fund route polls **86–87% (87R / 88–90D)**, and extending it upward to the President and Vice President polls **87% (87R / 90D)** — [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) §3–4. The nearest live proxy with a published party split is the crypto self-dealing item: barring officials and their families from profiting from crypto in office draws **71% (67R)**, holding at **66% (55R)** after a counterargument — [Progressive Policy Institute, 2025](https://www.progressivepolicy.org/as-trump-pockets-1-2-billion-from-crypto-new-poll-finds-voters-want-the-industry-and-self-dealing-officials-reined-in/) *(center-left advocacy)*. Both are prohibitions on *conduct in office*. Neither asks anyone to liquidate what they built before it.

**Legislative and comparative evidence is the strongest argument the clause has.** The Senate HONEST Act (S. 1498), ordered reported by Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs July 30, 2025 and placed on the calendar December 10, 2025, is stricter than the amendment: as reported it *prohibits* qualified blind trusts and requires sitting Members to divest before their next term ([CRS TE10119](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/TE/PDF/TE10119/TE10119.1.pdf), Table 1), and it advanced with sponsors from both parties. Canada's Conflict of Interest Act requires reporting public office holders to divest controlled assets within 120 days by sale *or* blind trust ([s. 27](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-36.65/section-27.html)), and a 2025 Commons committee recommended full divestment by sale for the Prime Minister, "since placement in a blind trust does not constitute true divestment" ([ETHI Committee](https://www.ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/45-1/ETHI/report-5/page-24)). The mechanism is neither unworkable nor unprecedented among peers — but institutional action is adjacent evidence, not a bipartisan floor.

### Nuances & caveats

- **The 2016 item is person-named and business-specific, which cuts both ways.** §11 of the method warns that person-coded framings distort, and a question naming one officeholder's family businesses measures partly what respondents thought of that officeholder. But the distortion runs toward *sympathy for the mechanism among his opponents*, and the mechanism still lost 69–26 — so the topline is likely a ceiling, not a floor, for a neutral rewording. That inference is a judgment, not a measurement.
- **Businesses are not portfolios.** The Bloomberg item asks about selling operating companies; Section 1 reaches individual securities, digital assets, and speculative contracts. Selling an index-fund holding and selling a family firm are different asks, and a forced-sale rule limited to *securities* has never been polled at all. This is the one live distinction a future question could exploit, and the reason this memo records a test rather than a flat closure.
- **The HONEST Act's blind-trust prohibition is a legislative choice, not measured opinion.** No poll accompanies it; a committee vote is adjacent evidence, not a bipartisan floor.
- **Stock-ban support is format-sensitive** — ~63% in quick polls, 86–87% after deliberation; the word "ban" costs about 5 points ([Economist/YouGov, May 2026](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/54851-most-americans-think-congressional-corruption-is-widespread-may-22-26-2026-economist-yougov-poll)). A mechanism whose only measurement is a one-shot phone item was measured at the format that flatters it least — and it still lost badly.

## 3. The gate result

**Floor: none — Unpolled by party.** No published party breakdown exists for the only direct measurement, so §4.1's metric cannot be computed. The topline is **26% for the mechanism**, which is below the Weak threshold at the topline level, and a floor is by construction no higher than a topline.

**Failure modes (§4.3):**

- *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **failed, within a single instrument.** 67% endorse the separation principle; 69% reject the sale remedy. The pattern the gate exists to catch, measured without the usual confound of different pollsters or months.
- *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **failed by construction.** The question's framing *is* the cost ("does that go too far"), and support sat at 26% with the cost named. Compare balanced budget: 69–80% abstract, 31% once entitlement cuts were specified.
- *Framing dependency* — **at risk, and the only place a path exists.** The framing the foundation would actually draft — securities and speculative contracts rather than operating businesses, forward-looking, no officeholder named — has never been asked. The rule is that framing dependency is disqualifying unless *one honest framing clears the bar and is the framing we would draft*; here there is a candidate framing and no evidence it clears.
- *Staleness* — **failed.** Ten years old, fielded at a peak-salience moment in a transition news cycle.
- *Incumbent / regime coding* — **unmeasurable and probably severe.** Person-named wording during a transition is regime coding by definition; §11's trap applies directly.
- *High-topline / low-floor* — **not applicable;** there is no high topline to hide a split behind. *Cleared:* none.

**Non-polling tests (§5):**

- *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **the parent's answer carries it, as is usual for a clause.** §208 exempts exactly these offices and Congress cannot durably self-bind, so the *duty* belongs in the Constitution. But sale-versus-trust is the kind of mechanic §6.2 assigns to Congress "by law" — the corpus already lists blind-trust mechanics and divestment clocks under **inherently statutory**. The safe harbor is in constitutional text not because mechanics belong there, but because it is doing ratification work: a guarantee about what the rule is not.
- *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes on its face, fails in perception.** The rule binds every future officeholder of both parties equally and is prospective. But it falls unequally by wealth composition rather than by party — an officeholder whose assets are a diversified portfolio complies at no cost; one whose main asset is a family firm may be unable to comply at any acceptable price. That asymmetry is not partisan, and it is the substance behind the wealth-penalty objection.
- *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **the parent clears it; this clause adds little.** What forced sale adds is closure of the residual case where an officer knows what went into the trust — real, and the reason Canada's committee recommended sale for its chief executive, but incremental rather than existential on its own.
- *Blast radius (§6.3)* — **the largest of any ethics clause.** Under a forced-sale rule the compliance window becomes a compelled-liquidation clock on a known date for a known set of people, with tax consequences the Constitution cannot address and market consequences for closely held assets. The corpus's own statutory list already anticipates "capital-gains rollover/tax-deferral relief for compelled divestment" — machinery a constitutional forced-sale rule would demand and could not supply.

## 4. Path back

**A path exists, narrowly, and it is a test the clause is unlikely to pass.** It is written out so the rejection is falsifiable rather than assumed, per §3's rule that rejections are revisable.

The question to field — forward-looking, both-parties, mechanism-specific, naming no officeholder, asked *after* the parent trading-ban anchor so the contrast is measured within respondents:

> "Federal officials — the President, Vice President, members of Congress, and federal judges — would be barred from owning individual stocks and similar investments while in office. There are two ways to do that. **One:** they must sell those investments and may hold only broad index funds, government bonds, or similar diversified holdings. **Two:** they may either sell, or place the investments in a blind trust managed by an independent trustee who can buy and sell without telling them what they own. Which comes closer to your view — should the rule require them to sell, or should the blind-trust option also be allowed?"

**Costs-named follow-up**, asked of everyone:

> "Supporters of requiring sale say a blind trust is not truly blind, because an official still knows roughly what they put in it. Supporters of the blind-trust option say requiring sale forces people to break up businesses they built before taking office, and would keep some people from serving at all. Having heard both, which rule would you favor?"

And, to isolate the distinction the 2016 item conflated:

> "Should this rule apply the same way to someone whose investments are stocks and funds, and to someone whose main asset is a business they own and operate?"

**Threshold: ≥60% in both parties for the sale-only option, holding above 60% in both parties after the costs-named follow-up.** Anything less is a directional finding, not a mandate to strike a safe harbor the 86–87% version depends on. A Moderate result (45–59% in both parties) would **not** reopen the clause; at most it would justify a narrower rewrite reaching *securities and speculative contracts only* while leaving operating businesses to the trust route — a different clause, needing its own question. Under 45% in either party closes the file.

Two design requirements, both from the corpus's traps: **name no officeholder** (§11), and **carry the parent trading-ban item in the same instrument** as the anchor, so the gap between the 86–87% version and this one is measured within respondents rather than across surveys — the measurement the 2016 instrument accidentally produced and no one has reproduced since.

## 5. Recommendation

**Rejected clause.** The one direct measurement puts the mechanism at 26% against 69% saying it goes too far — from the same respondents who endorsed the underlying principle by 67%, the abuse-vs-remedy and depth tests failing together in one instrument. No party breakdown was published then and no independent pollster has asked since, so there is no floor to report and no basis for believing one exists above the bar.

The clause should stay out for a reason beyond its own number: the safe harbor is load-bearing, not a concession. It is what makes Section 1 a rule against self-dealing rather than a condition on who may serve, and the corpus keeps it in constitutional text rather than statute so that guarantee cannot be traded away later. Removing it would put the platform's strongest measured proposal — 86–87%, the smallest partisan gap in the corpus — at risk to close a residual case the trust route already mostly closes.

Carry it in the registry as `status: rejected, kind: clause` with the §4 question attached, and field that question only if it rides free in an ethics instrument already being commissioned; it is not worth a survey of its own. If the founder prefers the registry to hold only live gates, this entry is the clearest candidate for dropping to page prose — §E.1's open question, on which this memo takes no position beyond noting the path back is real but narrow.
