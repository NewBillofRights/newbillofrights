# A Constitutional Right to Deletion — Clause Memo (Privacy)

*Held back in [research/PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md](../../PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a rejected clause unless the evidence surprises (the parent memo gives no return condition; substantiate the rejection or name a path back) clause of the Privacy amendment from 2026-08-18. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

**Status in one line:** 30% floor on the government-records half of the idea (Pew 2019) — the doctrinal objection is now corroborated by the one party-split number that exists — what holds it is that deletion polls as a *consumer* right and collapses as a *records* right, exactly along the line the First Amendment already draws — **rejected clause (test: an independent both-parties poll on a narrowed, records-excluding deletion right clearing 60% in both parties, fielded alongside the privacy instrument).**

## 1. The clause

The parent memo's abstraction candidate 3 was drafted with deletion delegated rather than granted. The **adopted** text, now Section 3 of the article, reads:

> *"Every person has the right to know what personal information the government holds about them and to obtain correction of material inaccuracies. Personal information acquired by government for one purpose may not be used for an unrelated purpose, except as provided by law consistent with this article. Congress and the States may by law secure rights of access, correction, and **deletion** of personal information held by others."*

The held-back clause is the counterfactual: deletion as a **right** on the same footing as access and correction, running against government as well as private holders, rather than an enumerated legislative power exercised against private holders only. No separate text was drafted for it — the parent describes it as the rejected breadth of candidate 3, so this memo prices the idea rather than a sentence.

**What it would add to the ratified article.** Three things the article does not now do: a self-executing individual claim to erasure, enforceable under Section 6 without waiting for legislation; that claim running against *government-held* records, where the article now gives only access and correction; and removal of Congress's discretion over scope, since a constitutional right sets a floor no statute may narrow.

**Why the parent memo held it back.** Not on a number. The objection is doctrinal and archival — the **right-to-be-forgotten trap**: a constitutional deletion right "applied to public records, court records, criminal history, credit files, or journalism becomes an EU-style right-to-be-forgotten fight that loses to the First Amendment — and deleting government records undermines accountability and archives." *Google Spain* (CJEU 2014) permits compelled de-listing of lawfully published truthful material; U.S. doctrine forbids exactly that, under *Cox Broadcasting v. Cohn* (1975), *Smith v. Daily Mail* (1979), and *Florida Star v. B.J.F.* (1989). The article's own savings clause (Section 7 — "Nothing in this article shall abridge the freedom of speech or of the press") was written to disarm that attack pre-emptively, so a deletion *right* would sit in tension with the article's own text.

## 2. The evidence

**Best first — and it is a genuine surprise, in both directions.** The corpus recorded no polling on deletion. There is some, and it is more useful than the doctrinal argument alone.

- **Pew Research Center, June 2019 (n=4,272 U.S. adults), on whether all Americans should have the right to have material removed from public online search results — the closest fielded measurement of this clause that exists.** Support is not one number; it is a gradient that tracks the doctrinal line almost exactly:
  - potentially embarrassing photos and videos: **85%**
  - employment history or work records: **67%**
  - negative media coverage: **56%**
  - data collected by law enforcement, such as criminal records or mugshots: **39% overall — 41% D / 30% R**
  ([Pew, Jan 27, 2020 write-up of June 2019 fieldwork](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/01/27/most-americans-support-right-to-have-some-personal-info-removed-from-online-searches/))
- **Same instrument, on deletion by the holder rather than de-listing:** personal financial data held by a tax preparer, **79% — 83% R / 77% D**; personal medical data held by a health care provider, **69%** (no party breakdown published). Same source.
- **YouGov, Jan 26–30, 2023 (n=1,000 U.S. adult citizens):** **79%** say Americans should have a right to delete personal data collected online; on requiring companies to give users the ability to delete their personal data, **89% R / 77% D** — one of the few privacy items where the Republican number is the higher one. ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/45147-us-tech-regulation-receives-overwhelming-support))
- **Context anchor already in the corpus:** more regulation of what companies do with personal data, **72% — 68% R / 78% D** ([Pew, May 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/10/18/how-americans-view-data-privacy/)); and deletion is the one substantive core appearing in every comprehensive federal privacy bill from both parties, 2018–2026 ([ADPPA H.R. 8152](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/8152); [APRA H.R. 8818](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/8818); [SECURE Data Act H.R. 8413](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8413/text)). The bipartisan DELETE Act builds a federal deletion mechanism against data brokers by statute ([S. 1287, 119th](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1287/text)).

**Nuances & caveats.**

- These items measure a search-engine de-listing right and a private-holder erasure right, not constitutional text. They are adjacent evidence and are labeled as such (§11); none can carry a verdict alone.
- **The gradient is the finding, not any single number.** Support falls 46 points as the target moves from a person's own embarrassing content toward the public record, with the steepest drops at press coverage (56%) and government records (39%). That is the First Amendment line and the archival line, measured — the doctrinal objection and the public's intuition agree.
- **The one published party split in the series is on the government-records half, and it is the low one:** 30% R / 41% D. The commercial half is Strong and, unusually, Republican-led.
- The YouGov 89R/77D item asks about a *statutory requirement on companies* — which Section 3's third sentence already authorizes. It is evidence for the adopted drafting, not for the held-back clause.
- **Staleness:** June 2019 and January 2023 fieldwork. Neither sits at a peak-salience moment, so this is a caution rather than a disqualifier. All three sources are independent pollsters; none is advocacy-commissioned.

## 3. The gate result

- **Floor: 30%** (Republicans, Pew June 2019, deletion applied to law-enforcement records). **Verdict: Weak** on the piece that distinguishes this clause from the adopted text. The commercial piece is Strong (77%) — but that piece is not held back; it is Section 3's third sentence.
- **The decisive structural fact:** the clause is a bundle, and it splits at exactly the seam the article already cuts along. Everything polling above 60% is already in the amendment as a delegated legislative power; everything the held-back clause would newly add polls at 39% overall and 30% among Republicans. There is no version of "constitutionalize deletion" that adds something to the article *and* clears the bar on present evidence.

**Failure modes (§4.3).**

- *High-topline / low-floor* — **fails.** A 79% headline conceals a 30% floor the moment the right reaches government records. The canonical shape the gate exists to catch.
- *Blast radius (§6.3)* — **fails; the primary objection.** Read literally the right reaches court files, criminal history, vital records, agency archives, and credit reporting. Scoping rescued the acquisition rule; here it hollows the clause out. A deletion right narrowed to exclude public records, journalism, and archives is *nearly* the commercial suite the article already delegates — not entirely, since a non-trivial residue survives the carve-outs: non-public government holdings such as investigative and intelligence files, erroneous agency records, and unarchived tracking data. That residue is the honest limit of this objection. But it is thin as a warrant for constitutional text, because the ratified article already reaches most of it by another route — Section 3 grants access and correction against government, and its purpose-limitation sentence bars reuse of government-held data for unrelated purposes — and it is also the part that polls worst (30% R). The blast-radius test therefore does not defeat the clause on its own; it removes most of the clause's marginal value, and the Weak floor does the rest.
- *Framing dependency* — **fails.** An honest framing clears the bar (89R/77D on company deletion tools), but §4.3 asks whether it is "the framing we would actually draft." It is not, and it is already covered.
- *Depth* — **untested, likely to fail.** No survey has named the cost; support already falls 46 points unprompted.
- *Incumbent coding* and *abuse-vs-remedy* — **not the pattern.** Deletion is not regime-coded, and both parties agree on the remedy, splitting only on reach.

**Non-polling tests (§5).** A clause normally inherits the parent's why-an-amendment answer, and the parent's is strong. **This is the one place in the article where it does not transfer.** Deletion against private holders is the single provision both parties have repeatedly agreed on in bill text; those bills died on preemption and the private right of action, not on deletion, and Section 3's third sentence plus Section 7's enforcement power already supply the authority. *Symmetry (§5.2)* passes cleanly — deletion binds every future government and company. *Existential-problem (§5.1)* is weak: erasure of already-collected data is remediation layered on the purpose-limitation sentence that does the durable work.

## 4. Path back

Not a poll question in the ordinary sense — the ordinary question returns 79% and means nothing (§3). A path back exists and is narrow: solve the doctrinal problem in drafting first, then measure the narrowed thing.

**Step 1 — the drafting condition.** Text granting a deletion right while expressly excluding information lawfully published; records of judicial and legislative proceedings; records required by law to be preserved for archival, historical, or statistical purposes; and information whose retention is necessary to a legal obligation or claim. If that can be written without swallowing itself, the clause returns to the polling stage. If the exclusions leave only the commercial suite, it folds into Section 3's third sentence — where it already is.

**Step 2 — the question, if step 1 succeeds.** Independent pollster, same instrument as the privacy package, forward-looking and both-parties:

> *"Would you favor or oppose adding to the Constitution a right for every person to require that personal information about them be deleted — a right any person could enforce in court against companies and against government agencies alike? The right would not reach anything lawfully published by the press, court and legislative records, or records the law requires to be kept for historical or statistical purposes."*

**Costs-named follow-up (required — depth is the untested mode):**

> *"Supporters say people should be able to make organizations erase data collected about them without their knowledge. Opponents say a constitutional deletion right would let people erase government records that keep officials accountable, and would be fought in court for decades over whether it reaches news archives. Knowing both arguments, do you favor or oppose it?"*

**Split-sample companion:** the identical item with "and against government agencies alike" removed, to measure the government-records increment directly rather than inferring it from the 2019 proxy.

**Threshold: ≥60% in both parties on the costs-named item, and the government-inclusive version within 10 points of the companion.** The second condition matters as much as the first — if the government increment costs more than 10 points, the clause is being carried by the commercial half the article already has. Below 60% in either party, or a repeat of the 2019 gradient, closes it permanently.

## 5. Recommendation

**Rejected clause, with the narrow path back in §4 — the rejection is now substantiated by evidence rather than doctrine alone.** The only party-split number on the piece this clause would add is 30% R / 41% D, Weak in both parties, while every number above the bar describes the commercial deletion suite the ratified article already makes an express legislative power. It fails the high-topline/low-floor and blast-radius tests at once, and it is the one clause whose why-an-amendment answer does not transfer from the parent. Do not draft it; keep the idea alive only as the two-step condition in §4, and close the entry if the exclusions cannot be written without collapsing the right into what the article already delegates.

## Review Additions (2026-08-18)

*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

**Extends §4 Step 1 (the drafting condition) and §3 (blast radius).**

- **A statutory deletion right with carve-outs of this shape already exists and operates: California Civil Code § 1798.105 (CCPA, as amended by Proposition 24, operative January 1, 2023).** Subdivision (a) grants a consumer the right to require a business to delete personal information it collected from the consumer; subdivision (d) then lists eight grounds on which the business need not comply, including to "exercise free speech, ensure the right of another consumer to exercise that consumer's right of free speech, or exercise another right provided for by law" (d)(4); to "engage in public or peer-reviewed scientific, historical, or statistical research" (d)(6); and to "comply with a legal obligation" (d)(8) ([Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.105](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.105)). **This resolves Step 1 in the affirmative on the narrow question of drafting feasibility:** exclusions for speech, research and archives, and legal-retention obligations can be written without the right swallowing itself, and have been enforced at scale for three years. §4's "If that can be written without swallowing itself" should be read as answered for private holders.
- **The same statute confirms, rather than rebuts, the memo's blast-radius conclusion — and it does so on the point the reviewer raised.** California achieved workability by *narrowing both the holder and the record*. The duty runs only against a "business," defined as an entity "organized or operated for the profit or financial benefit of its shareholders or other owners" meeting revenue or volume thresholds — government agencies are outside the Act entirely — and "personal information" is defined to exclude "publicly available information or lawfully obtained, truthful information that is a matter of public concern," with "publicly available" expressly including "information that is lawfully made available from federal, state, or local government records" ([Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(d), (v)(2)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.140)). So the operational model is precisely the commercial suite Section 3's third sentence already authorizes; it is not a model for a deletion right running against government.
- **Reviewer's counterpoint, recorded and partly accepted.** The reviewer argued the memo conflates the type of record with the holder, and that a deletion right against government excluding public archives would still reach non-public government data — internal surveillance holdings, erroneous agency files, unarchived tracking data. That distinction is sound as a matter of logic and the memo's §3 wording overstated it; the sentence has been softened accordingly. It does not change the verdict, for two reasons the evidence supports: the only fielded party-split reading of a deletion right over government-held law-enforcement records is 39% overall / **30% R** / 41% D (Pew, June 2019), which is the Weak floor the rejection rests on and was never a blast-radius inference; and the residual category the reviewer identifies is largely already addressed by the ratified article, whose Section 3 grants access and correction against government and whose purpose-limitation sentence bars reuse of government-held data for unrelated purposes. **Gate impact: none — the clause stays rejected on the 30% floor, with the §4 path back unchanged.**
