# A Private-Sector Data-Protection Section — Clause Memo (Privacy)

*Registered 2026-08-19 as a candidate clause of the Privacy amendment on the founder's direction. The parent memo's strategic read names this fork explicitly: for the commercial side, "either a California-style text that reaches private actors, or an enumerated congressional power plus a floor of individual rights (access, correction, deletion)." The ratified article took the enumerated-power route (Section 3, third sentence; Section 7, second sentence). This clause is the other branch — a floor of individual rights in the data, binding private holders. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled as constitutional text — the adjacent statutory evidence is Strong (68% R / 78% D for more regulation of what companies do with personal data, Pew 2023; 89% R / 77% D for required company deletion tools, YouGov 2023), but no independent survey has ever asked whether the rulebook belongs in the Constitution, and the two honest framings (rights-in-the-data vs. fiduciary duty) have never been tested against each other — **candidate clause (gate: a commissioned split-sample instrument, threshold in §6).**

## 1. The clause

A ninth section of the privacy article. Two candidate formulations, deliberately different in kind; the poll in §6 is designed to choose between them. Both carry the article's operative term, "private or personal information" (the New Hampshire 2018 vocabulary, adopted throughout the article by founder decision 2026-08-19).

**Variant A — rights in the data (the Charter Article 8 model):**

> *"Every person has the right to the protection of private or personal information concerning them. Whoever collects or processes the private or personal information of others in the course of commerce shall do so fairly, for stated purposes, and no further than those purposes require, and shall keep it accurate and secure. Every person may know what private or personal information concerning them is so held, obtain correction of material inaccuracies, and obtain deletion of what is held without consent or other lawful basis. Congress and the States shall have power to enforce and further define this section by appropriate legislation."*

**Variant B — the fiduciary duty (the Data Care Act model):**

> *"Whoever collects the private or personal information of others as a condition of providing a service owes those persons duties of care, loyalty, and confidentiality with respect to that information, and may not use it to their detriment. Congress and the States may define and enforce these duties by law."*

**What either would add to the ratified article.** The article's shield (§§1–2, 4–5) runs against government only, by design. Its only reach into commerce is delegation: §3's third sentence makes commercial access, correction, and deletion an express legislative power, and §7's second sentence supplies enforcement authority. Until a legislature acts, the commercial collection model is untouched. The new section would state the principle itself at constitutional rank: the five processing principles that have been textually stable from Convention 108 (1981) through GDPR Article 5 (2016) — purpose limitation, minimization, accuracy, security, access/correction ([Convention text](https://rm.coe.int/1680078b37); [GDPR text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)) — or, in Variant B, the duty framework of S. 3744 (Schatz + 15 cosponsors, 115th Congress: care, loyalty, confidentiality; [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/senate-bill/3744); [Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/data-privacy/democratic-senators-introduce-privacy-bill-seeking-to-impose-fiduciary-duties-on-online-providers/)). Machinery — thresholds, consent mechanics, penalty schedules, the regulator — stays legislative in both variants, per the parent's Lesson 4 and the Draghi record on what happens when it doesn't ([Lexology on the Draghi report](https://www.lexology.com/pro/content/draghi-report-inconsistent-gdpr-enforcement-hinders-innovation); [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-draghi-dilemma-the-right-and-the-wrong-way-to-undertake-gdpr-reform/)).

**The central drafting question — how horizontal, how self-executing.** Constitutional rights against private actors are rare but not unprecedented: the Thirteenth Amendment binds private conduct directly, and California's Article I, §1 privacy right is applied against private entities under *Hill v. NCAA*, 7 Cal. 4th 1 (1994) — "an inalienable right which may not be violated by anyone," enforced through a three-element balancing framework rather than strict scrutiny ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/7/1.html)). Chile constitutionalized data protection in 2018 before its modern statute, and Mexico's Article 16 constitutionalizes the ARCO rights (access, rectification, cancellation, opposition) ([Constitute Project](https://www.constituteproject.org/topics/privacy?lang=en)). The EU Charter's Article 8 is the fullest model: a freestanding data-protection right, conditions on all processing, access and rectification, an independent authority ([Charter text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:12012P/TXT)). Both variants above follow *Hill* and Article 8 in stating the right horizontally, and follow the article's own architecture in routing machinery to legislation; how much of the core a court could apply before Congress acts is the question a drafting attorney would have to settle, and the memo flags it rather than resolves it.

## 2. The evidence

**What is Strong — the statutory version of the same idea.**

- More government regulation of what companies do with users' personal data: **72% — 68% R / 78% D** ([Pew, May 2023 fieldwork](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/10/18/how-americans-view-data-privacy/)). This is the parent category's own floor anchor.
- Requiring companies to give users the ability to delete their personal data: **89% R / 77% D** — one of the few privacy items where the Republican number is the higher one ([YouGov, Jan 26–30, 2023](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/45147-us-tech-regulation-receives-overwhelming-support)).
- Deletion by the holder: personal financial data held by a tax preparer, **79% — 83% R / 77% D** ([Pew, June 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/01/27/most-americans-support-right-to-have-some-personal-info-removed-from-online-searches/)).
- The legislative record agrees on substance and dies on machinery: ADPPA (117th) and APRA (118th) each contained minimization, purpose limitation, and the access/correction/deletion suite, and each died on preemption and the private right of action — Speaker Pelosi siding with California against preempting CCPA/CPRA; Sen. Cantwell objecting to enforcement design ([ADPPA H.R. 8152](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/8152); [APRA H.R. 8818](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/8818); [civil-rights letter to Speaker Pelosi](https://civilrights.org/resource/letter-to-speaker-pelosi-urging-vote-on-the-american-data-privacy-and-protection-act/)).
- The states have converged without waiting: **23–24 comprehensive consumer-privacy statutes by mid-2026, red and blue alike**, nearly all on the Virginia model — AG-enforced, no private right of action ([Byte Back, June 2026](https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2026/06/u-s-state-privacy-law-landscape-expands-to-24-states-what-the-latest-legislative-wave-means-for-businesses/); [IAPP on Vermont](https://iapp.org/news/a/vermont-becomes-23rd-state-to-enact-consumer-privacy-law)). California's deletion right has operated at scale for three years with workable carve-outs ([Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.105](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.105)).

**What is unpolled — the constitutional increment.** No independent national survey with a party breakdown has asked whether data-protection rules on companies belong in the Constitution, in either the rights-based or the fiduciary framing (searched 2026-08-19; nothing found beyond advocacy and industry surveys without usable crosstabs). The 89R/77D and 68R/78D numbers are evidence for the *statutory* version — which is what §3's third sentence already authorizes. The gap between "Congress should require this" and "this belongs in the Constitution" is exactly the increment the commissioned poll must measure, and the parent category's own history counsels against assuming it is small: support routinely moves double digits when the instrument changes what is being asked for.

**Nuances & caveats.**

- All three poll sources are independent pollsters; fieldwork is 2019–2023 — not peak-salience, but a 2026 refresh belongs in the commissioned instrument regardless.
- The state-statute landslide is legislative revealed preference, not a ballot result; no state has put the commercial rulebook to a popular vote. The parent's privacy ballot landslides (Michigan 89%, New Hampshire 81%, Missouri 75%) are all government-facing texts and are **not** evidence for this clause.
- The Virginia-model consensus (AG enforcement, no private right of action) cuts both ways: it shows red-state legislatures accept the substance, and it warns that a clause read as creating a private damages action against companies would be to the right of where 20+ legislatures landed. Both variants therefore leave remedies to legislation.

## 3. The gate result

- **Floor: none as drafted.** **Verdict: Unpolled** — with Strong adjacent statutory evidence (68% R / 78% D) and the constitutional increment unmeasured. Under SELECTION_METHOD §4.4, judgment may substitute for a direct poll only with a close named comparator; the statutory items are near comparators for the *substance* but not for the *constitutionalization*, so the clause holds for a commissioned poll rather than folding into the article now.

**Failure modes (§4.3).**

- *High-topline / low-floor* — **passes on present evidence.** Every party split on the statutory substance clears 68% R; the Republican number leads on deletion tools. The unknown is the constitutional framing, not the party structure.
- *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **untested; the live risk.** No instrument has named the costs: compliance burdens on small firms (the Draghi record), price effects of restricting the ad-funded model, or the "free services" trade. The commissioned item names them.
- *Framing dependency* — **present by design.** Rights-in-the-data and fiduciary-duty are different asks with plausibly different coalitions; the split-sample in §6 measures it rather than guessing.
- *Blast radius (§6.3)* — **the drafting risk, in two layers.** First, scope: an unscoped "whoever processes personal information" reaches churches, clubs, neighbors, and household address books; both variants scope to commerce ("in the course of commerce" / "as a condition of providing a service"), mirroring the GDPR's household exemption and CCPA's for-profit "business" definition ([Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.140)). Second, speech: *Sorrell v. IMS Health*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), holds that "the creation and dissemination of information are speech within the meaning of the First Amendment" and struck a state statute restricting the sale and marketing use of prescriber data ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/552/)) — the constitutional-rank answer to *Sorrell* is a large part of this clause's why-an-amendment case, but the same collision means the clause must stay on conduct-and-use rules (collection, purpose, retention, correction, deletion of unpublished data) and never touch publication; the article's §7 savings clause applies to the new section as to every other.
- *Staleness / incumbent coding / abuse-vs-remedy* — **not the pattern.** Data collection is not regime-coded; both parties' bills carry the same substantive core.

**Non-polling tests (§5).** *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **transfers from the parent, and adds a limb the parent does not need.** The parent's answer is that an amendment dissolves the preemption and private-right-of-action fights that killed ADPPA and APRA. For this clause there is also the *Buckley*-pattern answer: *Sorrell* shows courts trimming data statutes under the First Amendment, and a constitutional data-protection right rebalances that litigation the way the campaign-finance article answers *Buckley*. Honestly stated, the *Sorrell* limb is thinner than *Buckley* — *Sorrell* struck one state statute for speaker discrimination and did not void the field; a drafting attorney should treat it as reinforcement, not the load-bearing answer. *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes by construction**: the rule binds every company serving every customer, with no partisan valence in either direction; the bills carrying the substance have come from both parties (ADPPA was bipartisan out of committee 53–2). *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **inherited from the parent, with the parent's caveat**: the category's existential case rests on the aggregation of private data ultimately reaching government and public life; the commercial rulebook is the upstream half of that case. This is the softest of the three tests for this clause and the salon-ready question: whether the commercial half is amendment-grade on its own, or rides only as the completion of the article's architecture.

## 4. Relationship to the adopted article and the rejected deletion clause

- **Adopted §3, third sentence** (access/correction/deletion against "others" as a legislative power): the new section would convert that delegation into a constitutional floor. If the poll fails, the delegation stands and nothing is lost; the two are compatible by design.
- **Rejected clause `privacy-deletion-right`** (rejected 2026-08-18; 30% R floor on deletion applied to law-enforcement records): **no overlap with the piece that failed.** That clause died on its government-records increment and its self-executing breadth. This clause is commerce-scoped, keeps remedies legislative, and carries deletion only as one of three rights-in-the-data, in the scoped form (unpublished data held without lawful basis) whose statutory twin polls 89% R / 77% D. The rejected entry's path back — the two-step drafting-then-polling condition — remains the only route for any deletion right reaching government records; nothing here reopens it. The rejected memo's own Review Addition (CCPA § 1798.105's operating carve-outs) is affirmative evidence *for* this clause's drafting feasibility on the commercial side.

## 5. Path forward — the commissioned poll

Core item (Variant A framing):

> *"Would you favor or oppose adding to the Constitution a rule that companies collecting personal information may use it only for the purposes they stated, must keep it accurate and secure, and must let each person see, correct, and delete the information a company holds about them — with Congress and the states writing the enforcement rules?"*

**Split-sample arm (Variant B framing), identical placement:**

> *"Would you favor or oppose adding to the Constitution a rule that companies collecting personal information as a condition of a service owe the people it concerns duties of care, loyalty, and confidentiality — like the duties a bank or a lawyer owes a client — and may not use their information against them, with Congress and the states writing the enforcement rules?"*

**Statutory companion (split sample against the core item, to price the constitutional increment):** the identical Variant A wording with "adding to the Constitution a rule" replaced by "a federal law requiring."

**Costs-named follow-up (required — depth is the untested mode):**

> *"Supporters say people should have rights over data companies collect about them no matter which party writes the laws, and that every attempt to pass this by statute has failed for thirty years. Opponents say constitutional rules on businesses would burden small companies, raise the cost of free online services, and put judges in charge of how websites work. Knowing both arguments, do you favor or oppose it?"*

**Threshold: 60% in both parties on the costs-named core item, and the constitutional version within 10 points of the statutory companion.** The second condition is the decision rule the deletion clause pioneered: if constitutionalization costs more than 10 points, the public is endorsing the statute the article already authorizes, not the amendment text. Between the variants, adopt whichever framing clears; if both clear, Variant A, which matches the article's existing §3 vocabulary. Below 45% in either party on both framings, the clause moves to rejected with the statutory route noted as the path the article already provides.

## 6. Recommendation

**Candidate clause, gated on the §5 instrument.** The substance rests on some of the strongest bipartisan numbers in the corpus — 68% R / 78% D for regulating company data practices, 89% R / 77% D for required deletion tools — and the why-an-amendment answer (dissolving the preemption/PRA stalemate; rebalancing *Sorrell*) transfers from the parent. What has never been measured is whether the country wants the rulebook at constitutional rank rather than as the statute §3 already authorizes, and which of the two honest framings carries it. Do not fold into the article on adjacent evidence; the constitutional increment is precisely the kind of gap the method exists to price.
