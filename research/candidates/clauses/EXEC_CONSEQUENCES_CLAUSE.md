# Consequences for Willful Defiance — Clause Memo (Executive Compliance with the Courts)

*Held back in [research/EXECUTIVE_COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md](../../EXECUTIVE_COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a candidate clause of the Executive Compliance with the Courts amendment from 2026-08-18. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled — the parent article's 76–80% Republican floor covers the *duty* and says nothing about the *penalty*, and the three pieces here are three different asks — held by the absence of any measurement plus an unanswered "who adjudicates" question after *Trump v. Anderson* — **candidate clause (gate: three separate favor/oppose items, ≥60% floor each)**.

## 1. The clause

The drafted text, retained in full in the parent memo's held-back block:

> *"An officer of the United States who, by final judgment after notice and hearing, is found to have willfully refused compliance with a judgment or order binding upon that officer under this article shall thereupon be disqualified from that office; and the pardon power shall not extend to contempts of court. During any period in which a court of the United States has certified an officer's willful noncompliance, no funds may be obligated for the salary of that officer."*

The ratified article ([PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) §7) has two sections: tiered bindingness and a safe harbor for appealing, seeking stays, and arguing for a change in law. It states a duty and creates no remedy. The parent memo's implementation pass is explicit about what that leaves standing: the pardon power over contempt survives (*Ex parte Grossman*, 1925), the Marshals stay under the Attorney General ([28 U.S.C. §566](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A28+section%3A566+edition%3Aprelim%29)), and whether courts would infer any remedy beyond existing contempt and mandamus is open. This clause is what would close that gap — and it is **three severable rules**, not one:

1. **Disqualification** from office on a final judgment of willful refusal, after notice and hearing.
2. **A pardon carve-out** for contempts of court — prospectively overruling *Grossman* and answering the 2017 Arpaio precedent.
3. **Salary suspension** during a court-certified period of willful noncompliance — the smallest self-executing money lever.

**Why the parent memo held it back.** Two stated reasons. First, *partisan-coding risk is highest here*: in 2026 a disqualification-for-defiance rule reads as an anti-incumbent measure, and the parent memo's own strategic read says "do not lead with enforcement-against-officials provisions; lead with the rule, and treat enforcement as machinery." Second, and decisively for the gate, **it has never been polled in any form** — the 84% obey-the-Court consensus is a measurement of the duty, not the penalty. The parent memo's disposition is that "its sections rejoin one by one as polling matures; all are severable by design."

A third reason arrived after the fact, in the 2026-08-17 Review Additions: ***Trump v. Anderson*** (per curiam, March 4, 2024) held that "responsibility for enforcing Section 3 against federal officeholders and candidates rests with Congress and not the States," pointing to §5 enforcement legislation. A disqualification clause drafted to operate "thereupon" must therefore say expressly whether it is self-executing and who makes the finding — or *Anderson*'s logic routes it to Congress and reproduces the Impoundment Control Act's no-remedy problem, where the right exists and no one can enforce it.

## 2. The evidence

**Best first — and the best is adjacent, not direct.**

- **The duty is measured and stable; the penalty is not measured at all.** Marquette's series runs ten waves since 2019 and has "never dipp[ed] below 76%": [Jan. 21–28, 2026](https://today.marquette.edu/2026/02/new-marquette-law-school-national-survey-finds-63-say-u-s-supreme-court-should-limit-trumps-tariff-authority/) 82% overall (**76% R** / 79% I / 90% D); [Sept. 15–24, 2025](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/10/02/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-continuing-large-majority-who-say-a-president-must-obey-the-supreme-court/) 84% (80R/77I/92D); [Nov. 5–12, 2025](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/11/20/mlspsc29-court/) 84% (78R/76I/92D); [March 17–27, 2025](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/04/03/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-70-oppose-impeaching-judges-83-say-president-must-follow-supreme-court-decisions/) 83% (78R/82I/90D). Every one asks whether the president is *required to obey*. None asks what should happen if an officer does not.
- **The symmetry evidence is the stability itself.** Marquette reports 79% said "required to follow" in 2024, under a Democratic administration — meaning the identical clause would have bound officials in that administration on student-loan and eviction-moratorium orders. This is the specific fact the framing must carry.
- **The nearest measurement of an appetite for *penalties* points the other way — and it is the most important number in this memo.** Marquette's March 17–27, 2025 wave (n=1,021, ±3.5) asked whether federal judges who ruled against spending freezes and agency closures "should be impeached or should not be impeached for these rulings": **70% should not** — 52% R / 75% I / 87% D ([release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/04/03/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-70-oppose-impeaching-judges-83-say-president-must-follow-supreme-court-decisions/)). It measures the mirror image of this clause — removing the *judge* rather than the officer — and it establishes that the public's instinct against removing officials over a legal dispute is bipartisan and roughly two-to-one. Adjacent, and pointing at the disqualification piece's central risk: the public that overwhelmingly says obey does not automatically say *remove*.
- **Defiance itself remains a minority position in both parties, which is the floor the clause builds on.** [PRRI, Aug. 15–Sept. 8, 2025](https://prri.org/research/trumps-unprecedented-actions-deepen-asymmetric-divides/): "When decisions by Congress or the Supreme Court hold our country back, the president should be able to ignore them" — 16% agree / 78% disagree (24% R / 14% I / 10% D agree). [Annenberg (APPC), March 6–16, 2025](https://www.annenbergpublicpolicycenter.org/most-americans-support-checks-on-presidential-power/) (n=1,363): asked whether the president "should be able to ignore court decisions he believes intrude on his constitutional authority," 16% agree — **27% R** / 20% I / 5% D; and 69% say the president should follow a Supreme Court ruling even in a terrorist-attack hypothetical, up from 44% in 2007.
- **The pardon carve-out has one adjacent, non-party-broken reading.** [Strength In Numbers/Verasight, Feb. 18–20, 2026](https://www.gelliottmorris.com/p/two-thirds-of-americans-want-term) (n=1,566, ±2.5): "placing limits on the president's power to grant pardons and clemency for federal crimes" — 56% support / 24% oppose / 20% unsure, with Republicans net **+22**. Published R and D percentages are not given, so this cannot supply a floor; it establishes only that limiting the pardon power in the abstract is not a partisan proposition. The parent pardon package's own numbers (family ban 77%, 64R/88D) point the same way.

**Nuances and caveats.**

- **The three pieces will not poll alike, and pricing them together would waste the survey.** Salary suspension is a small, reversible, money-only consequence contingent on a court certification. The pardon carve-out is a limit on a power a majority already wants limited. Disqualification is removal from office by judicial finding — the one the 70%-oppose-impeachment number warns about. The inventory's own instruction is to ask them separately "since they are severable."
- **Concrete-application risk is documented and severe at the lower tier.** Marquette's Nov. 2025 wave found that district-court orders blocking the administration's actions were "a proper use of judicial authority" for 65% overall but **only 39% of Republicans** (71% I / 89% D) ([release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/11/20/mlspsc29-court/)). A consequences clause triggered by any district judge's finding inherits that 39%, not the 76–80%. The drafting answer is already in the text — *final judgment*, notice and hearing — and the poll wording must say so.
- **The *Anderson* problem is a drafting problem before it is a polling problem.** "Thereupon disqualified" reads as self-executing; *Anderson* says enforcement of a disqualification against federal officeholders is Congress's under a §5-style power. Either the clause names the adjudicating court and says it is self-executing on final judgment, or it delegates the finding procedure to Congress under the corpus's "principles, not machinery" rule ([SELECTION_METHOD](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) §6.2). It cannot stay silent.
- **The comparative case for personal consequences is strong and the domestic case is thin.** The parent memo's international file (South Africa's Nkandla and *Zuma* line) and the state file agree that consequences attached to *individuals* are the only mechanism with a compliance record, while contempt against a government as an entity produces slow, partial compliance — Washington's [$100,000/day *McCleary* fine](https://edlawcenter.org/washington-supreme-court-fines-state-for-not-fairly-funding-its-schools/) *(Education Law Center is advocacy; the order is primary)* ran for years as a scoreboard. The domestic removal precedents pair cleanly across parties and both are state-level: a Republican state chief justice removed from office in 2003 for defying a federal order, and a Democratic governor held in civil contempt by the Fifth Circuit in 1962 with a $10,000-per-day fine ([*United States v. Barnett*, 1964](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/376/681)). At the federal level, cabinet-officer contempts have been found under administrations of both parties and none has stuck personally — a 2002 Interior Secretary's contempt was [reversed on appeal](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dc-circuit/1344579.html), and a 2019 sanction against an Education Secretary was paid by the Department, not the Secretary ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/zackfriedman/2019/10/24/betsy-devos-student-loans-contempt/)).
- **India is the cautionary file.** The parent memo flags that consequence machinery controlled solely by judges invites its own abuse; notice-and-hearing plus finality are the due-process guardrail, and the survey wording must carry them or it tests a different, worse clause.
- **New-polling search, run 2026-08-18, honestly:** no independent national poll with a party breakdown asks about disqualification for defiance, a pardon carve-out for contempt, or salary suspension. The nearest new item found was the [Yale Youth Poll, Spring 2026](https://youthpoll.yale.edu/spring-2026-results) — 67% disagreed that a president should continue policies in defiance of court rulings — which is a duty question in a youth sample with no usable party floor for this clause, and is not relied on here. **That absence is the finding.**

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor: none. Verdict: Unpolled** (§4.1). No credible national data exist on any of the three pieces. The 76–80% Republican floor belongs to the parent article's Section 1 and cannot be transferred: the corpus's own trap entry warns against "attaching a broad-battery number to a specific mechanism" ([SELECTION_METHOD](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) §11).

- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **the governing risk, and the reason this clause is not simply a fourth section of the article.** Both parties condemn the abuse at 76–90% and the remedy has never been asked. This is the FBI/DOJ pattern exactly, and §6.4 records that the gate "systematically strips enforcement machinery" every time it is run.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **at risk, with the strongest mitigation in the corpus.** Marquette's series is stable across a change of administration (79% in 2024), so the underlying duty is not regime-coded. Whether the *penalty* is, is untested — and penalties are where coding usually enters, because a penalty has a visible defendant.
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — cannot be assessed; there is no topline.
  - *Framing dependency* — **at risk and untested.** "Disqualified from office," "removed from office," and "may not continue to hold that office" are three different sentences; so are "no salary may be paid" and "salary is withheld until the officer complies."
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **untested and the most likely point of failure.** The counterargument is potent and obvious: a court could remove an executive officer over a contested legal dispute. Any instrument that omits it will overstate support.
  - *Staleness* — not applicable; there is nothing to be stale.

- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3) — **passes, and as a clause the answer is largely the parent's*** (answer 4: the text is silent). But two pieces have independent constitutional answers of their own, which is unusual for a clause: the **pardon carve-out** cannot be done by statute at all, because *Grossman* reads the pardon power to reach contempts and only text can change that; and **disqualification** likewise, because a statutory disqualification of federal officers runs into *Anderson* and the Appointments/impeachment structure. Salary suspension is the weakest on this test — Congress can and does condition appropriations, so a statute could plausibly do it, which argues for dropping it first if the package needs trimming.
  - *Symmetry (§5.2) — **passes by construction, but the framing burden is real and asymmetric in the current moment.*** The clause binds every officer of every future administration, and the stability of the obey-the-Court number across 2024 and 2025–26 is the receipt. The parent memo's paired examples are the model: the same clause would have bound officials of the prior administration on student-loan and eviction-moratorium orders. Any poll fielded with a named incumbent measures the incumbent, not the rule.
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1) — **passes with the parent.*** A duty that no mechanism enforces is the parchment-barrier problem the parent memo logs as an open salon question ("is the gate-surviving version — bindingness + safe harbor, with no enforcement teeth — worth an Article V campaign at all?"). This clause is the affirmative answer to that question, which raises its value above an ordinary held-back item.

## 4. Poll question

Three items, asked separately, in one instrument, with the parent article's obey-the-Court item carried as an anchor. All forward-looking and unnamed as to incumbent, per §11.

**Core item — disqualification (verbatim):**

> *"Suppose a federal court finds, after a full hearing and a final judgment, that a senior official in any future administration — of either party — has deliberately refused to obey a court order that applies to that official. Should that official be removed from office as a result, or should removal be left to the president and Congress to decide?"*

**Item 2 — pardon carve-out (verbatim):**

> *"Today a president may pardon someone convicted of contempt of court, including an official held in contempt for disobeying a court order. Should any future president be able to pardon a contempt-of-court conviction, or should contempt of court be outside the pardon power?"*

**Item 3 — salary suspension (verbatim):**

> *"If a federal court certifies that an official is deliberately refusing to comply with a court order, should that official's government salary be suspended for as long as the refusal continues, or should the salary continue to be paid?"*

**Costs-named follow-up (asked after all three, once):**

> *"Some people say a rule like this is the only way to make a court order mean anything against the government. Others say it lets judges remove or defund officials the voters chose, over disputes about what an order actually required. Knowing both arguments, do you favor or oppose the rule?"*

**Second follow-up (the tier trap, asked on item 1):**

> *"Would your answer change if the finding came from a single district judge rather than from a final judgment upheld on appeal?"*

**Threshold:** **≥60% in both parties, on the costs-named version, item by item.** A piece clearing 60R/60D is drafted into the article as its own section. A piece landing 45–59% in either party is held and re-tested with alternative wording. A piece below 45% in either party is dropped to rejected-clause status, and — because the three are severable by design — a failure on one does not hold back the others. Design requirements, all mandatory: field with no named incumbent; carry the Marquette obey-the-Court item as the anchor; state the notice-and-hearing and final-judgment conditions inside each item, since a version without them tests a clause the foundation would not draft.

## 5. Recommendation

**Candidate clause**, with the gate now named — which resolves the inventory's §E open question 2 in favor of keeping it rather than re-filing it as rejected. The reason to keep it is that its symmetry passes by construction and its why-an-amendment answer is independently strong for two of the three pieces (only text can carve contempt out of the pardon power or disqualify a federal officer), so the clause is not merely unpolled machinery — it is the only affirmative answer the corpus has to its own standing question about whether a duty without teeth is worth an Article V campaign. The reason it stays held is equally clear: nothing has ever been measured, the nearest adjacent number (70% oppose impeaching judges, 52R) cuts against the disqualification piece, and *Trump v. Anderson* means the clause needs a drafting decision on who adjudicates before it is worth fielding. Recommend the three-item instrument above be commissioned alongside the parent article's re-poll, and that drafting counsel resolve the *Anderson* question first, since a clause that cannot say who makes the finding cannot be polled honestly.
