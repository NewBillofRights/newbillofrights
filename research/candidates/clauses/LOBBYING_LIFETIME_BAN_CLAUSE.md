# A Lifetime Ban on All Lobbying — Clause Memo (Lobbying)

*Held back in [research/LOBBYING_RESEARCH.md](../../LOBBYING_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a candidate clause of the Lobbying amendment from 2026-08-18. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** 47.5% floor (Republicans, UMD 2022 Q37 acceptability) — Moderate, and measured on the wrong instrument — what holds it is that this clause has never been read on a favor/oppose floor instrument, and it must stand or fall on its own number rather than on the five-year door's, which is a different policy and not a §4.1 framing rescue — **candidate clause (gate: a standalone favor/oppose re-test clearing 60% in both parties)**.

## 1. The clause

The held-back clause would replace the five-year term in Principle 1 of the lobbying article with a permanent one. Principle 1 as drafted reads:

> *"No person who has served as a Senator, Representative, or officer of the United States in a senior position defined by law shall, within five years after leaving such office, receive compensation for advocating before, or advising others in advocating before, the Congress or the executive agencies of the United States on behalf of any other person or entity. This section shall not be construed to abridge the right of any person to petition the Government on their own behalf, or without compensation on behalf of others."*

The lifetime variant strikes "within five years after leaving such office" for "at any time after leaving such office." Nothing else changes: the citizen-petitioner firewall (Principle 3), the enforcement and non-waiver clause (Principle 4), and the lifetime foreign-agent bar (Principle 2) are unaffected. Principle 2 is worth naming because it is *already* a lifetime ban that cleared the bar — the article is not averse to permanence, only to permanence applied to all domestic petitioning.

**What it would add.** Under the five-year door, a departing official's core asset — relationships and insider knowledge — is depreciated rather than destroyed, and the peer-reviewed record is that it decays on its own: lobbyists connected to a departing senator "suffer a 24 percent drop in generated revenue when that Senator leaves office" ([Blanes i Vidal, Draca & Fons-Rosen, *AER* 2012](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.102.7.3731)); connections carry "a more consistent monetary premium" than expertise ([Bertrand, Bombardini & Trebbi, *AER* 2014](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.12.3885)). Five years bets that decay does the rest. The lifetime version refuses the bet and closes a second channel a wait leaves open: the prospective-employment incentive, in which an official's *final* years in office are shaped by who might hire them after. That is the clause's strongest structural argument, and the survey put it to respondents directly (Q34, convincing to 83.9% — 81.6% R / 87.3% D).

**Why the parent memo held it back.** The gate-check paragraph states it plainly: the lifetime ban "was excluded from candidacy at the outset," because the five-year door polls at a Strong floor and it does not. The memo's drafting note calls it the "lifetime-ban temptation" and instructs: "Hold the line at five years except for foreign governments." This is now a *corrected-evidence* holding — see §2.

## 2. The evidence

**Best evidence — and the correction that reshaped it.** Both figures the corpus originally relied on were misread; the 2026-08-17/18 review re-based them. The primary instrument is the [UMD Program for Public Consultation lobbying questionnaire, fielded June 13–29, 2022, n=2,606 registered voters, ±1.9%](https://vop.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Lobbying_Quaire_0622.pdf) (Nielsen Scarborough sample), re-read against the questionnaire itself for this memo:

| Item | Instrument | National | R | D | I |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Q37** — lifetime ban on ex-Members lobbying, *acceptable (6–10)* | 0–10 acceptability scale | 50.3% | **47.5%** | 54.4% | 47.5% |
| Q37 — same, *at least tolerable (5–10)* | same | 64.7% | 62.1% | 68.4% | 62.6% |
| **Q36** — extend the wait to five years, *acceptable (6–10)* | 0–10 acceptability scale | 67.1% | **65.7%** | 73.3% | 57.2% |
| Q36 — same, *at least tolerable (5–10)* | same | 80.1% | 77.5% | 85.9% | 73.2% |
| **Q38** — "which would you recommend the most" | forced choice, one option | 5 yrs 43.3% / life 31.6% | 43.3 / **32.5** | 46.0 / 29.4 | 37.5 / 34.2 |
| Q38 — "at least 5 years" (5-yr + lifetime combined) | derived | 65.3% | 64.9% | 66.8% | 62.4% |

Two traps are visible there, and both had caught the corpus. The "32%" once cited as the lifetime ban's *support level* is the share who **prefer** it to five years in a single-choice item — not the share who would accept it; the "65% for five years" is the **combined** "at least five years" total. [SELECTION_METHOD.md §11](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) now records this as a named trap. Read correctly, the lifetime ban is not a fringe position: **half the country finds it acceptable and roughly two-thirds find it at least tolerable, in both parties.**

**The comparison that holds the clause.** On the one instrument asking both questions the same way, the five-year door beats the lifetime ban by **18.2 points among Republicans** (65.7% vs 47.5%) and 18.9 among Democrats (73.3% vs 54.4%). The gap is uniform, not partisan: the public is not split over this along party lines but *within* both parties, over permanence itself.

**Party polarity is unstable.** The same questionnaire carries the 2017 wave, and on Q37 the parties trade places: **51.5% R / 45.1% D** in 2017 against 47.5% R / 54.4% D in 2022. Neither party is reliably the floor party. That cuts both ways — the number is not anchored to a partisan identity, but neither is it stable enough to treat 47.5% as fixed. Cook PVI crosstabs agree: the clause's highest 2022 acceptability was in **very red** districts (55.5%, the only cell above 55%).

**Legislative provenance is genuinely cross-party.** The questionnaire's own footnote for the lifetime option cites the BLAST Act as **H.R. 1412 and S. 546 in the 117th Congress — both Republican-sponsored** — alongside two Democratic vehicles. In the 119th the pattern holds in both chambers: [S. 4524](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/4524/text) (introduced May 13, 2026, Republican sponsor, Democratic cosponsor) and its House companion [H.R. 9130](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9130/text/ih) (Democratic sponsor, Republican cosponsor); the [Close the Revolving Door Act (H.R. 3554)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3554/text) carries the same rule. This is the clause's strongest non-polling asset: sponsored from both flanks, disownable by neither.

**Two corrections to the parent memo**, found by re-reading S. 4524 — flagged, not applied:

1. LOBBYING_RESEARCH.md says the BLAST Act bans "lobbying contacts regardless of registration status." The text is narrower and differently structured: § 2(a) amends 18 U.S.C. § 207(e)(1) to reach a former Member who either **(A)** registers as an LDA lobbyist **or (B)** knowingly makes an influencing communication to Congress on another's behalf. Registration is one of two independent triggers, not an irrelevance.
2. The memo attributes "penalties up to 5 years' imprisonment and $50,000 civil fines per violation" to the bill. S. 4524 specifies no schedule; a violator "shall be punished as provided in section 216." The figures are right in substance but come from Title 18, not this bill.

Also for scope: **S. 4524 covers former Members and elected officers lobbying *Congress* — not senior executive officials, and not agency lobbying.** Our drafted clause is materially broader than the bill usually cited for it. Its § 2(d) applies only to those leaving office after enactment — the prospective-effective-date discipline §6.2 requires of our own drafting.

**Nuances & caveats.**

- *The acceptability scale is not a floor instrument.* Every number above comes from a 0–10 acceptability scale or a forced choice inside a deliberative briefing. §4.1 defines the floor as the lower of R/D support on a **best-polled core proposal**; no favor/oppose item on the domestic lifetime ban exists in the corpus or, per the search below, anywhere. Treating 47.5% as the floor is the most conservative available reading, not a measured one.
- *A deliberative instrument is a costs-named instrument — and the clause survived it.* Respondents had already seen the free-speech objection (Q31, convincing to 38.0% — 42.8% R / 31.5% D), the chilling-effect/"good causes" objection (Q33, 44.0% — 45.3% R / 41.4% D), and the fallback-career objection (Q35, 32.3% — 34.1% R / 27.9% D) before answering Q36–Q38. That partly discharges the §4.3 **depth** test in advance: 50.3% found it acceptable *after* the strongest counterarguments. The recruitment objection performed best against reform, and it bites harder on a permanent ban than on a wait.
- *No newer data exists.* Searched 2026-08-18 for any post-2022 standalone survey on a domestic lifetime lobbying ban with party breakdowns. Nothing was found — 2026 coverage of the BLAST Act is legislative, not survey. **That absence is the finding**, and the reason this clause is gated on fieldwork rather than judgment.
- *Contrast the plank that cleared.* The lifetime **foreign**-government bar was asked as a plain favor/oppose item (Q46): **71.3% (70.5% R / 73.7% D)** in 2022, 74.9% (81.2% R / 69.7% D) in 2017, with its counterargument (Q44) convincing only 29.0% (30.9% R / 26.0% D). Permanence is not what the public objects to; scope is.
- *Federal law already contains a permanent bar in miniature.* [18 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/207) permanently bars former officials from switching sides on the *particular matters* they personally handled, as do several states. These are the doctrinally safest permanent restrictions — closest to conditions-of-office under [*Carrigan*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/117/) (2011) — and they suggest a middle draft the corpus has not priced (§4).

## 3. The gate result

**Floor: 47.5% (R, UMD 2022 Q37). Verdict: Moderate** — inside the 45–59% band, 12.5 points below the Strong bar. §4.1 permits a Moderate only when "the *framing* that reaches Strong is identifiable and honest," and **the lifetime ban does not currently satisfy that rule**. The five-year door's 65.7% R is not a *framing* of the lifetime ban; it is a substantively different policy — a shorter term in the same clause — and §4.1's rescue asks whether the same rule, worded honestly another way, reaches Strong. A substitute clause's popularity cannot carry a failing one. What keeps the lifetime ban a candidate rather than a rejection is therefore narrower and stands on its own evidence: the 47.5% is an acceptability reading from a deliberative instrument, not a favor/oppose floor, and §4.1's floor is defined on the latter — so the clause has never been measured on the instrument the gate actually uses. That is a measurement gap, not a rescue. The five-year door's higher number is relevant only to the *cost* of pressing on (below), not to whether this clause clears the bar.

**Failure modes (§4.3):**

- **High-topline / low-floor — cleared.** A 6.9-point R/D spread, among the narrowest in the corpus. Whatever ails this clause, a hidden party split is not it.
- **Depth — largely cleared, unusually.** Costs were named *in the instrument* (Q31/Q33/Q35) before measurement — the opposite of the balanced-budget casualty, where support collapsed once trade-offs were specified.
- **Staleness — at risk.** Four years old, fielded before the entire 2025–26 legislative wave; direction of movement unknown.
- **Incumbent / regime coding — low risk, empirically.** Polarity reversed between 2017 and 2022 without a large swing either way, and the highest acceptability sits in very red districts. Post-office lobbying is not coded to either party's incumbency.
- **Framing dependency — the live problem, and the whole gate.** The three existing instruments return 50.3%, 31.6%, and 65.3% for the same policy — a 34-point range driven entirely by question format. §11's trap entry exists because of this clause.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — cleared on the abuse side.** The "super-lobbyists buy greater influence" argument convinced 84.7% (82.8% R / 88.8% D) (Q30) and the public-service argument 85.4% (83.7% R / 87.6% D) (Q32). Both parties condemn the abuse decisively and divide only over whether the remedy should be permanent.

**Non-polling tests (§5):**

- **Why-an-amendment (§5.3) — the parent's, unchanged.** The article's answer is answer 1, courts struck the statutes: [*Miller v. Ziegler* (8th Cir. 2024)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca8/23-1902/23-1902-2024-07-29.html) applied strict scrutiny to a voter-approved *two-year* ban and struck it for want of a compelling interest and narrow tailoring. A permanent ban is a harder tailoring case still, so the clause needs the amendment at least as much as the surviving text does. It inherits this answer rather than supplying its own.
- **Symmetry (§5.2) — passes by construction and on the record.** It binds every future Member of both parties; sponsors come from both parties, both chambers, and two Congresses; the text names no officeholder and no industry. The BLAST Act's prospective-applicability provision is the incumbent-grandfathering pattern §6.2 prefers.
- **Existential-problem (§5.1) — passes with the parent.** A parameter change to an accepted target, not a new category; the anti-capture rationale that carried the article carries the clause.
- **Blast radius (§6.3) — the reason for care.** A permanent ban on compensated advocacy by everyone who ever held a "senior position defined by law" reaches far more people, forever, than a five-year door. The citizen-petitioner firewall and the own-behalf/uncompensated savings clause survive intact, so the campaign-killer scenario is answered by text either way. But the good-faith objection — a former official wanting to advocate a cause for pay decades later — has no textual answer, and it is the objection that scored highest against reform (44.0%).

## 4. Poll question

To be fielded by an independent pollster with party breakdowns, in an instrument carrying the five-year item so the two are comparable within respondents. **Randomize the order of the five-year and lifetime items** — the 2022 instrument asked five years first, which may itself have anchored the lifetime reading downward.

**Core item (verbatim):**

> "Would you favor or oppose a constitutional amendment providing that anyone who has served in Congress or in a senior federal office may never, at any time after leaving office, be paid to advocate before the federal government on behalf of someone else? The rule would apply to everyone who takes office in the future, from either party, and would not restrict anyone from petitioning the government on their own behalf or without pay."

**Follow-ups:**

1. *Costs named (required before any verdict):* "Supporters say a permanent rule is the only way to remove the incentive for officials to do favors for future employers. Opponents say a former official who is an expert on an issue — say childhood cancer or veterans' care — should not be barred for life from being paid to advocate for it, and that a lifetime rule would discourage capable people from entering public service in the first place. Having heard both, do you favor or oppose the lifetime rule?"
2. *The head-to-head against the surviving clause:* "If the choice were between a five-year wait and a permanent ban, and both were equally likely to become law, which would you favor?"
3. *The scope split the corpus has not priced:* "Would you favor or oppose a permanent ban limited to advocating on the specific matters a person personally worked on while in office, with a five-year wait for everything else?"
4. *Anchor:* carry the foreign-government item (2022 Q46 wording) in the same instrument, so the 71% plank calibrates the sample against a known reading.

**Threshold: ≥60% in both parties on the core item, holding at ≥60% in both parties on follow-up 1.** A 45–59% reading in either party leaves the clause where it is — candidate, held, with the five-year door carrying the article. Below 45% in either party it should be re-filed as rejected, citing the foreign-agent bar as the permanence the evidence does support. Follow-up 3 could change the *drafting* rather than the verdict: if the particular-matter variant clears 60% in both parties while the blanket version does not, the honest move is to draft that variant — also the one closest to *Carrigan*, and so the most defensible.

## 5. Recommendation

**Keep it as a candidate clause; do not fold it now.** The corrected evidence is materially better than the record the original holding rested on — half the country finds it acceptable, two-thirds at least tolerable in both parties, it survived a costs-named deliberative instrument, and its bill lineage runs through both parties in both chambers — but "better than we thought" is not "clears the bar," and the only instrument that ever measured it is not a floor instrument. What holds it is not opposition but the absence of a floor reading of its own: the clause has never been asked favor/oppose with party breakdowns, which is exactly what §4.1 requires, and it cannot borrow the five-year door's 65.7% R because a shorter term is a different policy rather than a different framing of this one. The five-year door's popularity bears only on the *cost* of pressing the lifetime version — it makes the marginal gain small relative to the coalition risk — not on whether this clause passes.

Two things should happen regardless of the poll. **Commission the re-test as a rider on an instrument the foundation is already fielding** — three items cost little and this is the article's only held-back clause. And **correct the two BLAST Act descriptions in the parent memo** (§2): the bill is narrower in scope and quieter on penalties than stated, which matters because it is the clause's principal real-world referent. Founder decides; the honest summary is that this clause was held on a misread number, has been re-based onto a correct one, and is still held — for a better reason than before.
