# Congressional Disapproval of Pardons (the Mondale Check) — Clause Memo (Limited Presidential Pardon Reform)

*Held back in [research/PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../../PARDON_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a candidate clause of the Limited Presidential Pardon Reform amendment from 2026-08-18. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** **30% Republican floor** (YouGov, May 13–15 2026) — **Weak**, and the clause is now measured rather than unpolled — what holds it is that it is the *worst*-performing item in the same battery that gives the ratified article its strongest number — **rejected clause (test: an independent both-parties re-ask clearing 60% in both parties, which no evidence in the corpus predicts)**.

## 1. The clause

The drafted text, as the parent memo retains it in its held-back block:

> *"A pardon shall be void if, within ninety days of its publication, two-thirds of each House of Congress votes to disapprove it."*

The clause has real pedigree: Walter Mondale's post-Watergate [S.J.Res.241](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241), introduced September 18, 1974 — ten days after the Ford–Nixon pardon of [September 8, 1974](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/proclamation-4311-granting-pardon-richard-nixon) — under which no pardon "shall be effective if two-thirds of each House of the Congress disapproves the granting of the pardon within one hundred and eighty days of its issuance." It was referred to Senate Judiciary and never acted on again. The parent memo's drafting shortens the window to ninety days and runs it from **publication** rather than issuance, so end-of-term pardons are reviewed by the incoming Congress, declining the alternative of a lame-duck blackout that would bar legitimate end-of-term mercy along with suspect grants.

**What it would add to the ratified article.** The [package skeleton](../../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) has three sections: publication with reasons and naming, categorical exclusions for family and quid pro quo, and a savings clause preserving mercy for everyone not described. Each operates *without a second decision-maker* — conditioning a pardon on disclosure, or excluding classes of recipient defined in advance, asks no one to judge whether a particular act of mercy was wise. This clause is different in kind: it is the only proposal in the package that would let another branch **undo a specific grant on its own assessment of the merits**, converting the pardon from a power constrained at its edges into a power subject to review.

**Why the parent memo held it back.** Two reasons, stated plainly: "never polled and procedurally complex." The first is no longer true (§2), and the answer it produced is not the one the clause needed. The second is structural and independent of any poll — see the mercy-destruction analysis in §3.

## 2. The evidence

**The clause has now been directly polled, and the corpus's "never polled" finding is stale.** YouGov's standalone national **Presidential Pardons** survey, fielded **May 13–15, 2026** (n=1,127 U.S. adult citizens), put a twelve-item support/oppose battery to the same respondents. Item 6I is the mechanism itself — *"Would you support or oppose… Giving Congress the power to reverse a presidential pardon"* — and every item's party crosstab is published ([toplines and crosstabs](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results_P22Pm9s.pdf)):

| Item (May 13–15, 2026) | Support | Dem | Rep | Floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Requiring pardon details, **including the reasons**, be made public | **79%** | 83% | 79% | **79% — Strong** |
| Prohibiting a president from pardoning themself | 72% | 85% | 61% | 61% — Strong |
| Impeaching a president who grants a pardon for a corrupt reason | 71% | 90% | 54% | 54% — Moderate |
| Prohibiting a president from pardoning a family member | 64% | 74% | 57% | 57% — Moderate |
| Giving the **Supreme Court** power to reverse a pardon | 50% | 63% | 42% | 42% — Weak |
| **Giving Congress power to reverse a pardon** | **48%** | 73% | **30%** | **30% — Weak** |
| Removing the pardon power entirely | 39% | — | — | — |

**Bipartisan floor: 30%. Verdict: Weak** (§4.1: below 45%). This is not a good topline hiding a split — the topline itself (48%) fails to reach a majority, and Republicans oppose the clause outright **55–30**, the only item in the battery where a party's opposition exceeds its support by that margin.

Because the items share respondents and a field period, the comparison is free of house effects, and two readings follow. First, the **publication item at 79% overall with a 79% Republican floor closes the corpus's single most important polling gap** — [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) records public written justification as never directly polled and warns that the Brennan Center's 79% was a general corruption battery, not this mechanism. This is the direct, independent measurement, landing Strong with Republicans at parity with Democrats. Second, and decisive here: **Republicans who back publication at 79% back congressional reversal at 30%.** A 49-point drop inside one instrument is not a wording artifact. It measures where the public's appetite stops — at transparency and categorical limits, short of review.

**A two-point time series exists, and the number has not moved.** The same battery was fielded **August 15–19, 2024** (n=1,130), with the reversal item at **40% support / 41% oppose** ([YouGov, August 26, 2024](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50378-presidential-pardons-which-ones-do-americans-approve-of)). Across twenty-one months spanning an administration change and the most active clemency period in the modern record, the topline moved from 40–41 to 48–31 — support rose modestly, never approaching a passing floor. [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) calls pardon opinion the fastest-moving in the corpus; this mechanism is the part that did not move.

**The legislative record is a live but thin cross-party signal that cuts against the clause on inspection.** [H.J.Res.135](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/135), introduced December 18, 2025, is a Mondale descendant: three-day notification to Congress, 30 days to introduce and 60 to pass a nullification bill by two-thirds of both Houses, effect suspended 90 days, and the pardon void if the President fails to notify. As of August 2026 it has **seven cosponsors — six Democrats and one Republican** — and has sat in House Judiciary without action ([cosponsors](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/135/cosponsors)). Its notification-or-void half nearly matches the ratified article's Section 1 and is where the cross-party interest sits; the reversal half drew one cosponsor from the other party in eight months. The bill reads as evidence for publication, not for disapproval.

### Nuances and caveats

- **"Congress" versus "two-thirds of Congress" is a real wording gap, and it runs in the clause's favor.** YouGov's item states no threshold; the drafted clause requires two-thirds of *each* House within ninety days — a bar that would rarely bind, and a respondent told so might read it as a narrow check rather than a transfer of the power. That is the strongest honest argument that 30% understates the clause, and it is not enough to carry a reopening: the Supreme Court variant, which also names no standard, polls **42% among Republicans** — twelve points better — indicating respondents discriminate on **who reviews**, not on how hard the threshold is. A supermajority qualifier plausibly moves 30% into the 40s; nothing suggests 60.
- **The one figure that appears to support the clause bundles it and cannot carry weight.** The Brennan Center's 69% for an amendment "including giving Congress the power to reverse certain pardons" (April–May 2026, [toplines](https://www.brennancenter.org/media/15709/download/corruption-survey-toplines-2026-06-01-final.pdf)) is advocacy-adjacent, publishes no party breakdown, bundles the override with the popular limits, and drew the second-highest opposition of nine reforms tested. Under the §11 trap against attaching a broad-battery number to a specific mechanism, it is adjacent evidence at best.
- **Adjacent numbers point the same way.** A generic "amend the Constitution to limit pardon power" drew 56% overall but **34% of Republicans** ([Economist/YouGov, Dec 5–8 2025](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53702-majorities-americans-say-donald-trump-gives-too-many-pardons-want-to-limit-pardon-power-december-5-8-2025-economist-yougov-poll)) — close to this clause's 30%, consistent with the parent memo's finding that anything reading as aimed at a sitting president collapses inside his party. The corpus's older family-ban and associates figures (77%, 64R/88D; 69%, 50R/84D) are [YouGov for Protect Democracy, May–June 2024](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/survey-jan-6-pardon-opposition/) — advocacy-commissioned, battleground sample; the May 2026 independent battery supersedes them.

## 3. The gate result

**Floor: 30% (Republicans, YouGov May 13–15 2026). Verdict: Weak.** Under §4.1 a Weak floor has been disqualifying every time it has appeared in this corpus. The registry entry recording this clause as `floor: null, verdict: "Unpolled"` with gate "Never polled; procedurally complex" is superseded by this memo.

**Failure modes (§4.3):**

- **High-topline / low-floor — fails, worse than the standard case.** The usual pattern is a strong topline concealing a split; here the topline is 48% and the split is 43 points. No framing salvage lives in that gap, because there is no good topline to preserve.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — fails, and this is the central diagnosis.** Both parties condemn the abuse: violent January 6 pardons drew 83% disapproval ([WaPo/Ipsos via The Hill, Feb 2025](https://thehill.com/homenews/administration/5157765-donald-trump-jan-6-pardons-wapo-survey/)) and the Hunter Biden pardon 32% approval ([Monmouth, Dec 2024](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_121124/)) — both parties' grants unpopular with both parties' voters. Yet where 79% want the reasons published, 30% of Republicans will hand the reversal to Congress: agreement on the wrong, division on the structural fix.
- **Incumbent / regime coding — at risk, untestable in the present alignment.** The clause's value to a respondent depends on who holds the White House and two-thirds of both chambers, and no forward-looking wording has been fielded. The 2024→2026 comparison does span an administration change without a reversal — the number is low in both alignments, which is a stability finding, not a rescue.
- **Depth — untested, and the untested cost is severe.** No survey has told respondents the power would reach every commutation for an ordinary applicant, not only the notorious grants. Support already falls from 79% to 48% with no cost named.
- **Framing dependency — the one live question, answered against the clause in §2.** The supermajority qualifier is untested, but the Supreme-Court variant indicates respondents sort on the reviewer, not the threshold.
- **Staleness — clears.** May 2026 fieldwork.

**Non-polling tests (§5):**

- **Why-an-amendment (§5.3) — passes, and the answer is the parent's.** *Ex parte Garland* (1866) holds the power "cannot be fettered by any legislative restrictions" and *Schick v. Reed* (1974) that it "cannot be modified, abridged, or diminished by the Congress," so a congressional check is unavailable by statute. This clause needs an amendment more clearly than any other in the package — which is not a reason to keep it; every Weak-floor clause the corpus rejected also had an answer here.
- **Symmetry (§5.2) — passes on its face, fails in operation.** The text names no party. But symmetric *text* is not symmetric *effect* when the trigger is a two-thirds vote of a body that is itself partisan: a pardon is reversible only when the president's opponents hold supermajorities in both chambers, a condition that has almost never obtained. The rule would rarely operate at all, and when it did, it would operate at the moment most likely to look partisan.
- **The mercy-destruction trap (§11) — this holds the clause independent of any poll.** Rhode Island is the live experiment: [R.I. Const. art. IX, §13](https://rilegislature.gov/riconstitution/Constitution/C09.aspx) vests the pardon power in the governor "by and with the advice and consent of the senate," and the result is that "No pardon to a living person has been issued in many years," the consent requirement "evidently discourag[ing] exercise of power" ([CCRC profile](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/rhode-island-restoration-of-rights-pardon-expungement-sealing/)). The drafted clause is better designed — post hoc and supermajority-gated rather than a consent precondition, exactly the mitigation §11 prescribes. But H.J.Res.135 shows how fast the design drifts back: its 90-day suspension of effect turns a post-hoc check into a delay on every act of mercy, including the ordinary commutations already in a documented drought ([DOJ clemency statistics](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-statistics)).
- **Originalist counterargument — the strongest in the package, aimed here specifically.** The Framers considered and rejected the two-key model: Sherman's motion for pardons "with consent of the Senate" failed 1–8 on August 25, 1787 ([Madison's notes](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_825.asp)); Randolph's treason exception failed 2–8–1 on September 15 ([Madison's notes](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_915.asp)). Hamilton defended the design in [*Federalist* No. 74](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed74.asp): "one man appears to be a more eligible dispenser of the mercy of government, than a body of men." The parent memo's answer — that the lead provisions add no second decision-maker and no delay, leaving the "sole fiat" intact — is one the surviving clauses can give and this one cannot. It is the only provision in the package the Convention actually voted down.

## 4. Poll question

The recommendation is rejection, so this is the **path back** — the one instrument that could reopen the clause, worth fielding only to close the question definitively rather than leave it at a Weak reading. This memo does not expect it to clear.

**Core item (verbatim):**

> "Some have proposed that any future president's pardon could be canceled if two-thirds of both the House and the Senate vote to disapprove it within ninety days — a threshold high enough that it would require large majorities of both parties. This would apply to every president of either party, and to every pardon, including ordinary commutations for people already in prison. Would you support or oppose adding this to the Constitution?"

**Follow-ups (costs named):**

1. "Would you still support it if it meant that a president of the party you support could have a pardon canceled by a Congress controlled by the other party?"
2. "Would you still support it if it meant every pardon — including for ordinary people serving long sentences — could be undone for ninety days after it was granted, so no one pardoned could be certain they were free?"
3. Asked of everyone as the diagnostic: "Which comes closer to your view — that the way to prevent pardon abuse is to require the president to publish the reasons for every pardon, or to give Congress the power to cancel pardons it disapproves of?"

**Threshold: ≥60% support in both parties on the core item, holding at ≥50% in both parties after follow-up (1)** — the standard the surviving clauses meet or are measured against. **Design requirements:** an independent pollster; wording that names no incumbent and says "any future president"; and the publication item (79%, 79R/83D) carried in the same instrument as the anchor, so the comparison that decided this memo can be re-run rather than re-argued. Follow-up 3 is worth fielding regardless — it prices the clause against the alternative the public has already endorsed.

**What would move it out permanently:** a repeat below a 45% Republican floor under the supermajority wording. The clause would then have been asked in general and qualified form, in two alignments, and the corpus should stop carrying it as a live gate.

## 5. Recommendation

**Reject as a clause of the Limited Presidential Pardon Reform article.** The clause is no longer unpolled: an independent national survey measures it at a **30% Republican floor** — Weak under §4.1, the lowest of any pardon-reform item tested except abolishing the power outright — and the 2024 reading of the same battery shows the number is stable rather than merely low. Two grounds converge with the polling: the mercy-destruction trap Rhode Island demonstrates in practice, and the fact that this is the one provision in the package the Convention specifically voted down, making it the clause most expensive to defend and least likely to be missed. It should move from *held back pending polling* to *rejected with a path back* (§4).

Two findings belong in the parent memo regardless of the disposition. **The May 2026 publication item (79% overall, 79R/83D) closes the corpus's most important polling gap** and is the first direct, independent measurement of the article's lead provision — it should be added to PARDON_RESEARCH.md and POLLING_RESEARCH.md and the standing "never directly polled" flag retired. The same instrument also gives an independent read on the self-pardon ban at **61% Republican / 85% Democratic** — a Strong floor, far above the 43% the corpus carries and above the ~60% reopening threshold SELF_PARDON_BAN §5 sets. That bears directly on the separate `self-pardon-ban` entry and is flagged for that memo rather than resolved here.
