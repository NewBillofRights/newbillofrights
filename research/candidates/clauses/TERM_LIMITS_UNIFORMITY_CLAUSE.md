# Uniformity and the *Thornton* Settlement — Clause Memo (Term Limits)

*Held back in [research/TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md); tracked as a candidate clause of the Term Limits amendment from 2026-08-18 — on a drafting-counsel gate, not a survey gate. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled, and the search for polling returned nothing because the clause is not the kind of thing a survey can price — it adds no rule, only a rule of construction — held by a live drafting risk that its state-election-power half could be read against the redistricting article; status: **candidate clause (gate: drafting counsel, not a poll)**.

## 1. The clause

The text held back, verbatim from [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md):

> *"This article establishes the sole limitation on the number of terms of service in the offices to which it applies, and shall not be construed to deny the several States any power they otherwise retain over the manner of holding elections."*

It does two separable things, and the memo's difficulty is that they are stapled together.

**The uniformity half** makes the article's numbers the ceiling *and* the floor — no state may stack a stricter congressional limit on top. This is the *Thornton* settlement written down. In [*U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/779/), the Court held 5–4 that neither states nor Congress may add to the Qualifications Clauses, and that change "must come through a constitutional amendment properly passed under the procedures set forth in Article V"; state-crafted congressional qualifications "would be inconsistent with the Framers' vision of a uniform National Legislature." Twenty-three states had adopted congressional limits by 1994 ([CRS 96-152](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/96-152.html)); *Thornton* voided all of them, and several remain printed in state constitutions — Missouri Art. III §45(a) still bars more than four House terms, stricter than this article's six ([Missouri Revisor of Statutes](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=III++++45%28a%29&bid=31795&constit=y)).

**The savings half** preserves state power over "the manner of holding elections" — the Art. I §4 language ([LII, Article I](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei)) — so the article is not later read as a federal takeover of election administration.

**What it would add to the ratified article: on the operative law, nothing.** *Thornton*'s rule is not disturbed by ratifying this amendment; it is satisfied. The article sets its own numbers, and Missouri's and Arkansas's stricter provisions stay unenforceable afterward for the same reason they are unenforceable now — they are state-added qualifications ([TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md §2, §3(a)](../../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md)). The clause would only say so expressly, foreclosing an argument rather than changing an outcome.

**Why the parent memo held it back:** "unpolled and purely construction-guiding, and its state-election-power language risks being read against the redistricting category. Uniformity follows from the operative text anyway; revisit only if ratification lawyers demand it." The package skeleton omits it as reintroducible "during formal drafting if needed," and the site page says the same.

## 2. The evidence

**Best first: the clause has no evidence of its own, and that is the finding.** No independent national poll has ever asked whether a term-limits amendment should bar states from setting stricter congressional limits, or whether it should preserve state election powers. A search on 2026-08-18 for party-split polling on either half returned nothing on the mechanism — only the parent category's toplines. There is no advocacy poll on it either.

What the corpus has is the parent's floor, which the clause rides on and does not alter: congressional term limits at **87% (90% R / 86% D)** — [Pew, 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/07/28/most-americans-favor-big-changes-to-the-political-system-that-likely-would-require-amending-the-constitution/), the smallest partisan gap of any structural reform in the corpus — and **83% (86% R / 80% D / 84% I)** after counterarguments in a deliberative format ([UMD PPC, Feb 2023](https://publicconsultation.org/united-states/congressional-term-limits/)).

**The one piece of evidence bearing on the clause directly** is doctrine, not polling, and it cuts in the clause's favor: the *Thornton* majority rested part of its holding on uniformity itself, quoting the Arkansas court below — "If there is one watchword for representation of the various states in Congress, it is uniformity" ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/779/)). A clause declaring the article's limits exclusive restates the value the case already vindicated. That is the strongest argument for it, and it is a lawyer's argument, not a voter's.

**Nuances and caveats.**

- *The two halves have different risk profiles.* The uniformity half is nearly costless — it restates settled doctrine. The savings half is where the danger sits, and the danger is internal to our own package. The [redistricting article](../../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) §2 requires districts to be "established once following each decennial enumeration… and shall not thereafter be altered until the next enumeration," and §1 forbids drawing them for "the advantage of any incumbent or party" — a federal constraint on how states conduct congressional districting. A term-limits article simultaneously declaring that nothing shall "deny the several States any power they otherwise retain over the manner of holding elections" hands a litigant a textual argument that the two articles are in tension, even though districting is not obviously "manner of holding elections" and a later-ratified article would ordinarily control. The cost is not that the argument wins; it is that it exists, in a package meant to read as coherent.
- *The savings half also cuts against the article's own enforcement route.* Art. I §4 gives Congress power to "make or alter" state election regulations, and the implementation analysis contemplates Congress using exactly that power to direct states to keep term-barred candidates off congressional ballots ([TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md §3(d)](../../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md)). Preserving state power over "manner" without preserving Congress's alteration power is asymmetric drafting. It also complicates a question the same analysis flags as open — whether state ballot bars *mirroring* the ratified article are "manner" regulations under [*Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510 (2001)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-929.ZO.html), which struck Missouri's ballot-label scheme because "it does not regulate the time of elections; it does not regulate the place of elections; nor, we believe, does it regulate the manner of elections." A uniformity clause would not answer that; a savings clause invites the argument that states retain more here than they do.
- *One verified historical note, precedent rather than evidence:* Missouri's 1996 Article VIII provided for its own automatic repeal "should the specified Congressional Term Limits Amendment be ratified" (*Cook*, n. 5) — the one confirmed instance of state law keyed to ratification of such an amendment. States have written the transition themselves before.

## 3. The gate result

**Floor: none. Verdict: Unpolled — and, unusually, unpollable in any form that would carry a verdict.** To be explicit about what that does and does not mean under §4.1: **this clause has no bipartisan floor of its own and never will, and it is not exempt from the full-spectrum test — it rides the parent article's floor of 86% R / 80% D and adds no rule for the public to accept or reject.** It changes no one's rights, duties, or expectations; it tells a court how to read numbers the public has already been polled on at 87%. A rule of construction with no independent policy content passes or fails with the article it construes. If the founder concludes the drafting-counsel tag is not available to a clause of this kind, the correct disposition is not to poll it but to drop it and let *Thornton* supply uniformity by operation of law — which §1 of this memo already concludes it does. Every other candidate in the corpus is waiting on fieldwork; this one is not. A survey can price a rule people would live under; it cannot price a canon of construction, because respondents have no way to hold an opinion about whether a clause forecloses an argument a court would likely reject anyway. Asking would produce a number that means nothing — worse than no number, because it would enter the corpus looking like evidence.

**Failure modes ([SELECTION_METHOD.md §4.3](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)):**

- *High-topline / low-floor* — not assessable; no topline exists.
- *Framing dependency* — **at risk, and the clause's real polling-adjacent problem.** The two halves would poll differently if asked ("the same limits apply everywhere" versus "states keep their power over elections"), and the second reads as a states'-rights proposition in a package that elsewhere constrains states. Bundling them in one sentence is a drafting choice a survey would expose rather than resolve.
- *Blast radius ([§6.3](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md))* — **the operative test, and the savings half is where it bites.** The clause's purpose is narrow; its "manner of holding elections" language reaches every part of election administration the article never meant to touch, and one of our own articles regulates a piece of it.
- *Incumbent / regime coding, depth, staleness* — not applicable. The clause creates no benefit any incumbent or party could capture, and there is no data to go stale.

**Non-polling tests ([§5](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)):**

- *Why-an-amendment* — **the parent's answer, as expected for a clause.** Term limits require Article V because *Thornton* closed every other route and Congress cannot self-bind. A rule of construction inside an amendment is there only because the amendment is.
- *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes trivially.** Uniform limits binding every state equally are symmetric by construction; no party gains from the numbers being exclusive rather than a floor.
- *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **fails as a standalone, and need not pass.** Clauses inherit the parent's existential warrant; this one would never be proposed on its own.

The gate's honest summary: the clause is not held because it might be unpopular. It is held because it is probably unnecessary and its second half is affirmatively risky — and neither is a question the public can answer.

## 4. Poll question

**No poll should be commissioned for this clause.** That is a recommendation, not an omission — the survey spec is finite, and the corpus's genuinely unpolled clauses (the emoluments remedy, the enforcement clauses, the pardon variants) are waiting on fieldwork that would actually move them.

If the founder wants a question on record anyway, the only version worth fielding tests the uniformity half alone, never the bundled sentence:

> **"If the Constitution were amended to limit members of Congress to twelve years in each chamber, should those limits be the same in every state — so that no state could set a shorter limit for its own senators and representatives — or should each state be free to set stricter limits for the people it sends to Congress?"**

**Follow-ups:**

1. *Costs named:* "Supporters of letting states set stricter limits say a state should be able to demand more of its own representatives. Supporters of one national rule say a state that limits its members more tightly gives away seniority and influence in Congress to states that do not. Knowing that, which comes closer to your view?"
2. *The savings half, asked separately and never bundled:* "Should an amendment on term limits also say that it does not change any other power states have over how elections are run?" — reported on its own, because if it clears while the uniformity half does not, the finding is about states'-rights framing, not this clause.

**Threshold: ≥60% in both parties on the uniformity half, sustained through the costs-named follow-up** ([§4.1](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)). Even clearing that would not by itself put the clause in the draft — it would remove the "unpolled" objection and leave the drafting-counsel question, which is the one that governs. A floor below 60% would be a reason to draft the uniformity half more quietly, not to abandon uniformity, since *Thornton* supplies it either way.

## 5. Recommendation

**Keep as a candidate clause on a drafting-counsel gate, and split it in two before anyone drafts it again.** The uniformity half is close to costless and is what ratification lawyers would ask for if they ask for anything; the savings half carries a real collision risk with the redistricting article and should not return in this wording. The registry inventory's open question 8 is right that "deferred to drafting counsel" is a bucket the schema lacks, and this clause is the case for adding the tag rather than filing it next to clauses that are one survey away from resolution. Concretely: leave the article as drafted, tag the entry `drafting-counsel`, and revisit only when a drafting attorney reviews the package and says whether *Thornton*'s uniformity rule needs restating in text. If yes, draft the first half alone.

## Review Additions (2026-08-18)

*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

**Extends §2 (Nuances and caveats) and §3 (blast radius).**

- ***Moore v. Harper*, 600 U.S. 1 (2023)** — decided June 27, 2023, 6–3 (Roberts, C.J.), holding that "the Elections Clause does not insulate state legislatures from the ordinary exercise of state judicial review," and that legislatures regulating federal elections remain "bound by the provisions of the very documents that give them life," i.e. their own state constitutions; state courts in turn "may not transgress the ordinary bounds of judicial review" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/21-1271)). It rejected the independent-state-legislature theory. **Why it matters to this clause, and it sharpens the case against the savings half rather than changing the verdict:** the savings half preserves power in "the several States," while *Moore* confirms that Art. I §4 power is not a free-standing grant to a legislature but a power exercised inside the state's own constitutional order and subject to federal constitutional limits. So the half is doubly imprecise — it names the wrong holder of the power, and it purports to save a power that was never at risk from a term-limits article. It also does nothing to relieve the redistricting-collision risk §2 identifies, because *Moore* is about who reviews state election law, not about the scope of "Manner." **Gate impact: none.** The clause remains a candidate on the drafting-counsel gate; this addition strengthens the existing recommendation to draft the uniformity half alone if the clause returns at all.
