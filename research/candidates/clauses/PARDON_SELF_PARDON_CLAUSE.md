# No Self-Directed Clemency — Clause Memo (Limited Presidential Pardon Reform)
*Rejected 2026-08-06 as a category; reopened 2026-08-18 as a candidate clause of the Pardon Reform amendment on the founder's decision after YouGov May 2026. Every figure verified against the linked source.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — created on reopening; the 2024 and 2026 readings verified against the linked crosstab PDFs.*

**Status in one line:** Floor **61%** (Republicans, YouGov May 13–15, 2026, independent) against **43%** on the same wording twenty-one months earlier (YouGov Aug. 15–19, 2024, independent) — **Strong on the newest reading, Weak on the prior; the verdict is instrument-dependent** — what holds it is that one reading cannot distinguish a durable eighteen-point rise from a swing that tracks which party holds the office — **candidate clause (gate: one independent forward-looking, both-parties replication clearing 60% R).**

## 1. The clause

The drafted text, from [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) "Held back":

> *"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to himself or herself."*

**What it would add to the ratified article.** The article's operative rules reach how a grant is made (published with reasons, §1; named and specified, §2), who receives it (a relative, §3), and what was exchanged for it (something of value, §4). The self-benefit provision voids a pardon granted to shield the President from legal liability, which reaches the *purpose* of a grant to a third party. None of them reaches the simplest case: the President as the named recipient. The principle is the oldest in the package — no one may be a judge in his own case — and the Office of Legal Counsel's memorandum of August 5, 1974 reached exactly that conclusion ([DOJ OLC](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president); [PDF](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/olc/opinions/1974/08/31/op-olc-supp-v001-p0370_0.pdf)). An OLC opinion binds no court, no president has attempted a self-pardon, and no court has ruled; the Constitution Annotated treats the question as unresolved ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/article-2/section-2/clause-1/presidential-self-pardons)). The modern legislative vehicle bundles it with much else: [H.J.Res.13 (119th Cong., Jan. 9, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hjres13/BILLS-119hjres13ih.htm) bars pardons of "the President's self," relatives to the third degree, administration members and paid campaign staff, offenses committed at the President's direction, and voids any pardon "issued for a corrupt purpose." It has had no hearing and no markup in any Congress since 2017, and its sponsors have come from one caucus throughout.

**Why the parent memo held it back, and why the category was rejected.** [PARDON_RESEARCH.md](../../PARDON_RESEARCH.md) held the sentence out on the ground that Republican support was regime-dependent, and the category-level memo rejected it on 2026-08-06 with a 43% Republican floor — Weak under [§4.1](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) — recording the failure modes as high-topline/low-floor, beneficiary coding, and the abuse-vs-remedy gap ([SELF_PARDON_BAN_REJECTED.md](../../rejected/SELF_PARDON_BAN_REJECTED.md)). The rejection was entirely a polling result: the category passed all three non-polling tests then and passes them now. That memo also wrote the path back — an independent, forward-looking re-poll clearing about 60% Republican — and it is the arrival of a number above that line, in an instrument the foundation did not commission, that reopens the clause here.

## 2. The evidence

**The two readings, side by side.** Both are YouGov, both are independent national samples of adult citizens, and both asked the same battery in the same support/oppose format. The item is *"Would you support or oppose… prohibiting a president from pardoning themself?"* (support = strongly + somewhat).

| Fieldwork | n | Overall | Rep | Ind | Dem | Floor | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **May 13–15, 2026** | 1,127 | **72%** (58 + 14) | **61%** (42 + 19) | 71% (61 + 10) | 85% (72 + 13) | **61%** | **Strong** |
| **Aug. 15–19, 2024** | 1,130 | 64% (50 + 14) | 43% (24 + 19) | 67% | 81% (71 + 10) | **43%** | **Weak** |

2026 figures from the [toplines and crosstabs PDF](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results_P22Pm9s.pdf), table 6B, "Party ID" panel; 2024 figures from the [2024 crosstabs PDF](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results.pdf), table 7B, with the [YouGov write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50378-presidential-pardons-which-ones-do-americans-approve-of) rounding to 82/65.

**The movement is eighteen points among Republicans and four among Democrats.** Republican support rose 43 → 61; Democratic support rose 81 → 85; the topline rose 64 → 72. The Republican "not sure" share fell from 27% to 16% and Republican opposition rose slightly, from 29% to 22% — so most of the eighteen points came out of uncertainty rather than out of opposition. That is consistent with the 2024 memo's own reading that 43% was "a Weak floor with headroom, not a hard ceiling."

**Why the movement may be regime-coded, stated by date.** The 2024 fieldwork ran under a president of one party, three months before a presidential election, while the other party's nominee faced pending federal indictments — a self-pardon was a live prospect for one identifiable person, and the question could be read as a verdict on that person. The 2026 fieldwork ran under a president of the other party, with no election imminent and no charge pending against a president. Between those dates the office changed hands. The corpus records the same pattern running the other way on an adjacent item: asked in [January 28 – February 3, 2025](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Acceptability_of_President_Actions_poll_results.pdf) whether it is acceptable for a president to issue pardons to family members, 72% of Republicans and 42% of Democrats called it unacceptable — each party's floor collapsing on the provision its own president had just used ([YouGov write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/51548-where-americans-draw-the-line-on-presidential-behavior)). The honest statement is not that the 2026 number is wrong; it is that a party's support for a limit on the presidency has, in this corpus, moved with which party holds the presidency, and both of the self-pardon readings sit in alignments that could produce the numbers they produced.

**The 2026 instrument also anchors the clause against the rest of the package.** From the same battery and the same respondents (support = strongly + somewhat, Party ID panel):

| Item (Q6) | Overall | Rep | Ind | Dem | Floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Requiring pardon details, including reasons, be made public (6A) | 79% | 79% | 75% | 83% | **79%** |
| **Prohibiting a president from pardoning themself (6B)** | **72%** | **61%** | **71%** | **85%** | **61%** |
| Prohibiting a president from pardoning a family member (6D) | 64% | 57% | 62% | 74% | **57%** |
| Giving Congress the power to reverse a pardon (6I) | 48% | 30% | 46% | 73% | **30%** |

Inside one instrument, the self-pardon ban is the second-strongest provision in the package — behind only the publication requirement, ahead of the family ban the article already carries, and thirty-one points ahead of the congressional-reversal clause the corpus rejected on 2026-08-18 ([PARDON_CONGRESSIONAL_DISAPPROVAL_CLAUSE.md](PARDON_CONGRESSIONAL_DISAPPROVAL_CLAUSE.md)). A package that drafts the family ban at a 57% Republican floor and excludes the self-pardon ban at 61% is not internally consistent.

**The norm has been Strong throughout, in both instruments and in 2018.** Asked whether it is appropriate or inappropriate for a president to pardon themself, 76% said inappropriate in May 2026 — 64% of Republicans, 76% of independents, 88% of Democrats ([2026 crosstabs, table 5A](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results_P22Pm9s.pdf)) — against 77% inappropriate in August 2024, with Republicans at 63% ([2024 crosstabs, table 5A](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results.pdf)). The norm number barely moved (63 → 64 among Republicans) while the remedy number moved eighteen points. In 2018, before any live beneficiary, 85% called a self-pardon unacceptable if the president were charged with a crime, with the AP's write-up reporting about 75% of Republicans and above 90% of Democrats ([AP-NORC topline, June 13–18, 2018, CORR2–CORR3](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/June-2018-AP-NORC-Poll-Topline_Release2.pdf); party split via [AP/PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/most-americans-oppose-presidential-self-pardons-poll-finds); [project page](https://apnorc.org/projects/american-attitudes-toward-international-relations/)).

**Advocacy-commissioned, and it now brackets rather than exceeds the independent reading.** YouGov for Protect Democracy, May–June 2024, n=1,200 validated voters in 43 battleground House districts, ±3.3: 78% disagreed that "presidents should pardon themselves" — 59% of Republicans, 76% of independents, 96% of Democrats ([Protect Democracy](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/survey-jan-6-pardon-opposition/)). Labeled advocacy; it is a norm statement, not a ban proposal, and its 59% Republican reading now sits two points below the independent 2026 ban number rather than well above it.

### Nuances and caveats

- **One reading cannot separate three explanations.** The eighteen-point rise is consistent with (a) genuine movement toward the rule, (b) the removal of a specific plausible beneficiary from respondents' minds, and (c) regime coding, which would predict Republican support for a limit on the presidency to *fall* rather than rise under a president of their own party — the direction that makes explanation (c) the least likely of the three, and worth stating plainly, since it cuts in the clause's favor. The 2025 family-pardon reading shows the mechanism operating in both parties. No wording in either instrument controls for it, which is precisely why the gate is a replication and not an acceptance.
- **The 2024 reading was never re-verified as stale — it is the same pollster and the same question.** This is the cleanest two-point series in the pardon corpus: same house, same battery format, same universe (national adult citizens), twenty-one months apart. It removes the sample-frame and house-effect objections that muddy the conspirators clause's 50-vs-43 comparison. What remains is time and alignment.
- **A rise from uncertainty is softer than a rise from opposition.** Republican "not sure" fell eleven points (27 → 16) while opposition fell seven (29 → 22). Support built partly out of respondents who previously had no view, and unformed support is the most movable kind under a costs-named follow-up — which has never been run on this item in either year.
- **The public does not know the answer to the underlying legal question, and that is an argument for the clause rather than against it.** Asked whether the president has the power to pardon themselves for a federal conviction, respondents in 2026 split 28% yes / 36% no / 36% not sure ([2026 crosstabs, Q1](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results_P22Pm9s.pdf)); in 2024 the split was 26 / 38 / 37 ([2024 crosstabs, table 1A](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Presidential_Pardons_poll_results.pdf)). Roughly a third of the country is unsure whether the Constitution already forbids what the clause would forbid.
- **The generic wrapper remains far weaker than the specific rule.** "Should the Constitution be amended to limit the president's ability to grant pardons?" drew 56% overall — 34% of Republicans, 58% of independents, 76% of Democrats ([Economist/YouGov, Dec. 5–8, 2025, table 46](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_qLhWxpK.pdf); [write-up](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53702-majorities-americans-say-donald-trump-gives-too-many-pardons-want-to-limit-pardon-power-december-5-8-2025-economist-yougov-poll)). That instrument contains no self-pardon item. The lesson the corpus already drew holds: anything that reads as aimed at a sitting president collapses inside that president's party, and the specific categorical rule outperforms the general grant of power to limit.
- **Named-individual polling stays excluded.** A self-pardon by a named president drew 68% opposition in January 2021 ([ABC News/Washington Post via Langer Research, Q16](https://www.langerresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/1219a2TransitiontoBiden.pdf)) and 55% disapproval in June 2023 ([Yahoo News/YouGov](https://www.yahoo.com/news/can-trump-pardon-himself-if-he-wins-in-2024-202946662.html)); neither published a party breakdown, and both measure a person rather than a rule ([§11](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)).

## 3. The gate result

**Floor: 61% (Republicans, YouGov May 13–15, 2026) — Strong under [§4.1](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) on the newest reading, against 43% — Weak — on the identical wording from the same pollster in August 2024.** The verdict the corpus can defend today is therefore neither Strong nor Weak on its own terms but **instrument-dependent**: the clause has cleared the reopening threshold [SELF_PARDON_BAN_REJECTED.md §5](../../rejected/SELF_PARDON_BAN_REJECTED.md) named — about 60% Republican with Democrats holding above 60% — on one independent measurement, and the method does not accept a single reading as a verdict where a prior reading of the same wording landed eighteen points lower.

**Failure modes ([§4.3](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)):**

- **High-topline / low-floor — cleared on the 2026 reading, failed on the 2024 one.** The 2026 topline-to-floor gap is eleven points (72 to 61) with a 24-point party gap; in 2024 it was 21 points (64 to 43) with a 38-point party gap. The gap narrowed on both measures.
- **Incumbent / regime coding — the open failure mode, and the reason for the gate.** The two readings sit in opposite alignments, and the corpus's adjacent family-pardon series shows each party's floor moving with its own president's conduct. Forward-looking, both-parties wording has never been fielded on this item in either year.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — closing, and this is the substantive finding.** In 2024, 63% of Republicans called a self-pardon inappropriate while 43% backed the prohibition — a twenty-point gap between condemning the act and adopting the rule. In 2026, 64% called it inappropriate and 61% backed the prohibition: a three-point gap. The norm held steady and the remedy caught up to it. That is the pattern [§4.3](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md) describes as the gap closing rather than the topline inflating.
- **Framing dependency — materially reduced but not eliminated.** The 2024 memo's central objection was that the framings clearing the bar described the act while the framing that would be drafted described the rule. In 2026 the drafted framing clears the bar on its own. The generic pardon-power wrapper still reads 34% Republican, so the family of questions remains framing-sensitive at the level of specificity, not at the level of act-versus-rule.
- **Depth — untested, in both years.** No survey has put a cost to respondents: that the rule would also bind a president of the respondent's own party, or that it would resolve by text a question the courts have never answered. This is the largest unmeasured quantity.
- **Staleness — cleared.** Three months.

**Non-polling tests ([§5](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md)) — all three passed at rejection and are unchanged.**

- **Existential-problem — passes.** A president who may pardon himself is, as to federal crime, outside the law he executes. The 1974 OLC memorandum states the principle in one line.
- **Symmetry — passes by construction, and more cleanly than any other clause in the package.** The text names no party, binds every future president identically, and no president of either party has ever attempted a self-pardon, so there is no partisan abuse record to inherit. Its packaging risk is inherited rather than intrinsic: the only legislative vehicle carrying it has been a single-caucus artifact since 2017.
- **Why-an-amendment — passes, as the parent's, with an extra step.** The pardon power is constitutional text; *Ex parte Garland* and *Schick v. Reed* hold that Congress cannot abridge it by statute, and *Trump v. United States* named it among the conclusive and preclusive powers ([opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-939_e2pg.pdf)). Bauer and Goldsmith argue Congress could declare self-pardons ineffective by statute ([Lawfare, Feb. 26, 2020](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/how-reform-pardon-power)); the foundation's answer is that a statute can regulate conduct around a pardon but cannot make one void, and that the amendment's distinctive contribution here is settling a question the text does not expressly address — the method's fourth accepted answer at [§5.3](../../SELECTION_METHOD.md).

## 4. Poll question

Fielded by an independent pollster in the same instrument as the article's other provisions — the foundation's designated first commissioned poll on the pardon package — so the self-pardon item is measured within respondents against publication, naming, family, and sale. The core item is written forward-looking and for both parties, which is the one control neither the 2024 nor the 2026 instrument applied.

**Core wording (verbatim):**

> *"Some have proposed adding a sentence to the Constitution reading: 'The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to himself or herself.' This would apply to every future president, of either party, including presidents you vote for. Would you support or oppose adding this to the Constitution?"*

**Follow-ups, in order:**

1. *Costs named (the depth test):* "Supporters say no one should be able to pardon himself, and that leaving the question unsettled invites a crisis no court has ever resolved. Opponents say the Constitution may already forbid it, that no president has ever tried, and that amending the Constitution over a hypothetical is a poor use of the process. Having heard both, do you support or oppose the rule?"
2. *Symmetry probe:* the core item asked in split samples, one naming no president and one reading "including a president of your own party who you believe was prosecuted unfairly," to size the regime-coding effect the 2024→2026 movement leaves unresolved.
3. *Norm anchor, carried unchanged for comparability:* "Do you think it is appropriate or inappropriate for a president to pardon themself?" — the verbatim YouGov item from both 2024 and 2026.
4. *Remedy anchor, carried unchanged:* "Would you support or oppose prohibiting a president from pardoning themself?" — the verbatim YouGov 6B/7B item, so the commissioned instrument extends the two-point series to three rather than starting a new one.

**Threshold: ≥60% among Republicans and ≥60% among Democrats on the core item, holding at ≥55% in both parties after the costs-named follow-up.** That is the reopening threshold the rejection memo set, applied once more to a forward-looking instrument. A result of 45–59% among Republicans on the core item keeps the clause a candidate and is a reason to draft it as a severable section rather than to drop it, given that the family ban already in the article reads 57% Republican. A Republican result below 45% — a return to the 2024 reading under forward-looking wording — would establish that the 2026 number was alignment-driven and would move the clause to rejected, with the OLC opinion and the statutory route as the record of where the principle then lives.

## 5. Recommendation

**Candidate clause, reopened.** The rejection of 2026-08-06 was a polling result and nothing else, and the specific number that produced it has been replaced by a higher one from the same pollster on the same wording: 61% Republican and 85% Democratic against 43% and 81%, clearing the ~60% Republican threshold the rejection memo itself named as the condition for reopening. The abuse-vs-remedy gap that was the central diagnosis has narrowed from twenty points to three, and the clause now polls above the family ban the article already drafts.

It is a candidate rather than a drafted provision because a single reading cannot establish that an eighteen-point move is durable. The gate is one independent replication in forward-looking, both-parties wording clearing 60% among Republicans — no more than that, and it should be fielded in the same commissioned instrument as the rest of the package. If it clears, the clause is drafted into the article as its own section rather than left to ride inside the self-benefit provision; if it returns to the 2024 reading, the honest conclusion is that the 2026 number measured an alignment rather than a rule.
