# Stopping Unlawful Executive Action — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** 37% (Republicans) on "agencies and spending authorized by Congress are beyond the president's authority to close or freeze," Marquette May 2025 — **Weak** on the spending sub-item, unpolled on executive privilege, and Moderate-but-regime-coded on immunity — held by the symmetry test on appropriations and by regime-coding on immunity — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: a forward-looking, both-directions appropriations question clearing 60% R, which would fold it into Target 7).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

This category is the remainder left over after Target 7. Target 7 (Executive Compliance with SCOTUS) already carries the court-obedience half, resting on the strongest floor in the corpus after term limits: 84% say a president must obey the Supreme Court — **78% R / 76% I / 92% D** ([Marquette, Nov 2025](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/11/20/mlspsc29-court/)); Pew's April 2025 companion finds 78% say the administration must follow a federal court's ruling — **65% R / 91% D** ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/04/23/views-of-how-the-trump-administration-governs/)). Drafting for that half lives in [EXECUTIVE_COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md](../EXECUTIVE_COMPLIANCE_RESEARCH.md).

What remains — and what this memo evaluates — is the three sub-items [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) lists under this candidate, drafted by Michael Hamilton in the "Executive-accountability drafts" section of [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) (added 2026-08-06):

**(a) Power-of-the-purse dominance.** Four related drafts: foreign-affairs authority confers no discretion to withhold or condition funds Congress has directed; an absolute Appropriations Clause rule that no emergency, transfer, or commander-in-chief authority overrides a congressional decision to deny or limit funding; a supermajority requirement for large reallocations during a declared emergency; and the Appropriations Fidelity Clause already drafted and held back inside Target 7 — *"Funds appropriated by law shall be made available for obligation and expenditure as provided by law… no appropriation shall be withheld, delayed beyond its period of availability, or terminated except by law."* The public thread that fed this pipeline reached the same place: u/TacosAndBourbon's comment (22 points, sixth-ranked of 478) named frozen funding and closed agencies as the core grievance ([REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md)).

**(b) Limits on executive privilege for oversight.** Stripping the ability to assert privilege against congressional investigations into impoundment, election interference, or the conditioning of foreign aid.

**(c) Narrowing executive immunity.** Clarifying that presidential immunity does not reach acts performed in a candidate capacity — efforts to alter election returns, pressure state election boards, or disrupt the transfer of power. This sub-item exists because of a constitutional holding: [*Trump v. United States*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-939) (July 1, 2024, 6–3) gave former presidents absolute immunity for conduct within the President's "exclusive sphere of constitutional authority," "at least presumptive immunity" for other official acts, and none for unofficial acts.

The three sub-items do not share a gate result, and this memo does not treat them as one proposal.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Marquette Law School Poll, May 5–15, 2025 (n=1,004 adults).** "Do you think agencies and spending that have been authorized by Congress are beyond the president's authority to close or freeze, or do you think the president has the authority to take these actions without congressional approval?" — **62% beyond the president's authority / 38% has the authority**, by party **37% R / 64% I / 88% D** saying beyond (so **63% of Republicans say the president has the authority**). Little changed from March 2025 (64% beyond) — [Marquette](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/05/22/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-strong-support-for-authority-of-courts-in-cases-involving-president/). **The floor number for sub-item (a)**, and the only independent national party-split reading of the spending question in the record.
- **PRRI American Values Survey, Aug. 15–Sept. 8, 2025 (n=5,543).** "When decisions by Congress or the Supreme Court hold our country back, the president should be able to ignore them" — **16% agree / 78% disagree — 24% R / 14% I / 10% D agree**, i.e. a ~76% Republican floor on the abstract principle that a president may not ignore *Congress or* the courts. Same survey: 46% say checks and balances are "working as it should" — **70% R / 42% I / 28% D** — [PRRI](https://prri.org/research/trumps-unprecedented-actions-deepen-asymmetric-divides/).
- **Marquette Law School Poll, Jan. 27–Feb. 6, 2025 (n=1,018 adults).** "In July 2024, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that, while there is no immunity for unofficial acts, former presidents have broad immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts as president. How much do you favor or oppose this decision?" — **39% favor / 61% oppose**, by party **68% R favor / 31% I / 13% D favor**. Read as opposition to the holding, the Republican floor is **32%** — [Marquette](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/02/13/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-approval-of-u-s-supreme-court-above-50-for-first-time-since-march-2022/). §4 shows what that number does when the rule is put as an amendment rather than as a verdict on a decision.
- **Marquette Law School Poll, July 22–29, 2026 (n=1,076 adults).** Forward-looking wording: "Would you like to see the **next** president have more control over federal departments and agencies, less control, or about as much control as the president has today?" — **18% more / 45% less / 36% about the same**, by party **R 25/20/55 · I 21/46/33 · D 9/72/18** — [Marquette](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/08/05/marquette-law-school-poll-finds-national-approval-of-supreme-court-now-at-47-has-remained-below-50-since-november-2025/). The newest reading in the record and the only one written about a future officeholder: even so, 20% of Republicans want less presidential control over agencies.
- **AP-NORC, April 17–21, 2025 (n=1,260 adults).** 57% say the president "has gone too far in using presidential power to achieve his goals," 30% "about right," 10% "not using them enough"; **62% of Republicans say "about right."** 50% say the president has too much power, up from 33% in March 2024 — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/many-feel-trump-has-gone-too-far-in-using-presidential-power-to-achieve-goals/), whose framing notes concern about executive power tracks which party holds it.
- **USA Today / Ipsos, Aug. 2–4, 2024 (n=1,024; 282 R, 324 D, 310 I).** Support for "enacting a Constitutional amendment to make clear that no person is above the law, even the President of the United States" — **54% of Republicans**, with majorities of Democrats and independents (party figures for D and I not published as numbers) — [Ipsos](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/most-americans-believe-presidential-election-will-have-major-impact-future-supreme-court). The only amendment-framed item found for (c), and the only one on which the Republican number is a majority.
- **CBS News/YouGov, Oct. 1–3, 2025 (n=2,441 adults, ±2.3).** "Which of these should have the most authority to decide how the U.S. government spends money?" — **72% Congress / 19% the president / 9% the courts**, by party **59% R / 77% I / 82% D** choosing Congress (Republicans: 37% the president, 4% the courts). The article reports only that majorities of all three groups say Congress; the percentages are in the released topline and crosstabs, recovered 2026-08-18 — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/cbs-news-poll-shutdown-trump-democrats-republicans/), [topline and crosstabs (Q27)](https://www.scribd.com/doc/928352494/Cbsnews-20251005-Shutdown). **This is now the highest Republican floor in the category on an appropriations question — 59%** — and it belongs in the "with party breakdowns" tier rather than below it. Two cautions keep it from settling the gate on its own: it is a *forced-choice allocation of authority among three institutions*, not a favor/oppose on a binding rule, so it measures which branch should decide rather than whether a president may disobey the decision; and it was fielded on the third day of a shutdown, when the salience of congressional spending authority was at a local maximum.

**National polls without party breakdowns**

- **YouGov, Feb. 12, 2025 (n=2,809 adults).** "If a president wants to withhold funds that Congress has passed a law to spend, what should happen?" — **46% the funds should be spent / 24% the funds should be withheld / 30% not sure** — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/daily-results/20250212-b0e7a-1). (No party breakdown published.) The cleanest principle-level impoundment wording in the record — no officeholder named, no party cue — and **30% were not sure**, the highest don't-know share of any item here. The question is not merely under-polled; it is under-known.
- **Economist/YouGov, June 2024.** Only **20%** of registered voters agree "presidents should have legal immunity protecting them from being charged for any actions they have taken as president"; 63% disagree. The split published is by 2024 vote choice, not party (38% of Trump voters vs. 8% of Biden voters) — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50017-supreme-court-2024-rulings-what-americans-think-poll). Immunity for *any* action overstates the holding, so this bounds the extreme position rather than reading the decision.

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **States United Democracy Center / YouGov, March 4–6, 2025 (n=1,524 adults; States United is a democracy-advocacy organization, YouGov fielded).** Whether "President Trump should be able to withhold money from federal agencies without congressional approval" — 26% agree / 56% disagree overall; **Republicans 47% agree / 27% disagree**; 84% D and 54% I disagree. On withholding disaster relief from states over policy disagreements, 15% agree overall; 55% R / 64% I / 86% D disagree — [States United](https://statesunited.org/resources/withholding-funds-poll/). Officeholder-named, so regime-coded by construction; brackets the Republican floor on withholding at roughly 27–55% depending on framing.
- **Issue One / YouGov, Aug. 25–29, 2025 (n=1,070 registered voters; Issue One is a democracy-reform advocacy organization, YouGov fielded).** The battery asked directly about allowing presidents "to unilaterally withhold or block funds that have been enacted into law by Congress" and "to redirect military or disaster funding without a vote in Congress"; separately, after a neutral explainer, every checks-and-balances feature tested — including "the congressional power of the purse" — drew **at least 67%** saying it strengthens government, fewer than 19% skeptical. **The memo publishes these as charts without printed percentages, and no toplines or crosstabs PDF could be located** — re-checked against the full memo text on 2026-08-18, and the withhold/redirect items appear only in the "existing norms" battery narrative and an unlabeled chart, so those numbers and their party breakdowns remain unavailable — [YouGov memo to Issue One, Sept. 17, 2025](https://issueone.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/YouGov-IPMemo-9.12.25.pdf); [press release](https://issueone.org/press/new-poll-finds-voters-concerned-about-presidential-power/). Two figures in the memo *are* printed and bear on this category: **78% of voters agree congressional oversight strengthens the political system**, and on a forced choice between working within checks and balances "even if it means the majority doesn't always get its way" and letting "a handful of elected leaders simply pass the policies the majority voted for," **47% of Republicans** chose the checks-and-balances side. The closest existing instrument to the question the foundation would commission.

**Legal and legislative record (not opinion evidence, but §4.2 evidence of where the parties actually stand)**

- **The statutory settlement is failing in practice.** GAO concluded in [B-337805 (Sept. 12, 2025)](https://www.gao.gov/products/b-337805) that "the ICA does not permit the withholding of funds past their date of expiration, even if the 45-day period for congressional consideration has not passed"; the funds lapsed anyway. Two weeks later, in [*Department of State v. AIDS Vaccine Advocacy Coalition* (Sept. 26, 2025)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/25A269), the Court stayed the injunction requiring obligation of roughly $4B in expiring foreign aid, reasoning that the ICA "precludes respondents' suit, brought pursuant to the Administrative Procedure Act" because the statute gives the Comptroller General a way to sue (three Justices dissented) — leaving the remedy with one officer, prospectively.
- **Live bills point opposite directions, with no cross-party vote either way.** The Congressional Power of the Purse Act ([H.R. 5220](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5220)) would ban pocket rescissions and penalize knowing violations, every original cosponsor from one party; [S. 515](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/515) and [H.R. 1180](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1180) would *repeal* the Impoundment Control Act on the theory that presidents may constitutionally spend less than appropriated, sponsored from the other. The immunity amendment resolutions are similarly one-sided.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The abuse-vs-remedy gap is the defining feature of this category.** PRRI finds a ~76% Republican floor on "a president may not ignore Congress or the courts"; Marquette finds 37% on the concrete application of that principle to spending and agencies. Both independent pollsters, both fielded 2025. The 39-point drop is the distance between a principle and a mechanism, not measurement error.
- **Everything here is regime-coded, and the record shows the flip directly.** AP-NORC's 33%→50% jump on "the president has too much power" between March 2024 and April 2025 is that migration measured. The immunity numbers are the sharpest case: the Republican share favoring the *Trump v. United States* holding (68%) and the Republican share backing an amendment that "no person is above the law, even the President" (54%) come from the same eighteen-month window and point opposite directions.
- **The forward-looking re-poll the method calls for has partially been run, and it did not rescue the category.** Marquette's July 2026 "next president" wording is the §11 trap-catalog remedy applied; Republicans still split 25% more / 20% less / 55% same on presidential control over agencies.
- **Wording sensitivity is extreme on the spending item.** Across four instruments the Republican number runs 27% (States United, disaster-relief framing), 37% (Marquette, agencies-and-spending), 47% agreeing the president *should* be able to withhold (States United, general), and 59% choosing Congress over the president on spending authority (CBS, Oct. 2025 crosstabs) — no two the same question, and a 32-point spread inside twelve months. The two independent readings alone, Marquette's 37% and CBS's 59%, differ by 22 points on what is nominally the same subject; the difference is that Marquette asks whether the president has *authority to close or freeze* what Congress funded, while CBS asks which branch *should decide* spending in the abstract. That is the same abuse-vs-remedy geometry seen elsewhere in the corpus, appearing here inside one policy area.
- **Executive privilege has never been polled at all.** Searches on 2026-08-18 for national polling on executive privilege against congressional oversight, subpoena enforcement, or testimonial immunity returned no survey instrument from any pollster, independent or advocacy. Not a thin record — an empty one.
- **The one clean impoundment question ever asked found 30% "not sure"** — a salience finding: most Americans have not formed a view on the mechanism at the centre of sub-item (a).

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **37% (Republicans)** saying agencies and spending authorized by Congress are beyond the president's authority to close or freeze, Marquette May 2025 → **Weak** per §4.1. **(b) executive privilege: Unpolled** — no national poll asks the proposal, with or without a party breakdown; the closest proxies are the Issue One oversight battery (78% say congressional oversight strengthens the system, no party figures published) and PRRI's abstract "may not ignore Congress" item at a ~76% R floor — proxies, because neither names privilege. **(c) immunity: 32% R** opposing the *Trump v. United States* holding (Marquette Jan–Feb 2025) → Weak as a verdict on the decision, but **54% R** on the amendment framing (USA Today/Ipsos, Aug. 2024) → **Moderate**; §4 explains why the second is the honest read.

- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — **applies squarely to (a).** 62% overall conceals 37% R / 88% D, a 51-point gap — the same shape as the Electoral College rejection (63% overall, 46R/80D).
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **applies to all three sub-items** and is the binding constraint on (c): the immunity readings invert depending on whether the question is a verdict on a decision benefiting the sitting president (68% R favor) or a rule for whoever comes next (54% R back the amendment). AP-NORC's 33%→50% shift across an administration change is the same effect on a different item.
  - *Framing dependency* — **applies.** The Republican number for constraining executive spending discretion runs 27% to a bare majority across four instruments in one year; the immunity number moves 22 points between two framings of one rule.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **applies, and is the category's signature.** ~76% R on "may not ignore Congress" (PRRI) versus 37% R on the operative application (Marquette).
  - *Staleness* — **cleared.** 2024–2026 fieldwork throughout, including a July 2026 reading.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **not tested.** No instrument has named the costs of an anti-impoundment rule — that it would equally bar a future president from spending or forgiving beyond an appropriation. Untested is not passed.

- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem* — **passes for (a) and (c).** Whether appropriations bind the executive is a structural question about which branch controls the purse, not a policy preference, and the GAO opinion plus the *AVAC* stay show the statutory settlement failing in practice; whether criminal law reaches a president is a rule-of-law question at the mission's core. **(b) is the weakest of the three** — oversight-privilege disputes recur, but the case that they threaten the country's future has not been made.
  - *Symmetry* — **open, and decisive for (a).** §5.2 uses this exact category as its worked example: the same clause "would have blocked Biden-era spending beyond appropriations and border-wall reprogramming — if it can't carry both, it drops to the statutory list." The materials to write it symmetrically exist — the Antideficiency Act (31 U.S.C. §§1341, 1342, 1517) bars spending in excess of or before appropriations, and [*Biden v. Nebraska*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-506) (June 30, 2023, 6–3) held the HEROES Act did not authorize "canceling $430 billion of student loan principal" — so a "neither less nor more than Congress appropriated" clause has receipts pointing both ways. What does not exist is *evidence that the symmetric version polls*: no instrument has asked it. Open, not failed — and closable only by fieldwork. **(c)** is harder to write symmetrically: a candidate-capacity carve-out is drafted against one fact pattern, with no matched pairing identified.
  - *Why-an-amendment* — **splits by sub-item, which is why they cannot be recommended together.** For **(a)**, presently **weak**: *Train v. City of New York* (1975, unanimous) and the ICA already say the President must spend what Congress appropriates, so the problem is remedial rather than textual, and §5.3 routes "Congress can fix this by statute" to the statutory list — H.R. 5220 would close the pocket-rescission hole by ordinary law. That *AVAC* channeled enforcement to one officer while one party moves to repeal the ICA is an argument about durability, not textual silence. For **(b)**, **weak**: privilege is common-law, and Congress has never enacted a subpoena-enforcement mechanism it could enact tomorrow. For **(c)**, **strong and unambiguous**: *Trump v. United States* is a constitutional holding about Article II's scope that no statute can narrow — the one place in the category where only an amendment will do.

Two findings sit in this table. The first is the **abuse-framing/remedy-framing gap**, unusually wide here: the same electorate that rejects "a president may ignore Congress" by ~76% among Republicans declines the operative version of that principle at 37%. The corpus has seen this before — DOJ independence (60% oppose the abuse, 36% R on the remedy) and FCC (61% R on the abuse, 39% R on the removal remedy) — and both times it produced a candidate rather than a target.

The second is that **immunity is the only sub-item where a framing clears honestly.** The 54% R figure is not an abuse framing dressed as a remedy: "no person is above the law, even the President" *is* the operative principle, put as an amendment, which is what the foundation would draft. Two caveats keep it from carrying a verdict alone — August 2024 fieldwork during a campaign in which the holding was a live issue, and Ipsos published only the Republican number, so the *floor* is confirmed but the distribution is not. A 54% floor is Moderate, and §4.1 accepts Moderate "only when the framing that reaches Strong is identifiable and honest." None has been identified for immunity.

For appropriations, no framing clears **60%**, and the spread across framings is the finding rather than any single number: the symmetric "neither less nor more than Congress appropriated" version **has never been asked**, and the adjacent framings that have been asked land between 20% and 59% among Republicans. The top of that range — CBS's 59% R for Congress over the president on spending authority — sits one point below the threshold §5 sets, which is closer than this category has previously come, but it is reached by a question that allocates authority among three institutions rather than one that binds a president to an appropriation, and it was fielded during a shutdown. Two instruments asking recognisably about congressional spending power in the same year return 37% R (Marquette) and 59% R (CBS); until that 22-point gap is explained by wording rather than averaged away, it is a framing-dependency finding, not a floor.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** For (a), one commissioned question — forward-looking, explicitly two-directional, fielded by an independent pollster: *"Some propose a constitutional rule that any future president must spend the money Congress has appropriated — no more and no less — and may not withhold, delay, or cancel funds without Congress voting to change the law. This would bind future presidents of both parties equally: it would bar a president from freezing funds Congress approved, and equally bar a president from spending or forgiving money Congress never appropriated. Do you favor or oppose?"* The "no more and no less" phrase and the paired both-directions sentence are the whole design — they convert the question from a referendum on the incumbent into the veil-of-ignorance rule §5.2 demands. **Threshold: 60% among Republicans**, holding within the Moderate band under a costs-named follow-up. At that result, (a) folds into Target 7 as the Appropriations Fidelity Clause already drafted and held back there. A cross-party roll-call — any recorded vote in which majorities of both parties back a pocket-rescission ban — is arguably the stronger signal, since it would test elite as well as public symmetry.

For (c), a re-ask of the USA Today/Ipsos amendment wording with **full party breakdowns published**, fielded outside a presidential campaign, clearing 60% R. For (b), any national poll at all: the first task is not clearing a threshold but establishing that a measurable opinion exists.

**Evidence that would move it out.** For (a): a symmetric, forward-looking question that still returns a Republican floor below 45% would reject it on **high-topline/low-floor** — the test that rejected the Electoral College — and confirm the **symmetry** failure §5.2 anticipated, routing it to the statutory list regardless of the number. For (b): if the first poll ever fielded shows the remedy splitting by party the way DOJ independence does, record it as rejected on **abuse-vs-remedy** rather than held. For (c): a re-ask of the amendment framing falling below 45% R once the incumbent is out of view would show the 54% was regime-coded in the opposite direction, moving it out on **incumbent coding**.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate**, with the sub-items gated separately — they are not one proposal.

Sub-item (a) is held on the **symmetry** test, exactly as §5.2 predicted when it named this category as its worked example. The polling is Weak (37% R, Marquette May 2025), but that number was measured on an incumbent-coded application, not on the symmetric rule the foundation would draft — and the symmetric rule has never been asked. Rejecting on 37% would reject a question we have not put; accepting on the ~76% PRRI figure would commit the §11 error of attaching a broad-principle number to a specific mechanism. The why-an-amendment answer is also weak here (§3), so even a Strong floor would argue for folding into Target 7 rather than a standalone amendment.

Sub-item (b), executive privilege, is held as **genuinely unpolled** and is the weakest of the three: it does not clear the existential-problem test on the current record and has no why-an-amendment answer. If one sub-item is to be dropped, this is it.

Sub-item (c), immunity, is held on **regime-coding** despite carrying the category's only honest clearing framing (54% R) and its only strong why-an-amendment answer. The 54% is Moderate, single-sourced, campaign-window fieldwork with an incomplete party breakdown. One clean re-ask could move it to a target; it is the highest-value item in this category's commissioned-survey queue.

Founder decides.

## Review Additions (2026-08-18)

- **CBS News/YouGov's spending-authority percentages, recovered from the released crosstabs.** See §2 and §4: **72% say Congress should have the most authority to decide how the government spends money, 19% the president, 9% the courts — 59% R / 77% I / 82% D for Congress** (fieldwork Oct. 1–3, 2025, n=2,441 adults, ±2.3) — [topline and crosstabs, Q27](https://www.scribd.com/doc/928352494/Cbsnews-20251005-Shutdown). The memo previously recorded this as "majorities of all parties, percentages never published." **This bears on the gate result for sub-item (a)** and is the most consequential recovery in this memo: it is the highest Republican reading in the category on appropriations, and it moves CBS from the "without party breakdowns" tier into the §4.2 first tier. It does **not** change the verdict. The §3 floor stays at 37% R (Marquette, May 2025), because Marquette asks the operative question — whether agencies and spending authorized by Congress are beyond the president's authority to close or freeze — while CBS asks which branch should decide in the abstract, and §11 forbids attaching a general-authority number to a specific mechanism. What the recovery does change is the shape of the open question: the category now has two independent 2025 readings 22 points apart, so the framing-dependency finding in §4 is sharper and the commissioned question in §5 should carry both wordings as arms of one instrument.
- **Reuters/Ipsos February 2025 funding-freeze figures, verified at the pollster.** 34% support / 62% oppose "temporarily freezing federal funding for government grants and services in the U.S.," fieldwork **Jan. 31–Feb. 2, 2025**, n=1,032 adults — [Ipsos](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/support-remains-mixed-executive-orders-and-actions).
