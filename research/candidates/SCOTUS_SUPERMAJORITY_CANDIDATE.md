# Supermajority Requirement for the Supreme Court to Strike Down Federal Laws — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-18. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled — no independent national survey has ever asked the mechanism with a party breakdown — held by regime coding (Court approval runs 75R/11D, Gallup July 2025) and by an unresolved blast-radius problem the 2021 Presidential Commission names in its own text — recommendation: hold (gate: one forward-looking both-parties poll of the mechanism, written so neither side knows which Court it binds).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The idea is a voting rule, not a structural one: the Supreme Court could hold an Act of Congress unconstitutional only with the concurrence of more than a bare majority — 6–3, 7–2, or two-thirds — on the theory that a statute has already cleared both houses and a presidential signature, so a one-vote judicial majority should not be enough to undo it. Nothing in the Constitution or the Judiciary Act of 1789 sets the Court's voting rule; the 2021 Presidential Commission records that "[f]rom the beginning, ... the Court appears to have assumed that a simple majority vote was sufficient to determine its rulings" ([Final Report, Dec. 2021, ch. 4, p. 170](https://roar-assets-auto.rbl.ms/documents/35825/SCOTUS-Report-Final.pdf)).

The design space has three axes that matter more than the headline number. **Threshold:** 6–3 through 9–0. **Coverage:** federal statutes only; federal statutes plus executive action; or all federal *and state* lawmaking — the Commission states the trade-off plainly: "the more actions to which the supermajority voting requirement applied, the greater the shift in influence to other institutions" (p. 175). **Instrument:** statute or amendment (Section 3).

The pedigree is long, and both coalitions have reached for it when the Court ruled against them. The first congressional proposal came in 1823; Prof. Evan Caminker's catalogue — the most complete available — lists **sixty-three** congressional supermajority proposals, with the caveat that it "may not include every original supermajority proposal ever introduced" ([*Thayerian Deference to Congress and Supreme Court Supermajority Rules*, 78 Ind. L.J. 73 (2003), n.52 and Appendix](https://ilj.law.indiana.edu/articles/78/78_1_Caminker.pdf)). During Reconstruction the House passed such a bill and the Senate did not act on it (Commission Report, p. 170). The most discussed version is Sen. William Borah's, February 1923, requiring that "at least seven members of the court concur before pronouncing said law unconstitutional" (S. 4483, 67th Cong.; quoted in Caminker n.9 and Commission Report, pp. 170–71); he reintroduced a version reaching state statutes the next session (S. 1197, 68th Cong.), and a 1967 bill would have required six Justices for federal *or* state law (Commission Report, p. 171). The parallel Progressive-era proposal ran the other way: the 1924 Progressive Party platform asked for "a constitutional amendment providing that congress may by enacting a statute make it effective over a judicial veto" — an override, not a threshold ([platform text, Nov. 4, 1924](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/progressive-party-platform-of-1924-resolutions-committee/)). None has ever been enacted.

Three states adopted the rule for their own supreme courts in the same era. **Nebraska**: "No legislative act shall be held unconstitutional except by the concurrence of five judges" — five of seven, adopted 1920, still in force ([Neb. Const. art. V, §2](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/articles.php?article=V-2)). **North Dakota**: the supreme court "shall not declare a legislative enactment unconstitutional unless at least four of the members of the court so decide" — four of five, adopted 1919, still in force ([N.D. Const. art. VI, §§2, 4](https://ndlegis.gov/constit/a06.pdf)). **Ohio** adopted a six-of-seven rule in 1912 and repealed it in 1968 (Section 2).

**Pipeline entry.** Raised by **Alex MacGregor** (text message, 2026-08-18), recorded in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md), with the framing note that it can be pitched as "limiting the power of activist judges." Michael Hamilton's response: saved, but "the problem is it is new and not popular yet." Filed as a candidate the same day.

**Overlap with existing work.** Court *structure* — the number of seats — is the rejected Supreme Court Expansion category (39% R floor; see [COURT_EXPANSION_REJECTED.md](../rejected/COURT_EXPANSION_REJECTED.md)). Court *tenure and conduct* — eighteen-year fixed terms on biennial appointments, and an enforceable ethics code — are Target 2 and Target 3, resting on floors of 73% R and 88% R (Marquette 2026; YouGov 2024). This category is neither. It is the Court's **decision rule**, which no target touches and which the expansion memo never evaluated. The remainder to be judged here is therefore the whole of it: whether the threshold for invalidating a federal statute belongs in the Constitution.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls that ask the mechanism**

- **None.** No independent national poll asking whether the Supreme Court should need a supermajority to strike down a federal law, with or without a party breakdown, could be located. Searched 2026-08-18 across Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, YouGov/Economist, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI and UMD PPC, and across the advocacy aggregators (Brennan Center, Fix the Court, Data for Progress, Navigator, Verasight). The Commission's 2021 report, the February 2026 CRS product and the May–July 2026 commentary wave all discuss the mechanism at length and **none cites a poll of it** — including its most prominent current advocate, whose article proposing it contains no public-opinion data ([Brennan Center, May 11, 2026](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/why-5-4)).

**The nearest miss — a full reform battery that omits the item**

- **YouGov, May 4–6, 2026** (n=1,127 adult citizens). A six-item Supreme Court reform battery asked support/oppose on an ethics code, maximum years of service, a partisan-balance requirement, **a two-thirds Senate supermajority to confirm a nominee**, one appointment per presidential term, and expanding the Court. The confirmation-supermajority item — the closest thing anyone has asked — drew **51% support / 20% oppose / 30% not sure; Republicans 44% support (31% oppose), independents 41% (17%), Democrats 71% (10%)**. The same battery's ethics code ran 79% and maximum-years 74%; expansion ran 33%. **A supermajority to strike down a law was not among the six items.** — [toplines and crosstabs, Q8/Q8D (PDF)](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Supreme_Court_poll_results.pdf). *Labeled a proxy: this measures appetite for a supermajority at the appointment stage, not at the decision stage; a 44% R floor on the adjacent idea is not evidence for this one (SELECTION_METHOD §11).*

**Proxies on the Court's power (labeled — none is evidence for the mechanism)**

- **Judicial review itself is not contested.** "The Supreme Court has the power to review laws passed by Congress and to declare them invalid if they conflict with the Constitution": **87% yes / 13% no — Republicans 87%, independents 86%, Democrats 86%** — Marquette, Jan. 27–Feb. 6, 2025 (n=1,018) — [release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2025/02/13/new-marquette-law-school-poll-national-survey-finds-approval-of-u-s-supreme-court-above-50-for-first-time-since-march-2022/). The one court-power number with no party gap at all, and it cuts against the premise that the public wants judicial review restrained.
- **"Too much power" is regime-coded, not a demand for a rule.** YouGov, May 4–6, 2026: too much power **30% — 17% R / 32% I / 42% D**; "about the right amount" 49% overall, **68% R** ([crosstabs Q3, p. 11](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Supreme_Court_poll_results.pdf)). AP-NORC, July 10–14, 2025 (n=1,437): **38% — 20% R / 56% D**, up from 29% overall in April 2025 — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/confidence-in-the-supreme-court-is-increasing-but-so-are-concerns-over-its-power/).
- **The flip, shown.** Gallup, July 7–21, 2025 (n=1,002): Court job approval **39% — 75% R / 34% I / 11% D**, a 64-point party gap, the largest in Gallup's trend, exceeding the 61-point record set after *Dobbs* — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/693230/record-party-gaps-job-approval-supreme-court-congress.aspx). AP-NORC's confidence series moves the same way: at least some confidence rose from 82% to 90% among Republicans and 35% to 43% among Democrats between 2022 and July 2025 (AP-NORC, above). Marquette, July 22–29, 2026 (n=1,076): approval **47% — 74% R / 35% I / 22% D** — [release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/08/05/marquette-law-school-poll-finds-national-approval-of-supreme-court-now-at-47-has-remained-below-50-since-november-2025/). Every attitude toward the Court's power currently tracks who appointed the majority.

**Legislative and state record**

- **119th Congress:** H.R. 4124, the Restoring Judicial Separation of Powers Act, carries a supermajority provision — but for *multi-circuit panels*, requiring not less than 70% of judges to affirm a holding that an Act of Congress is invalid, not for the Supreme Court ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/4124/text); the page returned 403 on direct fetch — the provision is quoted from the search index and is listed in `unverified` accordingly). No 119th-Congress bill or joint resolution imposing a Supreme Court supermajority was found.
- **Hearing record:** the House Subcommittee on Courts heard testimony advocating a consensus requirement on May 21, 2026, and the mechanism was the most detailed proposal in a July 29, 2026 survey of court-reform ideas — [SCOTUSblog, July 29, 2026](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/07/the-push-to-reduce-the-supreme-courts-power/). No roll call, no bill number, no cross-party sponsorship identified.
- **Nebraska, operating record.** *Thompson v. Heineman*, 289 Neb. 798 (Jan. 9, 2015): four of seven judges held L.B. 1161 unconstitutional as an unlawful delegation, but because five did not so vote the statute stood — every judge who reached the constitutional question found the law invalid, and the law survived ([Kathleen Miller, "The Fifth Judge," Neb. L. Rev. Bulletin vol. 6 (2015)](https://lawreview.unl.edu/fifth-judge-thompson-v-heineman-and-nebraskas-judicial-supermajority-clause-0/)).
- **North Dakota, operating record.** *Bismarck Public School District No. 1 v. State*, 511 N.W.2d 247, 250 (N.D. 1994): the school finance system was upheld despite three of five justices finding it violated the state constitution (Caminker, n.74).
- **Ohio, the repeal.** The 1912 amendment read: "No law shall be held unconstitutional and void by the supreme court without the concurrence of at least all but one of the judges, except in the affirmance of a judgment of the court of appeals declaring a law unconstitutional and void" (Ohio Const. art. IV, §2 (1912); Caminker, n.63). That carve-out meant a 4–3 vote produced opposite results depending on how the lower court had ruled, leaving the law different in different parts of the state; the protocol was repealed in 1968 by the Modern Courts Amendment (Caminker, pp. 90–91, nn.68–70). One case it decided became *Mapp v. Ohio*: in *State v. Mapp*, 167 N.E.2d 387 (Ohio 1960), four of seven justices would have sustained the challenge and the rule denied them the outcome (Caminker, n.67). The Commission's summary: North Dakota's and Nebraska's rules "have not posed significant operational difficulties," while Ohio "repealed [its rule] in 1968 after experiencing difficulties in practice" (p. 171).
- **International.** At least ten countries impose a supermajority for constitutional adjudication, typically in the constitution itself. South Korea has required six of nine since 1987; one study counted 62 cases from 1987 to 2016 decided 4–5 — five justices thought the statute unconstitutional and it survived. Mexico generally requires 8 of 11 (Commission Report, pp. 171–72).

**Nuances & caveats**

- **This is absence, not staleness.** Not a category whose numbers have aged — one whose numbers do not exist. The corpus's one-line entry ("unpolled") is confirmed, not corrected.
- **The mechanism has never been separated from the Court it would bind.** Every proposal in the 63-item catalogue reacted to specific rulings — the 1820s over decisions striking state laws, Reconstruction over fear the Court would undo Reconstruction statutes, the 1920s over pro-business rulings by narrow majorities (Commission Report, p. 171). The Commission is explicit that the current wave has the same shape, naming *Shelby County v. Holder* and *United States v. Windsor* as 5–4 decisions that "would have been decided otherwise under a supermajority voting rule" (p. 176).
- **Advocacy framing runs one way today.** The mechanism's 2026 champions present it as protecting specific statutes from the current majority (Brennan Center, May 2026) — the mirror image of the contributor's "activist judges" framing. Both are available; neither has been tested.
- **One published claim did not check out.** The Brennan Center piece states three states — "Nebraska, North Dakota, and Ohio" — impose such requirements (May 11, 2026). Ohio's was repealed in 1968; only two states have one today, as the Commission and Caminker both describe it.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **Unpolled.** No independent national poll with a party breakdown asks the proposal, in any threshold or coverage variant. The closest proxy is a *different* supermajority — two-thirds of the Senate to confirm a nominee — at **44% R / 71% D (51% overall), YouGov May 2026**, which is a Weak floor on an adjacent question and is not evidence for this one. Verdict per §4.1: **Unpolled**.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - **Incumbent / regime coding — the central risk, and it is measurable.** A supermajority rule protects statutes against whichever Court majority exists. Attitudes toward that Court currently divide 75R/11D on approval (Gallup, July 2025) and 17R/42D on "too much power" (YouGov, May 2026) — gaps of 64 and 25 points. Ratification takes 38 states over years; a rule whose appeal to each party is a function of who appointed six of nine is being measured on one side of a flip that has not yet happened. The approval gap was also narrower under a president of the other party facing the same Court and widened after *Dobbs*, so the coding tracks salience as well as control.
  - **Framing dependency — at risk, unmeasured.** The identical rule reads as "limiting activist judges" (the contributor's framing) and as "protecting the laws Congress passed" from the current majority (the 2026 advocacy framing). These recruit opposite coalitions, and until one wording is tested on both parties there is no way to know whether either clears — or whether the two simply trade places when the Court does.
  - **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — likely, unmeasured.** Both parties in turn object to 5–4 rulings that go against them; the remedy, a permanent decision rule, has never been asked of anyone.
  - **High-topline / low-floor — cannot be assessed.** No topline exists.
  - **Depth (trade-off collapse) — untested and probably decisive.** No survey has priced the rule. The honest priced question ("even if a law five justices found unconstitutional stayed in force") is exactly what *Thompson v. Heineman* and *Bismarck Public Schools* are.
  - **Staleness — cleared** trivially; there is nothing to go stale.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - **Existential-problem (§5.1) — open, argued neutrally.** The case for: constitutional questions of the first order are settled by one vote, and the losing side each time treats the outcome as illegitimate, which corrodes the Court's standing with half the country in alternation. The case against: the public does not report the underlying grievance. Judicial review itself draws 87% with no party gap at all (Marquette, Feb. 2025), and 49% — including 68% of Republicans — say the Court's power is "about the right amount" (YouGov, May 2026). A dissatisfaction the public locates in *who* the justices are, rather than in *how many* it takes, is not one this rule addresses. Unresolved.
  - **Symmetry (§5.2) — passes by construction, not in the moment.** A decision rule is neutral across time: it binds whichever majority sits and protects whichever Congress legislated, and the abuse examples pair naturally — the Commission's own illustrations are a voting-rights statute and a marriage statute struck by 5–4 majorities falling on opposite sides of the ledger (p. 176). It is not neutral on the day of ratification, because the composition of the sitting Court is known to everyone. The corpus's existing fix — a prospective effective date, as with redistricting's post-2030 start — is available and would be the honest design.
  - **Why-an-amendment (§5.3) — genuinely open, and this is the surprise.** The intuitive answer ("Article III fixes it, so only an amendment can change it") is not what the authorities say. The Commission: "Whether Congress could impose some form of supermajority voting requirement on the Supreme Court without a constitutional amendment is a difficult question" (p. 178). It canvasses the Exceptions and Regulations Clause, noting it "is less clear that this clause allows Congress to regulate decisionmaking procedures of the Court, as opposed to matters affecting its jurisdiction," and that a rule grounded there would not reach original-jurisdiction cases, so the required majority "would depend on the route the case took to reach the Court" (pp. 178–79). CRS takes the same posture in February 2026: Congress "has some authority to enact legislation affecting Supreme Court voting," but "there is limited historical precedent for doing so, and the Constitution may impose limits," and because no such proposal has ever been enacted "there is no Supreme Court precedent considering the scope of Congress's authority in this area" ([CRS IF13166, Feb. 10, 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13166.html)). This is closest to §5.3's fourth accepted answer — the text is silent — but weaker than the corpus's other silences, because here a statute might work and has simply never been tried. The clean amendment answer exists only for a version that also reaches original jurisdiction, or that must survive a Court holding the statute unconstitutional.
  - **Blast radius (§6.3) — the unresolved design problem.** Coverage is not a detail. A rule reaching only federal statutes leaves executive action and state law at a bare majority; a rule reaching all federal and state lawmaking would also have required six votes to strike state laws under the Fourteenth Amendment, and even the federal-statutes-only version changes outcomes in the closest and most contested cases — the Commission's own examples are *Shelby County v. Holder* (Section 4 of the Voting Rights Act) and *United States v. Windsor* (part of the Defense of Marriage Act), both 5–4 invalidations of federal statutes that "would have been decided otherwise under a supermajority voting rule" (p. 176) — and the same arithmetic reaches other 5–4 rulings in both directions. The Commission also flags a problem with no U.S. precedent: what a 5–4 vote *means* when six are needed, and whether lower courts should follow the five or the four who control the outcome (pp. 174–75) — plus the evasion route, that a majority can reach its preferred result by construing a statute narrowly rather than invalidating it (p. 177). Ohio repealed its rule over a milder version of this same class of problem.

Two readings follow. First, **the abuse-framing/remedy-framing gap cannot even be measured here**, because neither end has been asked: the diagnosis items that do exist (17–20% R on "too much power") run *lower* among Republicans than Democrats, the opposite of the usual pattern in which both parties agree on the abuse and split on the fix. That is the signature of a regime-coded issue caught at one point in the cycle, not of a consensus waiting to be found.

Second, the two framings that *do* clear the bar are already ours, and neither is this proposal under another name. Fixed terms and an enforceable ethics code address the composition and conduct of the Court — where the public's stated dissatisfaction sits, given that 87% of every party affirms judicial review itself. A supermajority rule addresses the arithmetic of invalidation, which is a different thing, and not the thing the existing numbers support.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** One independent pollster (Marquette, Pew, AP-NORC, or Economist/YouGov) fielding a **forward-looking, both-parties, mechanism-specific** item naming neither the current Court nor any recent case, so the wording works identically whichever party appointed the majority. For example: *"Some people say the Supreme Court should be able to strike down a law passed by Congress and signed by the President only if at least six of the nine justices agree, instead of five. This rule would apply to any future Court and any future law. Do you favor or oppose it?"* — with a required follow-up that prices it: *"…even if that meant a law five justices believed was unconstitutional stayed in force?"* The threshold is **≥60% in both parties on the priced version**, since the unpriced version is the abstract that every court-reform idea passes. Two further requirements before acceptance: the reading should be **replicated once after a change in the Court's appointing-party majority**, or failing that the draft must carry a **prospective effective date** (the redistricting post-2030 device); and coverage must be settled — federal statutes only is the sole version whose blast radius is defensible on current evidence. A joint resolution with sponsors from both parties, or a fourth state adopting the rule by ballot, would count as §4.2 evidence.

**Evidence that would move it out.** Any of three results. (1) The priced question comes back below 45% in either party — **rejection on the floor**, §4.1. (2) The "activist judges" and "protecting laws Congress passed" framings produce Republican and Democratic numbers that trade places, confirming the rule is a proxy for the sitting Court rather than a principle — **rejection on incumbent/regime coding**, §4.3, the test that felled the Electoral College and self-pardon categories. (3) The unpriced version polls well but collapses when respondents are told what Nebraska's rule did in *Thompson v. Heineman* — **rejection on the depth test**, which ended the balanced-budget category.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate, with the gate "one forward-looking both-parties poll of the mechanism, priced."** The category is genuinely unpolled (§2), so §4.1 supplies no verdict either way, and under §9 an unpolled category with a named gate is a candidate by default. It carries more institutional precedent than most of this bucket — sixty-three congressional proposals since 1823, two states operating the rule today without significant difficulty, ten countries (§2). Two things keep it off the target list on today's evidence: the only measurable attitudes toward the Court's power are regime-coded at 25 to 64 points (§3), and the public's diagnosis does not match this remedy — 87% of every party affirms judicial review and 68% of Republicans call the Court's power about right (§3, §4), leaving the existential-problem test unresolved. One finding the founder should weigh directly: the Commission and CRS both treat the statutory route as open, so an amendment is not obviously required here — a statute is the cheaper experiment, and the amendment case rests on original-jurisdiction coverage and durability rather than necessity. Founder decides; if the answer is to pursue it, the poll above belongs near the top of the commissioned-survey list.
