# Appointment-Default Rule — Candidate Category Memo

*Superseded 2026-08-18: folded into Target 2 (Term Limits) 2026-08-18 as the confirmation-refusal default — see [../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) "Fold-in (2026-08-18)" and Article 2, Section 2 of [../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](../PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md). This candidate memo is retained as the gate-stage record.*

*Candidate since 2026-08-12. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** Unpolled as a mechanism — no independent national poll has ever asked whether a nomination should be confirmed, or forced to a vote, by default after a deadline; the closest matched questions are the most regime-coded numbers in the corpus (Republicans 36% → 83% and Democrats 74% → 16% on identical Monmouth wording, 2016 → 2020), while the one pre-vacancy reading finds 78% rejecting party-of-the-president as grounds to oppose a qualified nominee (74% D / 79% R, Marquette 2020) — recommendation: fold into Target 2 as a companion clause, conditioned on a forward-looking poll fielded when no seat is contested.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The Appointments Clause provides that the President "shall nominate, and by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, shall appoint … judges of the Supreme Court, and all other officers of the United States" ([Art. II §2 cl. 2, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articleii)). It makes Senate consent a precondition for appointment and says nothing about *when* the Senate must answer, or whether it must answer at all. An appointment-default rule fills that silence: after a fixed period, inaction produces a defined outcome rather than an indefinite vacancy.

Three variants, in descending order of how much they change Article II:

- **(a) Deemed confirmed.** A nomination not voted on within a fixed window (90 days in the version that entered our pipeline) is treated as confirmed — converting the Senate's default from "no" to "yes," the variant that most changes advice and consent.
- **(b) Forced floor vote.** After the window, the nomination is discharged to the full Senate for an up-or-down vote. The Senate keeps the power to reject and loses only the power to never answer. This has the deepest pedigree in the reform literature: Sarah Binder and Forrest Maltzman proposed fast-tracking nominees "to guarantee them a vote," noting a 2000 presidential candidate's proposal that the Senate "take no more than 60 days to act on the next president's executive and judicial branch appointees" ([Brookings, June 19, 2000](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/a-nomination-for-change-in-the-senate-process-for-confirming-judicial-appointees-in-desperate-need-of-reform/)).
- **(c) Knock-out / fallback.** Leaves consent intact but denies obstruction a permanent payoff — the seat sits vacant with the Court operating as constituted, or the appointment power passes to another body. Germany is the only working model in a peer democracy: the December 2024 amendments provide that "if the competent electoral body has not elected a successor within three months of receiving a nomination … the right to vote may also be exercised by the other electoral body," the resulting judge deemed elected by the originally responsible chamber. The Bundestag adopted the package **600–69** on December 19, 2024 ([Birmingham Law School, Jan. 2025](https://blog.bham.ac.uk/lawresearch/2025/01/strengthening-the-resilience-of-the-german-federal-constitutional-court/)). Germany kept its two-thirds consent requirement and attacked only the deadlock — the design most compatible with our drafting doctrine.

**How it entered our pipeline.** From the r/PoliticalDiscussion scrape of 2026-08-12 ([REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md) §"NEW Categories" #3), attributed to **u/musashisamurai** (nominations not voted on within 90 days considered passed), **u/mypoliticalvoice** (a version with deputies and deadlines), and **u/Ind132** (a knock-out fallback for blocked Supreme Court confirmations); logged in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) with the same attribution. The initial assessment was "not a standalone category, but a strong companion clause," and [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) records it as symmetric by construction and likely riding as a companion clause rather than a standalone amendment.

**What Target 2 already covers, and what it does not.** [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) names this gap in its own drafting notes: under the staggering trap, the amendment must "decide the *confirmation-refusal* default (a Garland scenario): specify that the Court sits with fewer justices until confirmation, so obstruction gains nothing permanent." That is variant (c), already identified as necessary drafting work inside an accepted target — the reason this memo recommends a companion clause rather than a category. The gap is live in the vehicles too: the Whitehouse–Booker bill requires the President to appoint a justice "within the first 120 days of the first and third years of a Presidential term" but contains **no provision** for Senate inaction ([S. 3096, 118th Cong.](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-118s3096is/html/BILLS-118s3096is.htm)) — a biennial calendar any Senate can stop. What Target 2 does *not* cover, and what this memo evaluates as the remainder, is extending a default rule beyond the Supreme Court to executive officers, ambassadors, and all Article III judgeships.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns — the matched-wording pair**

- **Monmouth University Polling Institute — identical wording, four years apart.** "Which of the following statements comes closer to your view: The sitting president has the power to nominate Supreme Court justices and the Senate should consider those nominations even if they occur at the very end of a president's term – or – Supreme Court vacancies that occur at the end of a president's term should be put on hold so that voters can consider the potential nomination when deciding who to elect as the next president?" March 17–20, 2016 (n=1,008 adults): **53% consider / 43% put on hold — 36% R / 52% I / 68% D** ([Monmouth](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_032116/)). September 24–27, 2020 (n=809 RV): **47% consider / 49% put on hold — 83% R / 48% I / 16% D** ([Monmouth](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_us_092820/)); the 2020 report states the 2016 comparison on a registered-voter base as 36% R / 60% I / 74% D.
- **The flip, stated precisely.** Republican support moved **36% → 83%**, Democratic support **74% → 16%** (68% → 16% against the 2016 adults base). Both parties reversed by roughly 50 points, in opposite directions, when the presidency changed hands. The floor on this question was **36% in 2016 and 16% in 2020** — Weak in both years, held by a different party each time. This is the cleanest regime-coding flip in the corpus, cleaner than the filibuster case (§4.3), because pollster and wording are held constant.
- **The nominee-specific version moves the same way.** "Now that the nomination has been made, do you think the U.S. Senate should or should not hold hearings to consider Obama's nominee?" (March 2016) — **69% should / 25% should not — 56% R / 66% I / 85% D**; the parallel Barrett item (September 2020) — **53% should / 43% should not**, against a 2016 comparison of 73% should. Monmouth publishes no party breakdown for the 2020 hearings item, so that flip is documented only at the topline.
- **Gallup, corroborating the flip on a second matched pair.** March 18–19, 2016 (n=1,019 adults): "Would you like to see the Senate vote in favor of Garland serving on the Supreme Court, or not?" — **52% favor / 29% not — 33% R / 44% I / 76% D** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/190091/support-garland-average-supreme-court-nominees.aspx)). September 30–October 15, 2020 (n=1,035 adults), same question form for Barrett: **51% favor / 46% oppose — 89% R / 52% I / 15% D** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/322232/amy-coney-barrett-seated-supreme-court.aspx)). Republicans **33% → 89%**, Democrats **76% → 15%**. Two independent pollsters, two question forms, the same reversal.
- **Pew Research Center, February 18–21, 2016 (n=1,002 adults).** **56%** said the Senate should hold hearings and vote / **38%** wait for the next president. Pew published the split one-sided only: **66% of Republicans said it should not** (71% among conservative Republicans), **79% of Democrats said it should**; no independent figure or two-way split is published — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2016/02/22/majority-of-public-wants-senate-to-act-on-obamas-court-nominee/). Pew's March 17–27, 2016 follow-up (n=2,254) on confirming Garland: **46% favor / 30% oppose — 22% R / 70% D**, a 48-point gap Pew notes is wider than for the two preceding nominees — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2016/03/28/garland-nomination-to-supreme-court-gets-positive-reception-from-public/).

**The pre-vacancy baseline — the one measurement taken while no seat was contested**

- **Marquette Law School Poll, September 8–15, 2020 (n=1,523 adults; NORC AmeriSpeak probability panel).** Fielded **before** the September 18, 2020 vacancy, so it measures the process in the abstract rather than a live fight — the only such reading in the record. "If a nominee for the US Supreme Court is qualified and has no ethical problems, would US Senators be justified or not justified in voting against that nominee **simply because the Senator is from a different political party from the president who made the nomination?**" — **21% justified / 78% not justified — R 21/79 · I 17/81 · D 24/74**. On ideological grounds (how the justice would decide abortion, gun control, or affirmative action cases): 41% / 58% — R 39/60 · I 36/62 · D 47/52 — [release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2020/09/23/detailed-results-of-the-marquette-law-school-supreme-court-poll-september-8-15-2020-part-2-nominations/), [crosstabs](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/MLSPSCOTUS02Crosstabs_Nominations.html), [instrument](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/MULawSC2020RBGReleaseInstrument.pdf). **A 78% cross-party consensus, floors of 74% and 79%, that opposing a qualified nominee purely because of the nominating president's party is not legitimate** — the norm a default rule would enforce, measured when neither side had a stake. Its limitation is precise: it asks whether a *senator* would be justified in voting no, not whether the *Constitution* should impose a consequence for never voting at all.

**The mechanism itself**

- **No poll of any kind was found asking about a default rule.** Searches for national polling on a deadline for Senate action, a deemed-confirmed or automatic-confirmation rule, or a guaranteed up-or-down vote — Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, UMD PPC — returned nothing. Every question in the record asks about a *particular pending nomination* during a *live partisan fight*, the one condition under which the answer is least informative about the rule. Marquette's recurring Supreme Court survey, the corpus's best court-reform tracker, has never asked it.

**Legislative and rules record**

- **The Senate solves throughput problems by simple-majority rules change, not by constitutional rule.** On September 11, 2025 it invoked the nuclear option to allow bundled consideration of nearly all executive nominees by simple majority; the first bundle confirmed 48 nominees on September 18, 2025, a second 108. The change "does nothing to alter the committee process," where most delay originates — [Brookings, Piper and Tenpas, October 10, 2025](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/will-the-new-senate-rule-make-it-easier-for-presidents-to-confirm-their-teams/). This cuts both ways; treated in §3.
- **Vacancy context (not evidence for the mechanism).** As of **August 18, 2026**: **26 Article III vacancies, 4 nominees pending** — [Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts](http://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/judicial-vacancies/current-judicial-vacancies). A snapshot of ordinary turnover, not a measure of obstruction; offered only as scale.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **Everything except the Marquette baseline was taken during a live confirmation fight**, and nothing has been asked since 2020 — the worst possible conditions for measuring a durable structural preference, and the same staleness problem that sank qualified immunity.
- **Every existing question asks something other than ours.** "Should this vacancy be filled now" is about a specific seat and a specific president; a default rule is about every future nomination under every future Senate. The §11 trap applies directly — a floor measured while one side holds the power is not the floor that survives the handover, and here we can *watch* it not survive.
- **Marquette fielded the ideal items and did publish their toplines — but no party breakdown.** The September 2020 instrument contains Q33 (whether the 2016 refusal to hold a hearing was right or wrong) and Q34 (whether the Senate should hold hearings on a 2020 nomination) — the two questions that measure the flip *within the same respondents*. Both toplines are in Marquette's published topline file: **Q33, 73% wrong thing / 25% right thing; Q34, 67% hold hearings / 32% not hold hearings** ([topline](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/MLSPSC02Toplines_RGB.html)). The companion crosstab file breaks out party for the Q25/Q26 nomination items only, so **no party split for Q33 or Q34 has been published** — the within-respondent partisan measure is still the thing to request from the poll's director. See Review Additions for what the toplines establish.
- **The Barrett-era party breakdown for the hearings item is missing** from Monmouth's page, so that flip is documented at the topline (69% → 53%) but not by party. Secondary write-ups citing a Republican 56→73 shift on that item do not match anything in the pollster's release and are not used here.
- **The variants are not interchangeable.** "Deemed confirmed" and "forced vote" are different amendments with different blast radii; no evidence distinguishes support for one from the other, because neither has been asked.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor: Unpolled.** No independent national poll with a party breakdown asks whether nominations should be confirmed, or forced to a vote, by default after a deadline. Two different proxies point in opposite directions, and the gap between them is the whole finding. Measured *during* a vacancy, the closest proxy is Monmouth's end-of-term question, whose floor is **36% (Republicans, 2016)** and **16% (Democrats, 2020)** on identical wording — Weak in both directions, held by a different party each time. Measured *before* a vacancy, Marquette's party-of-the-president item has a floor of **74% (Democrats, September 2020)** — Strong. The same public rejects blockade-by-party as illegitimate in the abstract and endorses it in the moment. Verdict per §4.1: **Unpolled**, because neither proxy asks about the mechanism, and because a category whose nearest measurements range from 16% to 78% has not been measured at all.

- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **at maximum risk, and demonstrated rather than suspected.** A ~50-point reversal in both parties on identical wording (Monmouth 2016 → 2020), corroborated by a second pollster on a second wording (Gallup: R 33→89, D 76→15), is the failure mode in its purest observed form — cleaner than the filibuster case the corpus currently treats as the benchmark, because the wording and pollster are held constant. Any poll fielded during a live vacancy will measure which party holds the White House, not what Americans think of the rule.
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — cannot be assessed; no mechanism topline exists, and the proxy has no honest topline either (53% and 47% are averages across two mirror-image blocs).
  - *Framing dependency* — **at risk and untested.** "Deemed confirmed," "guaranteed up-or-down vote," and "the seat stays empty until the Senate acts" are three different proposals that would almost certainly poll differently. A guaranteed-vote framing preserves the Senate's power to say no and is likeliest to clear — a prediction, not a measurement.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **at risk, but differently shaped than elsewhere.** The FBI/DOJ and FCC pattern is that both parties condemn the abuse and split on the remedy. Here the abuse framing holds when no seat is contested (78%, floor 74%) and collapses when one is (16–36% floors). The remedy has never been asked, so the risk is real but its source is identifiable: timing, not remedy language.
  - *Depth* and *staleness* — both untested and failing respectively: no survey has put the obvious counter ("a president could seat a justice the Senate never approved") to respondents, and the proxy data stop in 2020. Both are curable by fresh fieldwork.
  - *Clearly cleared:* none, because the mechanism has never been measured.

- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Symmetry (§5.2) — **passes, and is the category's strongest feature.*** The rule is symmetric by construction: it binds every future Senate and benefits every future president, and no drafter can know which party holds either when it first bites. The record pairs cleanly without editorializing — the 2016 vacancy was held open by a Senate of one party against a president of the other; the 2020 vacancy was filled at speed by that same Senate for its own president; each party's voters approved the procedure that suited them in the year it suited them (§2). This is the veil-of-ignorance construction [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) §1 cites as the reason the category became a candidate.
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3) — **passes, on answer 4 (the text is silent) and answer 3 (Congress cannot self-bind).*** The Appointments Clause conditions appointment on consent but imposes no duty to act, and that silence is what a blockade relies on. The scholarly split is live: Michael D. Ramsey argues the Constitution "does not require the Senate to do anything in response to the nomination," Article I §5 leaving the Senate to decide how to respond ([NCC, May 16, 2016](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/why-the-senate-doesnt-have-to-act-on-merrick-garlands-nomination)); Erwin Chemerinsky and David Gans read the clause to require the Senate to consider a nominee "either by approving the president's choice or rejecting it" ([NCC, Lana Ulrich, May 27, 2016](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/tracking-the-controversy-over-judge-garlands-nomination)). Nothing has resolved it, and that is the point: a rule that depends on which reading prevails is not a rule. Senate rules cannot supply one — a standing order adopted by simple majority can be undone by the next, as the September 2025 en-bloc change itself shows. **One qualification:** that change proves the Senate *can* fix throughput when it wants to, weakening the case that an amendment is needed for *delay*. It does not touch *refusal* — bundling speeds nominees a majority wants confirmed and does nothing for one the majority prefers never to answer.
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1) — **open, and this is the test holding the category.*** For: a seat that can be held empty indefinitely converts a constitutional appointment power into one chamber's discretion, and the same technique across the judiciary and executive branch could hollow out offices the Constitution assumes will be filled. Against: this is a scheduling preference, not a threat to the country's future — vacancies have been absorbed throughout the system's history, seats do get filled, and elections remain the remedy for a Senate voters think has misbehaved. Both readings are defensible on the current record; §5 states what would resolve it.

**The gap here is not abuse-versus-remedy but abstract-versus-live, and that is the finding.** In every other category with a framing gap, both parties condemn the abuse and split on the fix. Here both parties condemn the abuse *in the abstract* by wide margins — 78% say party-of-the-president is not a legitimate reason to oppose a qualified nominee, with floors of 74% and 79% — and then both parties endorse the practice when their own side is running it. The determining variable is not the wording of the remedy; it is whether a seat is currently contested when the question is asked. That is a more tractable problem than the qualified-immunity framing gap, because it points to a specific, fixable survey design: field the question when no seat is vacant, in wording that names no incumbent.

Two readings should not be lost in the flip. The Marquette pre-vacancy item clears the Strong bar with a 74% floor from a probability panel with no live fight to code the answer; and the 2016 nominee-specific question drew **56% of Republicans** for holding hearings *while their own party's Senate was declining to hold them* — a Moderate floor recorded against the respondents' own side's immediate interest. Neither asks about a constitutional default, so neither carries a verdict. Together they are the best evidence that a procedural-fairness instinct exists underneath the partisan reversal, and the reason this memo recommends a conditioned companion clause rather than a rejection.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

- **Evidence that would move it in.** One forward-looking, mechanism-specific poll from an independent pollster (Marquette's national Supreme Court survey is the natural home; Pew, AP-NORC, or a UMD PPC deliberative would serve), testing the variants separately in both-parties wording. Core item: *"When any president nominates someone to a federal office or judgeship, should the Senate be required to hold an up-or-down vote within a set period — say 120 days — or should the Senate be able to decline to vote at all?"* Then the harder variant: *"If the Senate does not vote within that period, should the nominee be considered confirmed, or should the position stay vacant until the Senate votes?"* **Threshold: a bipartisan floor of ≥60% on the forced-vote variant** would justify drafting it into the Target 2 package; 45–59% keeps it a flagged companion clause; the deemed-confirmed variant should be reported separately and held to the same ≥60% floor before being drafted at all. Three design requirements follow from §2 and are not optional: **field it when no seat is contested** (that single variable moves the nearest proxy fifty points); **carry Marquette's party-of-the-president item in the same instrument** as an anchor against its 78% benchmark; and use a deliberative format stating the counterargument ("a president could seat a judge the Senate never approved"), since the depth test is untested. A cheaper first step before any commissioning: request the **party crosstabs** for Marquette's September 2020 Q33 and Q34 items, whose toplines are published (73% and 67%; see §2 and Review Additions) but whose party breakdowns are not — they would supply a within-respondent measure of the flip that no new fieldwork can reproduce.
- **Evidence that would move it out.** A forward-looking poll in which either party's floor lands below 45% on every variant would make this a rejection on the **high-topline/low-floor** and **regime-coding** tests — the same disposition as filibuster reform, which the corpus classifies as unstable rather than merely unpopular. Independently, if a fielded question shows the answer still tracking which party holds the presidency even in forward-looking wording, the category fails the regime-coding test outright and should be rejected regardless of the level, because a rule the public supports only when it favors them cannot be ratified by three-quarters of the states.
- **Recommendation: fold into Target 2 as a companion clause, conditioned on the poll above.** The clause-level idea: **in the Supreme Court section of the term-limits article, specify the confirmation-refusal default for the biennial seats** — the Court sits as constituted with fewer justices until the Senate acts, and the seat's fixed 18-year term runs from its calendar start date regardless of when the vacancy is filled, so that refusing to confirm shortens the resulting justice's tenure rather than preserving the seat for a friendlier president. This is variant (c), it is the answer [TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md](../TERM_LIMITS_RESEARCH.md) already identified as required drafting work under the staggering trap, and it removes the incentive to blockade without altering who gives consent — which is why it can be drafted now, on §5.2 and §5.3 grounds, while the polling that variants (a) and (b) would need does not yet exist. Do **not** draft the deemed-confirmed variant or extend any default rule to executive officers and the wider judiciary on the present record: that is a much larger change to Article II, it is unpolled in a category where the nearest measurement swings 50 points with the party in power (§2), and the existential-problem test is unresolved (§3). Founder decides; the honest summary is that the strongest thing this category has is its symmetry, and the weakest is that the only number anyone has ever measured near it reversed itself completely in four years.

## Review Additions (2026-08-18)

*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Marquette's Q33 and Q34 were published after all, and they are the best pre-vacancy evidence in the category.** Both items were fielded September 8–15, 2020 — before the September 18 vacancy — in the same instrument as the 78% party-of-the-president item, and both toplines appear in Marquette's published topline file ([topline](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/MLSPSC02Toplines_RGB.html)):
  - **Q33** — "In February 2016, following the death of Justice Antonin Scalia, Republican Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell announced that the Senate… Was not holding a hearing on the nomination the right thing or the wrong thing to do?" — **73% wrong thing / 25% right thing.**
  - **Q34** — "If there is a vacancy on the Supreme Court during the 2020 presidential election year and President Trump nominates someone what should the Senate do?" — **67% hold hearings / 32% not hold hearings.**
  Q34 is the more valuable of the two and sharpens the memo's central finding. It asks *prospectively* about a nomination by a president of the party that then held the White House, days before the vacancy actually occurred, and returns 67% for holding hearings. Monmouth's comparable question, fielded September 24–27, 2020 — after the seat opened — returned 47%. The same window, a twenty-point gap, and the only variable that changed is whether a seat was actually contested. That is direct support for the memo's diagnosis that the determining variable is abstract-versus-live rather than remedy wording, and it is the strongest available argument for the design requirement in §5 that any commissioned poll be fielded when no seat is vacant. **No party breakdown is published for either item** — Marquette's crosstab file for this wave covers the Q25/Q26 nomination items only — so neither can supply a bipartisan floor, and the gate result (**Unpolled**) is unchanged.

- **The Senate's existing default is the opposite of the proposed rule, and it is now verified against primary text.** Senate Rule XXXI, paragraph 6 provides: "Nominations neither confirmed nor rejected during the session at which they are made shall not be acted upon at any succeeding session without being again made to the Senate by the President; and if the Senate shall adjourn or take a recess for more than thirty days, all nominations pending and not finally acted upon at the time of taking such adjournment or recess shall be returned by the Secretary to the President, and shall not again be considered unless they shall again be made to the Senate by the President" ([U.S. Senate Manual, 110th Cong., S. Doc. 110-1, Rule XXXI](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SMAN-110/html/SMAN-110-pg57.htm)). CRS confirms the operation: return is **automatic** unless the Senate acts to prevent it by unanimous consent, and all nominations are returned at the end of a Congress; a returned nominee must be nominated afresh and re-referred to committee ([CRS R46664](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46664)). Bearing on the category: this is a useful sharpening of the why-an-amendment analysis in §3 rather than a change to it. The Senate has not merely failed to adopt a deadline-forcing default — it has an explicit written default running the other way, in which inaction extinguishes the nomination, and that default lives in a standing rule the Senate can waive or rewrite by its own majority. It strengthens answer (3) in §5.3 (Congress cannot bind itself) and it gives the drafting work a concrete counter-text to displace. No change to the verdict or recommendation.
