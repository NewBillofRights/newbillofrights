# Striking the 13th Amendment's Exception Clause — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-12. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** 66% (Republicans) on a state ballot measure ensuring the constitution "doesn't legalize slavery and involuntary servitude as punishments for crime," YouGov October 2024 — **Strong** — held by the existential-problem test and by a wording-sensitivity problem that two defeats expose, not by the floor — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: a mechanism-specific national poll of the *federal* clause, plus a resolved answer on §5.1).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The Thirteenth Amendment's operative sentence reads: "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, **except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted**, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction." The proposal is to strike the bolded clause, leaving an unqualified prohibition — the narrowest edit in the pipeline, with no new grant of power and no new institution created.

Two variants exist, and the difference between them is doing most of the work in the evidence below:

- **Bare deletion.** Remove the exception and stop. This is the federal Abolition Amendment's approach: its operative text prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude and provides that Congress shall have power to enforce it by appropriate legislation, with the punishment carve-out simply gone ([Merkley draft text, 117th Congress](https://www.merkley.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/imo/media/doc/21.06.17%20Abolition%20Amendment.pdf)).
- **Deletion plus an express work-programs savings clause.** Tennessee's ratified state text is the model: "Slavery and involuntary servitude are forever prohibited. Nothing in this section shall prohibit an inmate from working when the inmate has been duly convicted of a crime" ([ballot text, Tennessee Amendment 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Tennessee_Amendment_3)). The second sentence preserves prison work programs on their face while removing the compulsion the first sentence forbids.

**The federal record.** The Abolition Amendment has been introduced by Sen. Jeff Merkley and Rep. Nikema Williams in consecutive Congresses and has never received a committee or floor vote in either chamber. Its cosponsor arithmetic is the central counter-evidence: in the 117th Congress the House resolution drew 193 cosponsors — 182 Democrats and 11 Republicans ([GovTrack, H.J.Res. 53](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/117/hjres53)); by the 118th, 129 cosponsors, 124 Democrats and 5 Republicans ([H.J.Res. 72](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/118/hjres72)). The Senate companions drew 14 cosponsors in each Congress — 13 Democrats and 1 independent, and **zero Republicans in either** ([S.J.Res. 21, 117th](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/117/sjres21); [S.J.Res. 33, 118th](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/118/sjres33)).

**Pipeline entry.** Surfaced by **u/Hwttdzhwttdz** in the r/PoliticalDiscussion thread mined on 2026-08-12 — "Make slavery illegal, ratify the 13th Amendment to eliminate the criminality clause" — a comment that scored zero in the thread and was promoted anyway because our method (§4.2) weighs ballot results above thread sentiment ([REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md); attribution in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)).

**Overlap with existing targets: none.** No target touches the Thirteenth Amendment, criminal punishment, or corrections. That cuts both ways: it also means this cannot ride inside another package the way the appointment-default rule rides with term limits.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent national poll with a party breakdown**

- **YouGov, Oct. 23–25, 2024 (n=1,139 U.S. adult citizens).** Asked "Would you support or oppose a ballot measure that does the following in your state?" across twenty 2024 measures, the item "Ensures the state constitution doesn't legalize slavery and involuntary servitude as punishments for crime" drew **73% support / 10% oppose / 17% not sure — 66% R / 85% D / 69% I** (unweighted n = 310 R, 410 D, 414 I). It ranked second of twenty, behind paid sick leave (78%). Opposition was in single or low double digits in every party group (11% R, 7% D, 11% I) — the Republican shortfall against Democrats is *uncertainty*, not opposition — [YouGov article](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50840-poll-2024-state-ballot-measures-workers-rights-abortion-drugs); [topline and crosstab PDF](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Ballot_Initiatives_poll_results.pdf).

**Real ballot results** (state analogues, with each state's 2024 presidential outcome as the partisan-lean marker, per [FEC official 2024 results](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/2024presgeresults.pdf))

- **Vermont Proposal 2 (2022)** — **88.71% yes** (238,466 / 30,335); also repealed consent- and debt-based servitude language — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Vermont_Proposal_2,_Prohibit_Slavery_and_Indentured_Servitude_Amendment_(2022)). *D in 2024.*
- **Utah Amendment C (2020)** — **80.48% yes** (1,138,974 / 276,171) — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Utah_Constitutional_Amendment_C,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_a_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2020)). *R in 2024.*
- **Tennessee Amendment 3 (2022)** — **79.53% yes** (1,294,296 / 333,071); every county voted yes; the ratified text carries the prison-work savings sentence quoted in §1 — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Tennessee_Constitutional_Amendment_3,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2022)). *R in 2024.*
- **Alabama recompiled constitution (2022)** — **76.49% yes** (888,456 / 273,040), ratifying a wholesale recompilation that removed racist language including the involuntary-servitude exception. *Not* a clean single-issue vote; cite as bundled evidence — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Alabama_Recompiled_Constitution_Ratification_Question_(2022)). *R in 2024.*
- **Nebraska Amendment 1 (2020)** — **68.23% yes** (603,204 / 280,898) — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Nebraska_Amendment_1,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2020)). *R in 2024 (4 of 5 electoral votes).*
- **Colorado Amendment A (2018)** — **66.21% yes** (1,599,790 / 816,342), clearing a 55% supermajority requirement — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Colorado_Amendment_A,_Removal_of_Exception_to_Slavery_Prohibition_for_Criminals_Amendment_(2018)). *D in 2024.*
- **Nevada Question 4 (2024)** — **60.60% yes** (835,627 / 543,236) — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Nevada_Question_4,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2024)). *R in 2024.*
- **Oregon Measure 112 (2022)** — **55.59% yes** (1,047,028 / 836,295) — the narrowest passage, in one of the most Democratic-voting states in the set — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Oregon_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2022)). *D in 2024.*

**Ballot defeats — the counter-evidence**

- **California Proposition 6 (2024)** — **DEFEATED, 46.66% yes / 53.34% no** (6,895,604 / 7,882,137) — in the state with the largest Democratic presidential margin in this set — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/California_Proposition_6,_Remove_Involuntary_Servitude_as_Punishment_for_Crime_Amendment_(2024)).
- **Louisiana Amendment 7 (2022)** — **DEFEATED, 39.15% yes / 60.85% no** (508,852 / 790,787). Its own legislative sponsor urged a no vote because the text would have prohibited involuntary servitude "except as it applies to the otherwise lawful administration of criminal justice" — language read as preserving or broadening the carve-out — [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Louisiana_Amendment_7,_Remove_Involuntary_Servitude_as_Punishment_for_a_Crime_from_Constitution_Measure_(2022)); [Louisiana Illuminator, Nov. 8, 2022](https://lailluminator.com/2022/11/08/louisiana-voters-reject-ban-on-slavery-involuntary-servitude-author-also-opposed-it/).
- **Colorado Amendment T (2016)** — **DEFEATED, 49.68% yes / 50.32% no** (1,280,037 / 1,296,722) — identical to the measure that passed two years later at 66%: the sharpest demonstration that the same substance can swing sixteen points on presentation alone (reported on the [Amendment A page](https://ballotpedia.org/Colorado_Amendment_A,_Removal_of_Exception_to_Slavery_Prohibition_for_Criminals_Amendment_(2018))).

**Legislative record (federal)**

- Zero Republican Senate cosponsors across two Congresses; Republican House cosponsors 11 → 5; no vote of any kind in either chamber (citations in §1). Against that, every state measure above was legislatively referred, which in Utah, Tennessee, Nebraska, and Alabama required Republican legislative majorities to vote yes to reach the ballot at all.

**Operational aftermath — the evidence base the 2026-08-12 note asked for**

- **Colorado, Feb. 17, 2026.** Denver District Judge Sarah Wallace ruled in a class action led by incarcerated plaintiff Harold Mortis (filed 2022) that the Colorado Department of Corrections violates the amended Article II, §26 by compelling work under threat of punishment. The order bars segregation and disciplinary charges for work refusal; it does **not** shut down prison work programs, and the department was given 28 days to appeal — [CBS Colorado, Feb. 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/denver-judge-colorado-department-corrections-violates-state-constitution-prison-labor/). This is the first squarely on-point ruling: what the amendment eliminates is *compulsion*, not work.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The one national number is a state-measure proxy, not the federal question.** The YouGov item asks how a respondent would vote on a measure in *their state*. No independent national poll with a party breakdown has ever asked about amending the **federal** Thirteenth Amendment. The mechanism is closely analogous but not identical, and §4.3's rule against attaching a broad number to a specific mechanism applies with real force here.
- **Wording sensitivity is the dominant variable, and it is documented rather than theorized.** Colorado's 16-point swing on identical substance (2016 → 2018), Louisiana's defeat on text its own sponsor disowned, and the Nevada/California split on the same November 2024 ballot all point the same way. Advocates and independent analysts attribute California's loss substantially to a ballot label that omitted the word "slavery"; PPIC survey director Mark Baldassare observed that when voters are uncertain "the default is to vote 'no,'" and PPIC's September and October fieldwork had the measure at only 41% support — [KQED, Nov. 2024](https://www.kqed.org/news/12013392/californians-voted-against-outlawing-slavery-why-is-prop-6-failing). The 17% "not sure" share in the YouGov crosstab (23% among Republicans) is the same phenomenon visible in a survey.
- **Staleness is mild but real.** The decisive poll is October 2024 — recent by this corpus's standards, but predating the Colorado ruling.
- **Never asked anywhere:** whether support survives a named trade-off (corrections costs, ended work programs, wages for incarcerated workers); whether it holds for the federal clause specifically; and whether it holds against opponents' arguments, since California is the only measure that faced sustained public argument, and even there no organized opposition *fundraising* materialized.
- **A statutory fight sits underneath the constitutional one.** Nothing in the deletion sets a wage, and the Colorado ruling did not reach compensation. Whether "voluntary" work must be paid is left to legislatures — where the operational opposition will concentrate.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **66% (Republicans)**, against 85% D and 69% I, on "ensures the state constitution doesn't legalize slavery and involuntary servitude as punishments for crime," YouGov October 2024 → **Strong** per §4.1 (≥60). Corroborated where §4.2 weighs most: single-issue passage at 80% in Utah, 79.5% in Tennessee, 68% in Nebraska, 61% in Nevada — four Republican-voting states, three above the floor's own number. The verdict rests on a proxy question, which is why §5 rather than §4.1 holds this category.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — **cleared.** 73% overall with a 66% floor is a 19-point party gap, among the narrowest in the corpus, and Republican *opposition* is 11%.
  - *Framing dependency* — **at risk, and this is the live failure mode.** Identical substance produced 49.68% and 66.21% in the same state two years apart, and the two 2024 measures on the same night landed at 60.60% (Nevada, "slavery" in the question) and 46.66% (California, "involuntary servitude" in the label). The framing that clears the bar — the plain word "slavery," paired with an explicit assurance that work programs continue — *is* the thing we would draft, which distinguishes this from qualified immunity, where the clearing framing was a different proposal. But it makes the drafting choice load-bearing rather than cosmetic.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **untested, therefore open.** No pollster has run a costs-named version. Given a 73% abstract number and one contested real-world campaign that lost, this is a genuine unknown rather than a cleared test.
  - *Staleness* — **cleared for now** (Oct. 2024 fieldwork plus a Feb. 2026 ruling), but the poll should be refreshed alongside any federal ask.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **cleared.** The clause binds no officeholder and confers no partisan advantage; no "your side holds the power" flip is available, and the state results span both parties' strongholds.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **cleared, unusually.** Abuse and remedy are the same object: there is no separate enforcement machinery to poll, because the remedy is a deletion. That absence of machinery is why the floor survives here when it collapses in the executive-accountability categories.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **open, and decisive.** Two honest readings: (a) an explicit constitutional authorization for slavery as criminal punishment is a live carve-out in the nation's founding charter, and a project calling itself a New Bill of Rights that leaves it in place has declined to finish that document's own arc; (b) the change is substantially declaratory — prison work programs continue under both drafting variants, and the operative fights (wages, conditions, discipline) are statutory and would remain so the day after ratification. Reading (b) is the objection that removed civic information from the platform under §5.1: a real problem, but not one whose non-solution threatens the country's future. The Colorado ruling cuts both ways — it shows the deletion has operational bite, and that the bite is delivered through ordinary litigation and statute.
  - *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **pass by construction.** The clause binds no party, branch, or incumbent and creates no weapon for either side to surrender. It needs no paired-abuse framing because no abuse is attributable to a party.
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **strong pass.** The exception is constitutional text. No statute can repeal it, and every state that removed its own version had to amend its own constitution. This is answer (4) in §5.3 — the text is the problem — in its purest form.

## 4. Framings tested

- **"Slavery … as punishment for crime" (the word "slavery" present).** Nevada Question 4, Nov. 2024: **60.60% yes**. YouGov's national item, which also uses "slavery," is at 73% / 66% R. This is the framing that travels.
- **"Involuntary servitude" without "slavery."** California Prop 6, same night: **46.66% — defeated**, against pre-election PPIC readings of 41% (KQED, above). Same substance, same election day, roughly fourteen points apart — the sharpest natural experiment in the corpus on any category.
- **Deletion plus an express work-programs savings clause.** Tennessee, **79.53%** — the highest single-issue result in a Republican-voting state, achieved with text that explicitly preserves inmate work. The savings clause did not depress the number. This is the framing most likely to be both honest and durable, and it is what we would actually draft.
- **Carve-out-preserving text ("except as applied to the lawful administration of criminal justice").** Louisiana, **39.15% — defeated**, and disowned by its own sponsor. The boundary case: text that looks like a repeal but preserves the exception fails badly and complicates a later attempt.
- **Bundled into a broader constitutional cleanup.** Alabama, **76.49%** — but the vote was on a whole recompiled constitution, so it cannot be attributed to this clause. Bundled corroboration only.
- **The federal ask.** Never put to the public in any poll. The only federal signal is the cosponsor pattern — 5–11 House Republicans and zero Senate Republicans — the clearest indication that the state-level number has not transferred to Washington. That divergence between officeholder behavior and public opinion is itself the finding, and the reason this is not a promotion on the strength of a 66% floor alone.

**Does any framing clear the bar honestly?** Yes — "slavery," plus a work-programs savings clause, at a 66% Republican floor and 79.5% in Tennessee. It is the thing we would draft, not a different proposal wearing the name. What it does not yet clear is §5.1.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

- **Evidence that would move it in.** An independent national poll with party crosstabs asking the *federal* mechanism — approximately: *"The U.S. Constitution's Thirteenth Amendment bans slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for a crime. Would you support or oppose amending the Constitution to remove that exception, while allowing prisons to continue operating voluntary or paid work programs?"* — clearing a **60% Republican floor**, in a split-sample design against a version omitting the savings clause and a costs-named version (corrections budgets, wages for incarcerated workers). A second in-mover: any state passing a "slavery"-worded measure with a savings clause after a *contested* campaign with organized, funded opposition — the one condition no passing measure has faced.
- **Evidence that would move it out.** A costs-named or contested-campaign version falling below 45% among Republicans would move this to rejected on the depth test (§4.3), matching the balanced-budget pattern. Separately — and more likely — a settled judgment that the change is substantially declaratory, with the operative fights remaining statutory, would move it out on §5.1, the same test that removed civic information from the platform. A *ballot* defeat alone would not be decisive: California's loss is already in the record and is attributable to wording rather than substance.
- **Recommendation: hold as a candidate.** Gate: a mechanism-specific national poll of the federal clause, and a founder ruling on §5.1. The polling case is Strong and unusually clean — a 66% Republican floor with 11% Republican opposition (§2), four Republican-voting states passing single-issue versions at 61–80%, symmetry by construction, and the strongest available why-an-amendment answer, since only an amendment can touch constitutional text (§3). Two things stop a promotion today. First, every national number is a *state-measure* proxy and the federal ask has never been put to voters, while the federal record — zero Republican Senate cosponsors across three Congresses, House Republican cosponsors falling 11 → 5 — is the one place the bipartisan pattern visibly does not hold (§1, §4). Second, §5.1 is open in a way §4.1 is not: this may be the completion of the Bill of Rights' own arc, or a change whose operative content is delegated to statutes and litigation the morning after ratification, as the February 2026 Colorado ruling illustrates in both directions (§3). That is a mission-pillar question, not an evidence question, and under §9 it is the founder's to decide. Founder decides.
