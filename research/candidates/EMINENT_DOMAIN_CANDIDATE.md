# Eminent Domain Limits (post-*Kelo*) — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate proposed 2026-08-18 (Alex MacGregor, text message; logged in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)). Memo written 2026-08-18 under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md). Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing; anything that could not be verified is listed under **Unverified** in §8.*

**Verdict in one line:** 79% (Democrats) opposed to the *Kelo* holding, Saint Index / UMass Lowell Center for Economic and Civic Opinion, Oct.–Nov. 2005 (85% R, 83% I) — **Strong on the number, but the number is twenty-one years old**; twelve state constitutional amendments passed at 60–86% (2006–2012) and a 376–38 House vote corroborate it, no independent national poll with a party split has been run since, and the deciding question is not the polling but the existential-problem test in §5.1 — laid out, not decided, in §4 below.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

**The holding.** In [*Kelo v. City of New London*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-108.ZS.html), 545 U.S. 469 (June 23, 2005), a 5–4 Court (Stevens, joined by Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer) held that New London's taking of fifteen unblighted homes and lots for a private redevelopment plan beside a Pfizer research campus was a "public use." The Court said it had long "embraced the broader and more natural interpretation of public use as 'public purpose'" — the line from [*Berman v. Parker*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/348/26) (1954) and [*Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/467/229) (1984) — so that economic development under a "carefully considered" plan qualifies, barring only takings that are a "mere pretext" for private benefit, and it added that "nothing in our opinion precludes any State from placing further restrictions on its exercise of the takings power" ([opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-108.ZO.html)). O'Connor's dissent (with Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas) answered that "the specter of condemnation hangs over all property. Nothing is to prevent the State from replacing any Motel 6 with a Ritz-Carlton, any home with a shopping mall, or any farm with a factory," and that "the government now has license to transfer property from those with fewer resources to those with more" ([dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-108.ZD.html)); Thomas dissented separately. Because the Takings Clause has bound the states through the Fourteenth Amendment since [*Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co. v. Chicago*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/166/226) (1897), *Kelo* is the federal floor for every level of government, and a federal amendment defining "public use" would bind all of them.

**The reform variants** (all appear in the state record):

- **(a) Flat ban on private-to-private transfer for economic development.** New Hampshire's 2006 amendment is the cleanest text: "No part of a person's property shall be taken by eminent domain and transferred, directly or indirectly, to another person if the taking is for the purpose of private development or other private use of the property" (CACR 30, per [IJ's enacted-legislation list](https://ij.org/issues/private-property/eminent-domain/enacted-legislation-since-kelo/)). Oregon, North Dakota, and Michigan use the same idea; North Dakota adds that public use "does not include public benefits of economic development, including an increase in tax base, tax revenues, employment, or general economic health," with a common-carrier and utility exception.
- **(b) Narrow "public use" definition with a blight exception.** South Carolina (2006), Texas (2009; blight found parcel by parcel), Louisiana (2006), and statutes such as Colorado HB 1411 (blight by clear and convincing evidence). This is where reforms differ most: a broad blight definition can swallow the rule.
- **(c) Heightened compensation and process.** Michigan (at least 125% of fair market value for a principal residence), Rhode Island (150%, 2008), Nevada ("highest price" valuation); Florida (no transfer to a private party for ten years, exceptions only by a three-fifths legislative vote), Georgia (elected bodies only), Mississippi (ten-year no-transfer rule).

A federal amendment would restore or define "public use" in the Fifth Amendment itself — most naturally form (a) with the health-and-safety and common-carrier exceptions the states have used, delegating procedure, compensation, and blight findings to Congress and the states "by law" (§6.2 of the method).

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Saint Index — Center for Economic and Civic Opinion, University of Massachusetts Lowell, Oct.–Nov. 2005.** **81% opposed the *Kelo* decision — 85% R / 79% D / 83% I**; 77% of men, 84% of women, 82% of whites, 72% of African-Americans, 80% of Hispanics. Reported with the same figures in the House Judiciary Committee's [Report 113-357](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-113hrpt357/html/CRPT-113hrpt357.htm) and [Report 115-859](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-115hrpt859/html/CRPT-115hrpt859.htm), and in Somin's *Limits of Backlash* ([SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=976298)). The Saint Index is a land-use survey series sponsored by a consulting firm and fielded by the UMass Lowell center; the party crosstabs are the only national ones in the record. This is the floor number.
- **Zogby International for the American Farm Bureau Federation, Oct. 29–Nov. 2, 2005.** 95% opposed *Kelo* — an advocacy-commissioned poll, reported alongside the Saint Index in the House reports; no party split published. Flagged, cannot carry a verdict.
- **Monmouth University Poll (New Jersey adults, n=800), Sept. 21–26, 2005** — the best depth evidence, though state-level and without a party split: 47% agreed "there are times when it is O.K. to use eminent domain to rebuild an area" (39% disagreed), but taking "low value homes from people in order to build a shopping center" was acceptable to **4%** (7% for "higher value homes"), versus 55% for taking "vacant and run-down buildings" for a shopping center and 88% for a school; **40% had heard nothing at all** about eminent domain even that autumn — [Monmouth](https://www.monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/monmouthpoll_nj_100505/).
- **Rasmussen Reports, Oct. 2–3, 2014 (n=1,000 adults, automated).** 15% favor / **66% oppose** the law allowing government to seize land "for public use including the construction of public buildings and even commercial development"; no party split — [Rasmussen](https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/lifestyle/general_lifestyle/october_2014/voters_to_government_keep_your_hands_off_my_property). A bound only.
- **Iowa Poll (Selzer & Co. for the Des Moines Register), March 5–8, 2023 (n=805 adults).** **78% oppose** companies using eminent domain to build carbon-capture pipelines — **72% R / 82% D / 79% I** — [via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/iowa-poll-strong-majority-opposes-111034669.html). The newest independent party split, but one state and a common-carrier question that most *Kelo* reforms exempt; adjacent, not a floor.
- **Embold Research for Bold Alliance (advocacy), July 25–31, 2024 (n=2,479 RV in IL, IN, IA, NE, ND, SD).** 81% oppose "corporations utilizing eminent domain for private projects"; no party split — [Pipeline Fighters Hub](https://pipelinefighters.org/news/poll-81-oppose-eminent-domain-for-private-use-across-midwest-states-facing-co2-pipelines-carbon-capture-projects/). Flagged.

Searched for and not found (2026-08-18): any national poll since 2014 by Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, or Cato asking about takings for private development with a party breakdown.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **79% (Democrats)** opposed to *Kelo* — Saint Index / UMass Lowell, Oct.–Nov. 2005 (85% R, 83% I) → **Strong** per §4.1. Corroboration: twelve constitutional amendments at 60–86% across red, blue, and purple states (2006–2012); 157–36 House Democrats and 218–2 House Republicans for the 2005 bill; 72% R / 82% D on the adjacent Iowa pipeline question (Selzer 2023).
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *Staleness* — **applies.** The only national party split is from autumn 2005; the last national topline is 2014 (Rasmussen, 66%). Against that: the ballot record runs to 2012 with no decay (Virginia 74% in 2012 vs. Florida 69% in 2006) and the 2023 Iowa reading is in the same band. Nothing suggests the number moved; nothing measures it either.
  - *"Everyone agrees because no one is thinking about it"* — **applies in part.** Monmouth found 40% of New Jerseyans had heard nothing about eminent domain at the peak of the controversy, and Mississippi's 2011 measure polled 51/39 three days out and won 73/27 — support is wide but not deeply held. Monmouth's item-level results are the caution: "rebuild an area" gets a plurality; "take homes for a shopping center" gets 4%. Which sentence the amendment sounds like matters.
  - *Framing dependency / bundling* — **applies and is well measured.** Every measure that attached regulatory-takings compensation lost (California 47.6%, Idaho 23.9%, Washington 41.2%) or barely won (Arizona 64.8%); every clean measure won by 60–86%. The framing that clears the bar is identifiable and is the one this project would draft.
  - *High-topline/low-floor* — does not apply (six-point gap). *Depth* — untested nationally; the Monmouth blight items and the Democratic votes on the federal mechanism suggest the drop comes when blight and redevelopment are named. *Regime coding* — does not apply (§4). *Abuse-vs-remedy* — visible only on the federal funding-cutoff mechanism.
- **Verdict:** **Strong — stale.** A candidate that would be a target on the number if the number were fresh.

## 4. The three non-polling tests

**Existential-problem test (§5.1) — laid out, not decided.**

*Against.* Economic-development takings are rare in practice, and rarer since 2006; forty-plus states have reformed, twelve constitutionally; the federal government already binds itself by executive order and appropriations rider; the Court's rule can be undone by the Court, and two sitting justices have asked to revisit it. On this reading it is a policy preference of the kind the method routes to statutes and state law — a good rule, not a threat to the republic. The contributor's own framing ("less important than things like campaign finance reform") is this argument.

*For.* The Takings Clause is in the Bill of Rights; this would be the first proposal on the slate to restore an original individual liberty the Court has read down, and it maps directly to the Freedom pillar. *Kelo* remains the federal floor: New York has never reformed, several statutory reforms are symbolic, and Utah's rollback shows state protections are repealable. The abuse pattern the dissent and the amici described — takings that fall on "those with fewer resources" for the benefit of "those with disproportionate influence" — is a rule-of-law concern, not only a property one. And the consensus is already ratified in twelve state constitutions — the "lock in agreement the country has already reached" posture the mission prefers.

The honest tension: this candidate has the deepest ballot-box record of ratified consensus on the list (twelve state constitutions), attached to the least urgent problem on it. Whether "the Constitution's own text has been read out and only an amendment can put it back" is enough to satisfy §5.1 without a present-day crisis is a judgment the founder must make.

**Symmetry test (§5.2) — symmetric by construction.** A public-use definition binds every level of government equally and every future majority; it protects whoever is politically weak in a given locality. The abuse record is bipartisan by any pairing: Detroit's Poletown clearance (1981, upheld by the Michigan Supreme Court and repudiated by it in 2004) and New London's Fort Trumbull plan (Democratic-led governments) sit alongside Norwood, Ohio's Rookwood project (2006) and Oneida County's Utica taking (2024); reforms passed under Republican-led legislatures (Florida, Texas, South Carolina) and Democratic-led ones (Michigan's referral, New Mexico 2007), and were resisted by a Democratic governor in Iowa and a Republican governor in Mississippi. The 2023–2024 Midwest pipeline fights are the same coalition again — farm bureaus, environmental groups, and landowners of both parties.

**Why an amendment, not a statute (§5.3) — a precise answer exists.** "Public use" is constitutional text; its meaning is fixed by the Court's reading, which only the Court or an amendment can change. Congress cannot legislate the meaning of the Fifth Amendment against the states; the Private Property Rights Protection Act is therefore a spending condition (lose federal economic-development funds for two years) plus a cause of action, and it has never passed the Senate. State constitutions can go further than the federal floor, but a state fix does not reach federal takings, does not protect owners in unreformed states, and can be repealed or narrowed (Utah 2007; the Nevada legislature's 2010 attempt). This fits the method's accepted answer 4 — the text as construed is what defiant actors cite — with a shade of answer 1: the statutory route has been tried since 2005 and has not become law. It is a stronger answer than qualified immunity had and a weaker one than campaign finance has, because here the Court could reverse itself without an amendment.

**Blast radius (§6.3).** A flat federal ban would override every state's blight and urban-renewal regime, common-carrier condemnations for pipelines and transmission lines, stadium and public-private partnership takings, and land assembly for housing. The states solved this with two exceptions — health-and-safety blight found parcel by parcel, and common carriers/utilities — and the amendment would need both plus "by law" delegation of procedure and compensation. Anything broader (regulatory takings) has been rejected by voters five times and would drag the category into the failure mode that sank the 2006 losers.

## 6. Fit with the slate

It would sit in the **Freedom** pillar beside Privacy — the two individual-liberty amendments in a slate otherwise about the structure and conduct of government — and it is the only proposal that would restore a clause already in the Bill of Rights, which strengthens the "New Bill of Rights" framing. On the "ten amendments" question ([MARKETING_IDEAS.md](../MARKETING_IDEAS.md)), the founder's rule is that the count must be earned by evidence; on the numbers this is the best-evidenced candidate after age limits, and it would be number nine. Cutting the other way: it is the lowest-urgency item on a slate that otherwise pitches itself as protecting the republic; the organized constituency (property-rights litigators, farm bureaus) is coded to one side in most coverage even though the vote record and the amici are not; and it would attract pressure to add regulatory-takings language, which the ballot record says must be refused. It does not overlap any existing target.

## 7. Recommendation options

**(a) Accept as target 9.** For: Strong floor (79% D / 85% R) with a six-point gap; twelve state constitutions at 60–86% across all partisan leans; 376–38 in the House; a clean, tested draft form (New Hampshire/North Dakota text plus the two exceptions); symmetric by construction; a precise why-an-amendment answer. Against: the national number is from 2005; §5.1 unresolved.

**(b) Hold as candidate pending fresh polling with party breakdowns** (the method's default for a Strong-but-stale number). Suggested question, forward-looking and mechanism-specific: *"Would you favor or oppose a constitutional amendment providing that no government — federal, state, or local — may take a person's home, business, farm, or land in order to transfer it to another private owner for economic development, even if the owner is paid fair market value?"* Follow-ups: (i) with the exceptions named ("except to remove a specific danger to public health or safety, or for roads, utilities, and pipelines open to the public"); (ii) costs named ("even if this means some redevelopment projects, stadiums, or shopping centers cannot be built"); (iii) a *Kelo* re-ask in the 2005 wording for a trend line. Gate: ≥60% in both parties on (i) and (ii). For holding: nothing in the record has moved since 2012, so a poll is likely to confirm; against: holding a Strong candidate on staleness alone would be a first, and the poll is cheap.

**(c) Reject on the existential test.** For: rare in practice, mostly remedied by the states, and a policy preference by the contributor's own account; the Court can fix it; the slate should not spend an amendment on a problem that is not threatening the country. Against: the method has not previously rejected a Strong, symmetric, amendment-only idea on §5.1 alone; the closest precedent (civic information) was unpolled and had no amendment-only answer. Path back if rejected: none needed on the polling; it would reopen only if the founder revises the §5.1 standard or a new wave of abuses raises salience.
