**Status in one line:** 23% (Republicans) on increasing the size of the House, Pew July 2023 — **Weak**, and it is the only national reading with a party breakdown in the modern record; a majority of *both* parties chose "keep it the same," the newest number (54% support, June–July 2026) is advocacy-commissioned with no party split published, and the why-an-amendment test is weak because 435 is a statutory number Congress can change tomorrow — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: an independent poll of the Wyoming-Rule / cube-root framings clearing a 45% Republican floor, plus a statutory repeal of the 1929 cap to satisfy the sequencing rule).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

**The cap is a statute, not a constitutional rule.** The Constitution sets only a ceiling on district size and a floor on state delegations — "The Number of Representatives shall not exceed one for every thirty Thousand, but each State shall have at Least one Representative." The House grew after nearly every census until the 1910 census fixed it at 435. After the 1920 census the House failed to reapportion itself — the only time in its history — in a fight between rural and urban factions, and the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 ended the deadlock by capping membership at the 1910 level and automating reapportionment after each census (House passed June 11, 1929; signed June 18, 1929 — [House Historian](https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1901-1950/The-Permanent-Apportionment-Act-of-1929/)). Under the 2020 census the apportionment population was 331,108,434 across 435 seats — **761,169 people per representative** ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2023/decennial/c2020br-01.html)).

**The main variants**, in the order they appear in the pipeline:

- **Repeal the cap outright / fix a ratio** (roughly one representative per 300,000, in the source thread's version). Statutory as to the cap; constitutional only if a ratio is entrenched.
- **The Wyoming Rule** — divide the national apportionment population by the population of the least-populous state, so the average district approximates the smallest state's. On 2020 numbers the American Academy of Arts and Sciences models this at **574 seats, averaging 572,000 constituents** per member ([*The Case for Enlarging the House*, §5](https://www.amacad.org/ourcommonpurpose/enlarging-the-house/section/5)).
- **The cube-root rule** — set chamber size at the cube root of national population, which the same report models at **692 seats** on 2020 data, noting the United States "has one of the largest gaps between chamber size and the cube root of its population" among democracies (§5).
- **"Article the First"** — the unratified first of the twelve articles sent to the states on September 25, 1789. Eleven states ratified, the last Kentucky on June 27, 1792, one short of the twelve then required; it carries no ratification deadline, remains pending, and would today need 38 ([ratification record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congressional_Apportionment_Amendment)). Two cautions belong with the story: a last-minute change of "less" to "more" in the final clause left the formula mathematically inoperable across part of its range, and read literally against a 331-million population its ratio would require **more than 6,000 representatives**. It is a pedigree argument, not a usable text. Its live legislative vehicle runs the other way: **H.J.Res. 186**, introduced May 14, 2026 by Rep. Issa and referred to the Judiciary Committee, would make the amendment valid "only if it is ratified … not later than December 31, 2026," with any later ratification "not … valid" ([full text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres186ih/html/BILLS-119hjres186ih.htm)) — extinguishing Article the First, not enacting it.

**The leading institutional proposal is statutory.** The American Academy of Arts and Sciences' *Our Common Purpose* Recommendation 1.1 is to "substantially enlarge the House of Representatives through federal legislation," and its FAQ states the reason plainly: "The size of the House is set by statute, so a vote of Congress is all that is needed." The companion report proposes adding 150 seats, for a House of 585, with regular expansion thereafter ([American Academy, Enlarging the House](https://www.amacad.org/ourcommonpurpose/initiative/enlarging-house-representatives)).

**How it entered our pipeline.** From the public-thread mining pass (SELECTION_METHOD §2.4): the top-scoring comment of 478 in the r/PoliticalDiscussion thread, from **u/averageduder** (173 points — repeal the 1929 Act, uncap, ~1 per 300k or the Wyoming Rule), supported by **u/musashisamurai**, **u/VaelinX** (constituents-per-representative as an international outlier), **u/MorganWick** (constitutionalize the cube-root rule), and **u/One_Term2162** (Article the First). The counterargument that this is statutory came from **u/Objective_Aside1858** and **u/the_other_50_percent**; **u/nanotree** and **u/DanforthWhitcomb_** argued smaller districts would still be gerrymandered. Logged in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) and [REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md); added to [CATEGORIES.md](../CATEGORIES.md) 2026-08-12 with the gate "new polling of Wyoming Rule / cube-root framings." The extraction memo flagged the risk this memo confirms: "national polling on enlarging the House has historically been tepid to negative."

**Overlap with an existing target.** Target 6 (Redistricting) covers the mid-decade redistricting ban only ([REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md](../REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md)); it does not touch chamber size, and no current target covers any part of House expansion. The thread's anti-gerrymandering rationale ("gerrymandering means a lot less when there are 3x as many reps") is a claimed side effect, not a redistricting rule, and the American Academy's enlargement analysis does not address gerrymandering or district competitiveness (§5). The categories are adjacent in motive and separate in mechanism.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Pew Research Center, American Trends Panel Wave 130, July 10–16, 2023 (n=8,480 adults; ±1.5 points).** Asked whether the size of the House should be increased, decreased, or kept the same: **29% increase / 53% stay the same / 15% decrease.** By party, **23% of Republicans and Republican leaners** favor increasing it against **35% of Democrats and Democratic leaners** — "larger shares in both parties favor keeping the House at its current size." Republican-leaning independents (30%) run ahead of Republican partisans (19%); adults under 30 favor an increase far more than those 65 and older (44% vs. 17%) — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/how-americans-view-proposals-to-change-the-political-system/); [methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/09/19/views-of-politics-methodology/). **This is the floor number and the only modern national reading with a party split.** For scale, the same survey found congressional term limits at 87%, an age limit for elected officials at 79%, and an age limit for justices at 74% — the House-size item is not depressed by general reluctance to change institutions; it is specifically unpopular.
- **Pew Research Center, American Trends Panel, January 29–February 13, 2018 (n=4,656; ±2.4 points).** A split-form experiment, and the most useful single piece of evidence in the file. Asked cold: **28% increase / 51% same / 18% decrease** — **Republicans 21% / 55% / 22%; Democrats 34% / 49% / 14%.** Asked with historical context — that the first Congress had 65 members to today's 435, and that each member then represented roughly 60,000 people against roughly 700,000 now — the increase share rises to **34%** overall (44% same, 21% decrease), but the movement is one-sided: **Democrats rise to 44%** while Pew reports **no differences among Republicans** — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/5-the-electoral-college-congress-and-representation/); [methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/methodology-91/). The strongest argument for expansion, delivered directly, moved one party and not the other.

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **Verasight for Fix Our House and New America, June 30–July 7, 2026 (n=1,000; ±3.2 points).** **54% support / 27% oppose** "expanding the House of Representatives" with 150 new members — the only majority reading in the record, and 25 points above Pew's 29% three years earlier. The verbatim item, verified on New America's release, is: *"The U.S. House of Representatives has had 435 members since 1911. Since then, the U.S. population has more than tripled. Would you support or oppose adding 150 new members to the House so that each representative serves a smaller number of constituents?"* — 18.3% strongly support, 36.0% somewhat support, 15.3% somewhat oppose, 11.8% strongly oppose, **18.6% not sure**. Two features matter: the stem carries the founding-era growth argument *inside the question*, which is the same information treatment Pew's 2018 split form found moved Democrats and not Republicans; and nearly one in five respondents is unsure, so the majority is built on a soft base — [Fix Our House](https://www.fixourhouse.org/new-poll-shows-growing-support-for-proportional-representation); [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/proportional-representation-poll/); [Newsweek, Aug. 10, 2026](https://www.newsweek.com/voters-back-adding-150-new-lawmakers-congress-12304081). **No party breakdown is published for the House-expansion item**, and no crosstabs are posted. Both sponsors advocate the reform. The same survey reports 50% support / 24% oppose for proportional representation, with the sponsors stating that the 2:1 ratio "holds true for Republicans, Democrats, and Independents alike" — a partisan claim made for that item only, not for House expansion.

**Legislative and ballot record**

- **No roll-call vote and no ballot measure exists.** The cap has stood since 1929 ([House Historian](https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1901-1950/The-Permanent-Apportionment-Act-of-1929/)), and House size is not a question any state can put to its voters. The only live federal vehicle found is H.J.Res. 186, which sits in committee and would end Article the First rather than enlarge the House; the last enlargement bill, the REAL House Act (H.R. 622, 118th Cong.), died in committee without a vote (see Review Additions) ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hjres186ih/html/BILLS-119hjres186ih.htm)). There is no §4.2 evidence of the kind that carried lobbying (Florida's 79%) or privacy (the state amendments) — the two evidence tiers below polling are empty here.

**Proxies (labeled — not evidence for the mechanism)**

- Republican support for *reducing* the House (22% in 2018) is close to Republican support for enlarging it (21%). Opinion is not split between "bigger" and "same" but distributed across all three answers with a majority at "same" — which is why an expansion floor cannot be inferred from general reform appetite.
- The academic literature is thin and old: Brian Frederick, "The People's Perspective on the Size of the People's House," *PS: Political Science & Politics* 41(2), April 2008, 329–335 ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/abs/peoples-perspective-on-the-size-of-the-peopleshouse/8EC3C4EC8F723FA590ECF2E3E1C43E52)). An open-access copy is available and its figures — including party crosstabs on three separate framings — are now used; see Review Additions.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **Nothing has ever polled the named framings.** No survey found asks about the Wyoming Rule, the cube-root rule, or a constitutional ratio by name or description. The gate CATEGORIES.md set on 2026-08-12 is unmet, and this memo does not close it.
- **The one majority reading is the one that cannot carry a verdict.** The 54% is advocacy-commissioned, has no party split, and describes a seat count (150) rather than a principle. Under §4.2 it can serve only as corroboration, and here there is nothing for it to corroborate — it points the opposite way from the only independent measure.
- **Question format is doing work.** Pew asks increase/decrease/same, offering a status-quo option; Verasight asks support/oppose, which does not. A three-way question with a "keep it the same" answer reads lower than a two-way form on the same underlying opinion, so part of the 29%→54% gap is likely format rather than movement. The two numbers are not comparable, and no independent pollster has run the support/oppose form.
- **Not regime-coded, but not stable either.** Nothing ties either party's position to who holds the chamber. The record does show that the modest Democratic edge is information-sensitive (2018: 34%→44% on context) while the Republican number did not move under the same treatment.
- **Salience is very low.** No independent-tier pollster — Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, UMD PPC — has asked about House size in the 2024–2026 window, and Cato/Morning Consult's June 2026 amendment battery of eight ideas ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/how-americans-would-change-the-constitution-new-poll-12152648)) does not include it. Low salience cuts both ways: opinion is unformed and therefore movable, but the 23% is a soft number rather than hardened opposition.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **23% (Republicans)** on increasing the size of the House, Pew July 2023 → **Weak** per §4.1 (< 45%). The corroborating independent reading is 21% R (Pew 2018), unchanged after an information treatment. The Democratic figure — 35% in 2023, 34–44% in 2018 — is itself below the Moderate band, so this is not a high-topline idea with a partisan floor. **Neither party has ever recorded majority support in an independent poll.** That is a worse pattern than every category rejected on polling: the Electoral College (46R/80D), the self-pardon ban (43R/81D), and qualified immunity (42R/79D) each had one party well above 60%.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *High-topline / low-floor* — **does not apply**, and the reason matters: there is no high topline to hide a split. The 29% overall is itself the finding.
  - *Staleness* — **at risk.** The only party-split data are 2023 and 2018; the newest reading of any kind is a 2026 advocacy poll. If movement has occurred since 2023, the floor understates support — the one live possibility that argues for holding rather than rejecting.
  - *Framing dependency* — **at risk, unresolved.** The 29%→54% gap between Pew's three-way form and Verasight's support/oppose form is confounded with sponsorship and question content. Until an independent pollster runs the support/oppose form, we cannot say whether a framing clears the bar.
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **tested once, and it failed.** Frederick's 2006 Knowledge Networks survey put both sides of the trade-off in the question stem — better representation against greater cost and a less efficient legislative process — and support fell to **18.9% overall with a Republican floor of 11.3%**, the lowest reading in the file (see Review Additions). That is the only costs-named measurement in the record, it is twenty years old, and it points the same direction as Pew. The depth flank on the 54% is therefore not merely untested; the one time costs were named, support did not survive them.
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **cleared.** No evidence that either party's position depends on who holds the chamber.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **not applicable in the usual form** (no "abuse" both parties condemn), but the structural analogue is present: the diagnosis in the source thread — districts are too big, gerrymandering too easy — has never been measured as a public grievance. Expansion is a remedy for a problem the public has not been asked whether it has.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **open.** The ratio change is real and documented: 65 members at roughly 60,000 constituents each in the first Congress against 435 at 761,169 today ([Pew 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/5-the-electoral-college-congress-and-representation/); [Census 2020](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2023/decennial/c2020br-01.html)), and the American Academy places the United States among the widest gaps between chamber size and the cube root of population in the democratic world (§5). Whether that threatens the country's future or is a good-government improvement is the judgment §5.1 reserves — and the public's own answer, 53% "keep it the same," is not the answer of a country that experiences this as a crisis. Stated, not decided.
  - *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes, and this is the category's strongest result.** The American Academy ran 10,000 simulations at each House size from 435 to 700 seats on 2020 results, applying national and state-level vote shocks, and found minimal partisan effect in either direction: Republicans gain roughly 2–3 points in probability of control between 436 and 524 seats; Democrats' odds rise above 525 seats but "never by more than roughly 2.7 percent"; and "changing the size of the House would not generate a strong partisan shift in either control of the House or the outcome of the Electoral College," with Electoral College effects favoring larger states but showing "no partisan impact" ([American Academy, §6](https://www.amacad.org/ourcommonpurpose/enlarging-the-house/section/6) — a think-tank source that advocates enlargement; method and figures published). A rule that shifts control probability two to three points in *both* directions depending on where the number lands is close to the definition of a rule written behind the veil.
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **weak, and this is the second decisive finding.** House size has been set by statute since 1911 and 1929; Congress can enlarge the House tomorrow by ordinary legislation, which is what the leading institutional proposal recommends ("a vote of Congress is all that is needed"). None of the four accepted answers applies: no court has struck an enlargement statute, the Court has not exempted itself, Congress is not structurally barred from acting (unlike term limits, where *Thornton* forces Article V), and the text is not silent — Article I fixes the 30,000 ceiling and the one-per-state floor and leaves the rest to Congress. The thread's entrenchment argument — "make it an amendment and it's harder to combat" — reads §7 backwards: the sequencing rule locks in agreement the country has *already reached*. Here the statute has never been passed, so there is nothing yet to lock in.

**Reading the table.** Only two framings have been measured by an independent pollster, and they are the same framing five years apart: both land at 21–23% among Republicans. The one majority result is advocacy-commissioned, format-incomparable, and publishes no floor. The abuse-framing-versus-remedy-framing gap that rescues other categories has no analogue here, because the "abuse" — an unrepresentative ratio — has never been polled as a grievance in its own right; the nearest thing is Pew's 2018 context treatment, the argument in its most favorable form, which moved Republicans by zero. The framings the gate named — Wyoming Rule and cube root — remain untested, so the honest verdict is that the category's best case has not been measured, not that it was measured and failed.

One framing distinction changes what the amendment *is*: constitutionalizing a *ratio* (Article the First's approach, which produces a chamber of thousands) is not the same as constitutionalizing a *floor or formula* (Wyoming Rule, cube root). Any poll commissioned here must test the formula, not the slogan.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** An independent national poll (Pew, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Monmouth, or UMD PPC's deliberative format) with published party crosstabs, testing the mechanism rather than the direction — for example: *"The number of House seats has been fixed at 435 by law since 1929, while the average district has grown to about 760,000 people. Would you favor or oppose setting the size of the House by a fixed formula tied to population — for instance, so that the average district is about the size of the smallest state — which would add roughly 140 seats?"* — asked alongside a costs-named follow-up naming the added members and their salaries and offices. The threshold is a **Republican floor of 45% or better**, which would move the category from Weak to Moderate; **60% or better in both parties** would make it a target on the polling. A second route is a §4.2 event: passage of a statutory enlargement, or a serious cross-party enlargement vote, which would supply the roll-call evidence the file lacks and satisfy the sequencing rule at once.

**Evidence that would move it out.** A well-formed independent poll of the Wyoming-Rule or cube-root mechanism returning a Republican floor still below 45% would resolve the framing question against the category and make this a rejection on the bipartisan floor — the test that rejected the Electoral College and the self-pardon ban. Separately, the **why-an-amendment test (§5.3) is already close to failing**: if a statutory enlargement passes, or if the founder judges that a statutorily changeable number does not belong in the Constitution before the statute has ever been tried, the category moves to the statutory list rather than to a target — the disposition given to special-counsel protections and DOJ firewall laws.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate — gate: commission the Wyoming-Rule / cube-root poll, and treat statutory repeal of the 1929 cap as the prerequisite step.** Three findings drive this. First, the only independent evidence (§2) puts the floor at 23% R with 53% of the country choosing the status quo — Weak under §4.1 and ordinarily disqualifying — but the framings the gate named have never been asked, and the 2018 split-form result (§4) shows a low-salience opinion that information can move, so rejecting now would reject a case that has not been made. Second, the symmetry test (§3) passes on published simulation evidence more cleanly than almost anything in the corpus, which is why the category is worth the price of a poll rather than a quiet close. Third, cutting the other way, the why-an-amendment answer (§3) is weak on its face and the sequencing rule is unsatisfied: 435 is a number Congress can change by majority vote and has never tried to, so even a Strong floor would argue first for the statute and only later for constitutional lock-in of a formula. The founder decides; a defensible alternative on this record is to move the category to the statutory list now and revisit only if Congress acts.


## Review Additions (2026-08-18)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **Statutory enlargement has been proposed, and did not move.** The Restoring Equal and Accountable Legislators in the House (REAL House) Act, [H.R. 622, 118th Congress](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/622), introduced by Rep. Earl Blumenauer on January 30, 2023, would have raised the House from 435 to 585 members beginning with the apportionment after the 2030 census, with an automatic mechanism thereafter; it drew two cosponsors, both Democrats ([GovTrack cosponsor list](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/118/hr622/cosponsors)), received no committee action or floor vote, and expired with the 118th Congress ([Democracy Docket, Feb. 1, 2023](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/u-s-rep-earl-blumenauer-introduces-bill-to-expand-house-to-585-members/); [American Redistricting Project summary](https://thearp.org/blog/apportionment/restoring-equal-and-accountable-legislators-house-act/)). Bearing on the gate: it confirms the why-an-amendment analysis (enlargement is done by statute) and adds a data point to §4.2 — the statutory route has been tried once in the modern era and drew no cross-party motion. No change to the verdict or recommendation.

- **The 2008 academic figures are open-access, and they add a third independent Republican floor — the lowest in the file.** Brian Frederick's *PS* article reports a Knowledge Networks probability panel fielded **September 13–19, 2006 (n=1,020 of 1,425 invited; 71.6% completion)**, and its full text with party crosstabs is posted by Bridgewater State University ([open-access PDF](https://vc.bridgew.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1013&context=polisci_fac)). Three framings were tested, each with a party breakdown:
  - **The costs-named trade-off item** — the stem recites that the House began with 65 members at roughly 30,000 people per district and now has 435 at roughly 640,000, then gives *both* arguments (closer representation for an increase; greater cost and a less efficient legislative process against it): **18.9% increase / 61.9% keep at current size / 19.3% decrease**, with **Republicans 11.3%**, Democrats 23.7%, Independents/Other 22.7%. Every subgroup reported in the study gave majority support to keeping the House at its present size.
  - **Enlarging the House to stop states losing seats after reapportionment** — **33.6% support / 66.4% oppose**, with **Republicans 26.2%**, Democrats 39.2%, Independents/Other 33.4%.
  - **Enlarging the House to create more opportunities for women and racial minorities to be elected** — the highest-scoring framing at **48.6% supportive** (15.5% very, 33.1% somewhat) against 51.4% opposed, and also the most polarized: **Republicans 33.9% supportive** (8.2% very, 25.7% somewhat) against Democrats 59.7%. Frederick's own conclusion is that a proposal built on this rationale "would not garner a bipartisan consensus."
  Bearing on the gate: this is a third independent measurement of a Republican floor on House size, it is the only one that names the costs, and at 11.3% it is the lowest number in the memo. It does not change the verdict — Weak was already the finding — but it removes "the costs have never been named" from the open questions in §3, and it means three independent surveys twenty, eight, and three years apart all place the Republican floor between 11% and 23%. No change to the recommendation: the Wyoming-Rule and cube-root framings the gate named remain untested, which is still the reason to hold rather than reject.
- **The 2026 majority reading carries the argument inside the question, and publishes no party split.** New America's release gives the verbatim House-expansion item and its five-point distribution (quoted in §2) and confirms that **no party breakdown is published for that item** ([New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/proportional-representation-poll/)). The sponsors' partisan claim — that the 2:1 support ratio "holds true for Republicans, Democrats, and Independents alike" — attaches to the proportional-representation item, not to House expansion. The memo's hedge stands as written; what is new is that the 54% is now known to rest on a stem containing the pro-expansion argument and on an 18.6% unsure share. No change to the gate result or recommendation.
