# Fair Single-Member Districting — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

**Status in one line:** 57% (Republicans) on "it is bad for the country when one political party controls how voting districts are drawn," Noble Predictive Insights for Common Cause (advocacy), Aug–Sept 2025 — **Moderate on the best available number, and no independent poll has ever asked about either operative mechanism** — held by the abuse-vs-remedy gap, the why-an-amendment answer being partly satisfied by Target 6 already, and a ballot record that runs against the polling — recommendation: hold as a candidate (gate: an independent poll of the "who may not draw" wording and of the Florida anti-favoritism text, each clearing a 60% Republican floor).

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The category entered the pipeline as a one-line founder question — **"Restrict gerrymandering via fair single-member districting?"** (Michael Hamilton, [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md)) — filed alongside proportional representation and fusion voting, and it has carried a single-word gate ever since: *unpolled*. The name compresses two ideas that must be separated before anything can be measured.

**The "single-member" half is already settled law and is not the live question.** Single-member House districts are required by statute — [2 U.S.C. §2c](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/2/2c) — and Congress imposed districting standards by statute from 1842 through the Act of 1911, including "no district electing more than one Representative," until the 1929 Reapportionment Act omitted them ([*Wood v. Broom*, 287 U.S. 1 (1932)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/287/1.html)). Constitutionalizing that rule would entrench the status quo against the *opposite* candidate category — proportional representation, which requires multi-member districts. This memo therefore reads the category as the founder's phrasing indicates: **"fair … districting" is the operative demand, and "single-member" describes the system within which fairness is to be secured.**

**What remains is precisely the two layers Target 6 held back.** [REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md](../REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md) accepted a minimal two-sentence amendment — the once-per-decade timing rule with a court-remedy exception, plus a prospective post-2030 effective date — and moved two layers to "Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar":

- **(a) The anti-favoritism floor** — *"No districting plan shall be drawn with the intent, or have the effect, of unduly favoring or disfavoring any political party."* The *Rucho* reversal, with one real-world model: Florida's Fair Districts Amendment 6 (2010), which wrote into Art. III §20 that no plan or district "shall be drawn with the intent to favor or disfavor a political party or an incumbent" — [Fair Districts Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Districts_Amendment), passed with **62.93%** against a 60% threshold.
- **(b) The neutral-authority principle** — *"Each State shall vest the establishment of districts in a body whose members are not, and are not appointed by, officers or candidates whose districts the body establishes."* The international norm, and the layer nearly every commission ballot measure has tested.

Federal vehicles for both exist: the [Tanner–Cooper FAIR Act, H.R. 5426](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5426/all-info) (commissions plus a re-redistricting ban, a template dating to 2005) and the [Padilla–Lofgren Redistricting Reform Act, S. 2885](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2885/text). Neither has moved. In the public thread that fed the pipeline, neutral or algorithmic line-drawing was among the most-requested reforms — u/nanotree, u/rigormorty, u/Journey2Jess and others ([REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md](../REDDIT_THREAD_IDEAS.md)).

**Overlap discipline.** The *timing* of redraws is covered — Target 6 owns it, with its own research doc and draft text. This candidate is the *content and authorship* of maps: who draws them, and whether a map may be drawn to favor a party at all. A state that redraws exactly once per decade, on schedule, and draws a maximally partisan map through its legislature complies fully with Target 6.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polls with party breakdowns**

- **Economist/YouGov, April 24–27, 2026.** States should **not** be allowed to draw congressional districts in a way that intentionally favors one party: **71% overall — 69% R / 74% D / 70% I**, with 7% saying it should be allowed — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/54644-most-americans-say-partisan-gerrymandering-should-not-be-allowed-april-24-27-2026-economist-yougov-poll). The newest independent reading and the highest Republican floor in the file — but it measures the *principle* that gerrymandering is wrong, not either operative mechanism.
- **NBC News Decision Desk / SurveyMonkey, Aug. 13 – Sept. 1, 2025 (n=30,196 adults, ±1.9).** **82%** prefer congressional districts drawn by nonpartisan commissions rather than by the party in power. No national party split is published; the regional breakdown is the more useful datum — **71% of Republicans in Republican-controlled states** and 88% of Democrats in Democratic-controlled states prefer commissions, including 66% of Texas Republicans and nearly 80% of California Democrats — [NBC News, via Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/poll-most-americans-oppose-political-130000966.html). Commission support survives in the states actively drawing co-partisan maps — the strongest evidence for the neutral-authority layer.
- **Noble Predictive Insights for The Center Square, June 1–4, 2026 (n=2,585 RV; 915 R, 1,013 D, 297 true independents; ±1.93).** Whom do you trust most to draw fair congressional lines: independent commissions **41%**, state legislatures 16%, courts 15%, not sure 27%. By party — **R commissions 38% / legislatures 19%; D 45% / 15%; true independents 38% / 8%, 43% not sure** — [Center Square via Newsline](https://www.newslinelocal.com/p/poll-voters-back-redistricting-commissions). An opt-in panel, media-commissioned. As a *forced choice among institutions* rather than favor/oppose, no option reaches a majority: 38% R is a plurality preference, not a floor, and is not comparable to the figures above (§11).
- **AP-NORC, June 22–26, 2023 (n=1,220 adults, ±3.9).** Gerrymandering — defined as "the practice of states drawing legislative districts that intentionally favor one political party" — is a "major problem" for **65%**; Democrats more than Republicans, with **"just over half" of Republicans** agreeing. No exact Republican figure is published — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/partisan-views-of-the-electoral-system/). The corpus's "~half of R" is accurate as far as the source goes; a precise number does not exist to cite.

**Advocacy-commissioned polls (flagged; cannot carry a verdict)**

- **Noble Predictive Insights for Common Cause, Aug. 26 – Sept. 2, 2025 (2,000+ RV nationally, plus 400–500 each in CA, FL, IL, NY, TX).** It is bad for the country when one political party controls how voting districts are drawn: **57% R / 76% D / 72% I**. Independent citizen commissions to draw district lines: **77% nationally** (FL 76%, TX 74%, NY 78%, IL 75%, CA 80%) — no party split on the commission item. Boundaries should prioritize "community interests ahead of political advantage": 78%. Companion mid-decade item: oppose 51% R / 70% D / 60% I — [Common Cause](https://www.commoncause.org/press/september-poll-64-of-republican-and-independent-voters-want-ban-for-mid-decade-redistricting/). The 57% R is the closest thing in existence to a party-broken-out number on the *anti-favoritism principle*, and it comes from a reform advocacy group.
- **R Street Institute, published March 9, 2021.** **57% of Republican voters** support independent redistricting commissions; 31% approve of how their state draws boundaries. R Street is a right-leaning think tank, which makes the finding notable, but neither [R Street's page](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/poll-57-of-republicans-support-independent-redistricting-commissions/) nor [Campaign Legal Center's write-up](https://campaignlegal.org/update/poll-57-republicans-support-independent-redistricting-commissions) publishes fieldwork dates, sample size, or wording. Five years old and methodologically unverifiable; corroboration only.

**Ballot results — the strongest evidence class per §4.2, and the one that cuts both ways**

Red, blue and purple states have all approved neutral-authority measures at 50–71% — full-spectrum evidence the polling cannot supply on its own. But two of the seven went the other way, and the reversals are the recent ones.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **The mechanism has never been polled by an independent pollster with a party breakdown.** Everything above is the *principle* (71%/82%/65%), an *advocacy* reading of the mechanism (57% R, 77%), or a *forced choice among institutions* (38% R). Searches of 2024–2026 fieldwork from Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac and PRRI surfaced no independent national poll asking whether districts should be drawn by a body insulated from the officeholders whose seats are drawn, and none asking whether the Constitution should forbid drawing maps to favor a party. That gap is the finding.
- **Situational defection is documented, and symmetric.** 70% of Democrats say gerrymandering is "never acceptable," yet only **18%** would keep California's independent commission if it cost their party seats, and 63% wanted their party to redraw in response — [Politico / UC Berkeley Citrin Center, Aug. 2025 (n=1,177 RV)](https://www.aol.com/majority-dems-gerrymandering-never-acceptable-141205887.html). On the other side, 66% of Texas Republicans still preferred nonpartisan commissions while their state drew a co-partisan map. Stated support is real and cross-party; it is also soft the moment seats are the price.
- **Regime coding, measured.** Marquette asked the symmetric question naming *both* Texas and California: oppose out-of-cycle partisan redraws **71–28 overall; R 70–30, I 68–31, D 73–27** (Nov. 5–12, 2025), versus **R 62–38 and D 80–20** in the Sept. 15–24, 2025 wave — [Marquette PDF](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/MLSPSC29PressReleaseNationalIssues.pdf). Republican opposition rose eight points and Democratic opposition fell seven after California entered the arms race. Support tracks who is ahead on maps — but tracks it *symmetrically*, which is a point in the category's favor.
- **The corpus's "82% overall but 57% R" pairing is not like-for-like.** The 82% is NBC 2025 (commissions vs. party in power); the 57% R is R Street 2021, five years earlier, different wording, undisclosed methodology. NBC's own Republican subgroup — 71% in Republican-controlled states — is the better read on that question, and 14 points higher.
- **Wording sensitivity is the whole category.** "Should politicians draw their own districts?" and "should the federal Constitution forbid a state from drawing a map that favors a party?" are different questions with, on all available evidence, different answers. The first has never been asked in a binding form; the second has never been asked.
- **Ohio 2024 is the cautionary datum, and its margin has an explanation.** It failed 53.7–46.3 after the Ballot Board approved language on a [3–2 party-line vote](https://spectrumnews1.com/oh/columbus/news/2024/09/19/ohio-officials-approve-issue-1-ballot-language) describing the anti-gerrymandering amendment as *requiring* gerrymandering; sponsors sued and the state supreme court ordered two of eight disputed sections rewritten. Evidence that commission support does not survive a hostile summary — not that a red-state electorate rejects commissions on the merits.
- **Utah 2026 is the newest real-world datum and points the other way.** A legislature-backed initiative to eliminate the Proposition 4 commission submitted over 200,000 signatures with more than 160,000 verified, but roughly 9,000 voters formally withdrew signatures, dropping one Senate district below the 8% threshold and keeping repeal off the ballot — [Ballotpedia News, March 28, 2026](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/03/28/utah-redistricting-initiative-to-repeal-proposition-4-will-not-be-on-the-2026-ballot-after-enough-voters-withdraw-their-signatures/). In the state where the commission passed by 0.34 points, voters defended it.

**Proxies (labeled — not evidence for either mechanism)**

- Fix Our House and New America (advocacy), reported Aug. 10, 2026 (n=1,000): **50% support proportional representation, ~25% oppose, ~33% unsure**; 61% would prefer "several representatives for a larger area, where you are more likely to have one who shares your views" over one representative; about 6 in 10 Trump voters *who have an opinion* back PR; only ~2% could name proportional representation unprompted — [NPR/OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/08/10/proportional-representation-has-majority-support-in-new-poll/). The closest thing to a poll on the "single-member" half of the category name, and it runs *against* constitutionalizing single-member districts. An advocacy poll on a concept with ~2% unaided awareness — a weak proxy.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **57% (Republicans)** on "bad for the country when one political party controls how voting districts are drawn," Noble Predictive Insights for Common Cause, Aug–Sept 2025 → **Moderate** per §4.1. Three heavy qualifications: the source is advocacy-commissioned and cannot carry a verdict alone (§4.2); it measures a *diagnosis*, not either remedy; and the independent alternatives measure different things — 69% R on the principle (Economist/YouGov 2026), 71% R in Republican-controlled states for commissions (NBC 2025 subgroup), 38% R as a forced-choice preference (Noble 2026), "just over half" of R (AP-NORC 2023). **On the mechanism itself the honest verdict is Unpolled**: no independent national poll with a party breakdown has ever asked either operative question.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - **High-topline / low-floor — at risk, unquantified.** 82% overall on commissions with no published national party split is exactly the shape the gate is built to catch. The subgroup evidence is reassuring (71% R in R-controlled states); the forced-choice evidence is not (38% R). Until an independent poll publishes a national R figure on the commission question, the category cannot be scored honestly.
  - **Depth / trade-off collapse — confirmed, and the category's central problem.** The Citrin Center number is the cleanest trade-off experiment in the corpus: 70% of Democrats call gerrymandering never acceptable; 18% would keep their state's commission if it cost seats. A 52-point collapse when the cost is named — the same shape as the balanced-budget rejection (69–80% → 31%). The mitigating difference: a *national, simultaneous* rule removes the cost that causes the collapse, because no state disarms unilaterally. That is a design answer, not a measured one.
  - **Regime coding — present but symmetric.** R opposition to partisan redraws rose 62→70 and D opposition fell 80→73 across Marquette's two 2025 waves as the arms race changed hands. Both parties move with who is ahead; neither party's support is an artifact of currently holding the maps. The mildest version of the failure mode.
  - **Framing dependency — confirmed, and the gap is large.** Condemnation framings return 69–71% R; institutional-choice framings return 38% R; the "who must draw" mandate carries the federalism problem the research doc names, and "who may not draw" has never been tested. The framing that clears the bar is not the thing that would be drafted.
  - **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — confirmed, and it is why this is a candidate rather than a target.** Both parties reject the abuse decisively (69% R / 74% D that partisan gerrymandering should not be allowed; 57% R that one-party control of districting is bad for the country). The structural remedies have no independent measurement at all. This is the DOJ-independence pattern in §4.3, at a much higher abuse floor.
  - **Staleness — cleared.** Fieldwork from April, June and August 2026 plus 2025 waves. The stale items (R Street 2021, AP-NORC 2023) are corroborating, not load-bearing.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - **Existential-problem — passes.** Districting determines who governs. FairVote's [Monopoly Politics 2026](https://fairvote.org/report/monopoly-politics-2026-update/) projects 81% of House seats already effectively decided, and the 2025–26 cycle saw ten states redraw mid-decade, netting roughly +14 R / +6 D and dissolving fourteen competitive seats ([REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md](../REDISTRICTING_RESEARCH.md)). The link between voters and representatives is what the mission's Democracy pillar names — not a good-government nicety.
  - **Symmetry — passes on the anti-favoritism layer, open on neutral authority.** "No map may be drawn to favor a party" binds every legislature in every state forever and can be sold with paired abuses from the same cycle: a mid-decade redraw in one state answered by a counter-map in another. The neutral-authority layer is harder — it federalizes a state institutional-design choice, and the states that would have to change most are not evenly distributed. Prospective effective dating (the Target 6 device) would carry most of the symmetry weight for either.
  - **Why-an-amendment — passes cleanly for the anti-favoritism floor; weaker for neutral authority.** [*Rucho v. Common Cause*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/18-422) (June 27, 2019, 5–4) held partisan-gerrymandering claims to be political questions "beyond the reach of the federal courts" for want of judicially manageable standards — and the majority's answer to the Florida example is the argument for an amendment in one sentence: *"The answer is that there is no 'Fair Districts Amendment' to the Federal Constitution."* The same opinion conceded that state text supplies a workable standard: *"We do not understand how the dissent can maintain that a provision saying that no districting plan 'shall be drawn with the intent to favor or disfavor a political party' provides little guidance."* That is §5.3 answer #4 (the text is silent) plus a direct judicial invitation. For neutral authority the answer is thinner: [*Arizona State Legislature v. AIRC*](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-129/arizona-state-legislature-v-arizona-independent-redistricting-commission/) (2015) already permits commissions, states adopt them by initiative, and Congress could mandate them under the Elections Clause — the case for constitutional altitude there is durability (Missouri's constitutional text was repealed by simple-majority referendum in 2020), not impossibility.

| Framing | Floor | Source | Clears? |

| "No plan shall be drawn with intent to favor or disfavor a party" (the operative text) | **never polled** | — | Unknown |

The gap between rows one and eight is the category in miniature. Voters reject partisan gerrymandering by a 69% Republican margin and reject it again at the ballot box — Florida wrote the operative anti-favoritism sentence into its constitution with 62.93% against a 60% threshold, and its supreme court then enforced it, striking the 2012 congressional map for unconstitutional partisan intent and ordering eight districts redrawn ([*League of Women Voters of Florida v. Detzner*, July 9, 2015](https://thearp.org/litigation/league-women-voters-florida-v-detzner/)). That is a real answer to the manageability objection and a real ballot result. What is missing is any measurement of whether that sentence keeps a Republican majority when it is *federal* and binds every state — and Florida's later record, where a reshaped state supreme court upheld a 20–8 map against a Fair Districts challenge in July 2025 and the legislature enacted a further map in 2026 with the amendment still on the books, is the reminder that a favoritism standard's force tracks the court applying it.

The framings are also separated by *who* is named. Every framing polling above 65% among Republicans describes what states may **not** do. Every framing that federalizes what states **must** build — a commission of a particular composition — either drops (38% forced-choice) or has never been asked. That asymmetry is the practical case for testing the anti-favoritism floor first: it is the negative-rule version, and negative rules are what the category's own evidence says clears.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** Two independent national polls (Pew, Marquette, AP-NORC or Economist/YouGov panels, or foundation-commissioned on one of them), both-parties and forward-looking, each clearing a **60% Republican floor**:

1. *Anti-favoritism floor, Florida wording, federalized:* "Should the Constitution provide that no congressional district may be drawn with the intent to favor or disfavor a political party — applying to every state and every future legislature, whichever party controls it?" Florida's 62.93% ballot result and *Rucho*'s own concession give this the strongest prior.
2. *Neutral authority, negative wording:* "Should the officeholders whose own districts are being drawn be barred from drawing them?" — the "who may not draw" framing the research doc named as the path back, avoiding the federalism wedge that "each state must create a commission" carries.

Either must then survive a costs-named follow-up in the Citrin Center's style ("even if it means your party wins fewer seats in your state") without dropping below Moderate. A second route: a neutral-authority or fairness-standard ballot measure passing after 2024 in a state whose legislature opposed it, on clean ballot language — the Ohio counterfactual run properly.

**Evidence that would move it out.** A replicated independent finding that the Republican floor on either operative wording sits below 45%, or a re-run of the trade-off question showing *both* parties' support collapsing when seats are the price. The failing test would be depth (§4.3 trade-off collapse) reinforced by the abuse-vs-remedy gap — the balanced-budget pattern, where the principle is popular and the priced version is not.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate, gated on the two polls above.** The category passes the existential-problem test and, for the anti-favoritism layer, passes the why-an-amendment test as cleanly as anything in the corpus — *Rucho* closed the federal courthouse and said in terms that the missing thing is a Fair Districts Amendment to the federal Constitution (§3). What it lacks is a single independent number on either mechanism, and the one trade-off experiment on record shows a 52-point collapse when seats are priced in (§2). Folding into Target 6 now would attach an unpolled layer to a target whose research doc deliberately stripped it, trading a two-sentence amendment with a ~70% symmetric floor for one carrying the ratification opposition that doc predicts would concentrate there — the sequencing rule (§7: ratify consensus, don't manufacture it) argues for measuring first. If the anti-favoritism poll clears 60% R, the natural home is not a standalone amendment but a **third clause in Target 6's package**, using the Florida wording rather than the "unduly favoring" formulation, since "unduly" hands its meaning to the Court that decided *Rucho* (§4). The neutral-authority layer should be held separately and longer: its why-an-amendment answer is durability rather than necessity, and Ohio 2024 is the only place this category has lost a clean fight. Founder decides.
