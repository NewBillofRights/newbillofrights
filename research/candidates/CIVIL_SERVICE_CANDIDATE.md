# Civil Servant Independence — Candidate Category Memo

*Candidate since 2026-08-06. Memo written 2026-08-18 to evaluate the category under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](../SELECTION_METHOD.md) by the same criteria as the target and rejected categories. Every figure below was verified against the linked source on the date of writing.*

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 1 correction applied in place (git history has the diff).*

**Status in one line:** 95% (Republicans, 2024) on merit-based hiring and promotion — but the same series' nonpartisan-civil-service item fell from 87% to 66% among Republicans in one year and rebounded to 78% the next, which is the corpus's clearest demonstration that this category's floor tracks who holds the presidency rather than a settled principle — Moderate-to-Strong on the principle, unpolled on every remedy, and the why-an-amendment test is contradicted by the Supreme Court's express preservation of the civil service system — recommendation: **hold as a candidate (gate: a forward-looking both-parties poll of the operative protection, plus an answer to why a statute cannot do this)**.

## 1. The proposal as commonly framed

The idea is to put in the Constitution what the [Pendleton Act of 1883](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/pendleton-act) and the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 put in statute: that federal employment below the political appointee layer is obtained by merit and cannot be taken away for political reasons. The contributor draft in [AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md](../AMENDMENT_IDEAS.md) is **"Establishing a Merit-Based Civil Service Clause"** (Michael Hamilton, 2026-08-06): *amend the Constitution to explicitly establish a non-partisan, merit-based civil service, guaranteeing career public servants protection against arbitrary political firing or patronage replacement.* It entered as one of the founder's executive-accountability drafts and was triaged to Candidate the same day, gated "regime-coded."

Three variants are separable, and they poll differently enough that treating them as one proposal is the category's central measurement error:

- **(a) The merit-entry principle** — hiring and promotion on merit rather than political belief; what the Pendleton Act did.
- **(b) The tenure protection** — career employees may not be removed for political reasons: a constitutional floor under the adverse-action and appeal procedures now in 5 U.S.C. chapters 43 and 75 and the Merit Systems Protection Board's jurisdiction.
- **(c) Structural insulation of the agencies themselves** — for-cause removal protection for agency heads and commissioners. **Not evaluated here:** that is the FCC/independent-agency candidate's territory, and the corpus already carries its numbers (removal protections 71%, 39% R floor, Marquette Nov 2025; 73%, 54% R floor, Marquette May 2026 — [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](../POLLING_RESEARCH.md) Review Additions).

The live legal vehicle is **Schedule Policy/Career**. Executive Order 14171 (January 20, 2025) directed OPM to redesignate policy-influencing positions; the final rule, *Improving Performance, Accountability and Responsiveness in the Civil Service*, [91 FR 5580 (February 6, 2026)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/06/2026-02375/improving-performance-accountability-and-responsiveness-in-the-civil-service), effective March 9, 2026, moves those positions into the excepted service as at-will jobs "excepted from adverse action procedures or appeals." The rule states that positions "will remain career jobs filled on a nonpartisan basis," "explicitly prohibits political patronage, loyalty tests, or political discrimination," and may not be used for mass layoffs ([OPM release](https://www.opm.gov/news/news-releases/opm-finalizes-schedule-policycareer-rule-to-strengthen-accountability/)); OPM stated that "its initial estimate of 50,000 positions was a reasonable approximation of potential conversions" ([rule text](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/text/2026/02/06/2026-02375.txt)). Note what that does to the polling: the rule claims variant (a) while changing variant (b), so a survey asking only about merit hiring cannot distinguish the rule's supporters from its opponents.

Congressional vehicles are statutory on both sides: the *Saving the Civil Service Act* ([H.R. 492](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/492/cosponsors) / [S. 134](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/134/cosponsors), 119th Congress) would bar moving competitive-service positions into the excepted service beyond the schedules in effect September 30, 2020; the *Protect America's Workforce Act* (H.R. 2550, and Senate companion [S. 2837](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2837/cosponsors)) would nullify the collective-bargaining orders. **None of them is bipartisan in any meaningful sense** — the cosponsor counts are in Review Additions, and they matter to §5: the second route the memo names for moving this category in is "a majority of *both* caucuses for a statutory merit-protection bill," and these bills are between 4 and 0 Republicans short of that. No constitutional amendment on the civil service has been introduced.

## 2. The evidence

**Independent polling — the Partnership for Public Service / Impact Research series.** The only series that asks the principle repeatedly with party breakdowns; its three waves are effectively the whole record. Nationally representative samples of 800–1,000 adults.

- **March 25 – April 1, 2024 (n=800, ±3.5).** "Civil servants should be hired and promoted based on merit rather than their political beliefs": **95% — 95% R / 96% D / 94% I.** "An apolitical civil service is critical to a strong democracy": **87%**, Republicans and Democrats at approximately the same rate (~87% each). Civil servants should serve the people more than any individual president: **90% — 90% R / 91% D / 83% I.** "Presidents should be able to fire any civil servants that they choose for any reason": **25% agree / 72% disagree — 37% R / 11% D** — [*The State of Public Trust in Government 2024*](https://ourpublicservice.org/publications/the-state-of-public-trust-in-government-2024/); [press release](https://ourpublicservice.org/press-release/new-partnership-for-public-service-report-finds-despite-low-levels-of-trust-in-the-federal-government-the-american-public-overwhelmingly-supports-a-nonpartisan-merit-based-civil-service/).
- **March 31 – April 6, 2025 (n=800, ±3.5).** "A nonpartisan civil service is important for having a strong American democracy": **66% overall — 66% R (from ~87–89%) / 81% D (from 88%)**, independents down sharply. Opposition to presidents filling federal jobs with policy allies: **71% → 47% overall; R 65% → 26%; I 72% → 40%.** Support for firing any civil servant for any reason: **25% → 48% overall; R 37% → 60%** — [*The State of Public Trust in Government 2025*](https://ourpublicservice.org/publications/the-state-of-public-trust-in-government-2025/); [report page](https://ourpublicservice.org/know-the-facts/resource-library/reports/the-state-of-public-trust-in-government-2025); [GovExec](https://www.govexec.com/workforce/2025/08/fewer-republicans-view-nonpartisan-civil-service-essential-survey-shows/407403/).
- **March 31 – April 5, 2026 (n=1,000, ±3.1).** Same item: **76% overall, up 10 points; Republicans 66% → 78%.** Opposition to filling any job with policy allies recovered to **53%** (agreement 38%). Competent **65%** (from 57%; independents 32% → 51%); "committed to helping people like me" **61%** (from 56%; Republicans 52% → 63%) — [*Steady Opposition: Public Disapproval of Trump Administration Funding and Workforce Cuts Remains Strong*](https://ourpublicservice.org/know-the-facts/resource-library/reports/steady-opposition-public-disapproval-of-trump-administration-funding-and-workforce-cuts-remains-strong); [GovExec](https://www.govexec.com/workforce/2026/06/most-americans-think-government-workers-are-competent-and-should-be-nonpartisan-according-recent-surveys/414126/).

**The trend, stated plainly.** Identical question, identical series: **87% (2024) → 66% (2025) → 76% (2026)**, Republicans **~87% → 66% → 78%**. A 21-point fall and a 12-point recovery, on a principle, in 24 months, with no intervening event but the exercise of the power and the reaction to it.

**Other independent evidence**

- **AP-NORC, January 9–13, 2025 (n=1,147, ±3.9).** About one quarter favors eliminating a large number of federal jobs, and about one quarter favors eliminating entire federal agencies; Republicans likelier than Democrats or independents to support both, with no party percentages published — [AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/although-support-for-doge-is-mixed-a-majority-believe-corruption-inefficiency-and-red-tape-are-major-problems/). The corpus's "~25% favor / ~48% oppose, AP-NORC March 2025" is January fieldwork on the favor side; the opposition figure is unverified (§5).
- **UMD Program for Public Consultation, May 19–30, 2023 (n=2,625 RV, ±1.9, deliberative).** Extending the congressional stock-trading ban to **all federal employees: 40% — 42% R / 37% D / 42% I**, against 86% (87% R / 88% D) for the ban on members of Congress — [PPC](https://publicconsultation.org/united-states/stock-trading-by-members-of-congress/). The public applies elite-accountability instincts to elected and appointed power and declines to extend them to the civil service — the best-measured statement of the category's ceiling.
- **H.R. 2550, Protect America's Workforce Act — House passage, December 11, 2025: 231–195; Republicans 20–195, Democrats 211–0** ([Clerk of the House, Roll Call 332](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2025/roll332.xml)). The bill restores collective bargaining rather than merit protection, so it is adjacent — but it is the only recorded federal vote in this area, and 9% of the majority party voted yes.

**Flagged (quota sample; not nationally representative)**

- **Partnership for Public Service, March 13–16, 2025 (n=1,000, Prodege).** Concern about "loss of experience and knowledge": **64% — 44% R / 85% D / 63% I**; opposition to the workforce changes 54% overall (87% D, 57% I; 79% of Republicans in favor) — [Partnership](https://ourpublicservice.org/know-the-facts/resource-library/reports/new-partnership-for-public-service-polling-shows-majority-of-the-american-public-concerned-about-cuts-to-the-federal-workforce-and-government-services). **Correction to the corpus:** the report states this wave is *not* nationally representative, unlike the spring waves above; it should not be weighted as independent evidence.

**Nuances & caveats**

- **Nothing in the record asks the amendment.** No wave asks whether merit protections should be constitutional, or tests clause (b) — "career federal employees may not be dismissed for political reasons." The 95% and 87% figures are principles; the 40% PPC figure is the only mechanism-level reading, and it is a different mechanism.
- **The 2025 wave is the outlier, and both directions of movement are informative.** A spring 2025 reading would have recorded a 66% R floor and called the category collapsing; twelve months later, 78% R. Neither is the durable number, and 2026 remains 11 points below 2024.
- **Wording does most of the work.** Merit hiring draws 94–96% in every party; "nonpartisan civil service is important for democracy" swings 21 points; the mirror item ("fire any civil servant for any reason") draws 37% R in 2024 and 60% R in 2025 — one series asking one underlying question three ways.
- **Independents are the most volatile group**, falling furthest in 2025 (competence 32%) and recovering furthest in 2026 (51%) — which cuts against reading the 2025 dip as a purely partisan artifact.
- **The 2026 Democratic figure is not verified**, so no floor is computed from that wave; the 2024 wave, where all three party figures are published, is used instead.
- **"Schedule F" is not a testable term.** No poll uses it or "Schedule Policy/Career," so even a well-worded merit question would not measure support for the policy actually in dispute.

## 3. The gate result

- **Bipartisan floor:** **95%** (95R/96D; 94I) — the lower of the two parties' figures per §4.1 — on "civil servants should be hired and promoted based on merit rather than their political beliefs" (Partnership/Impact Research, spring 2024) → **Strong** per §4.1 — but that measures variant (a), which is not in dispute and which the contested rule claims to preserve. On the contested framing ("a nonpartisan civil service is important for a strong American democracy") the floor is **66% (Republicans, 2025)** — Strong by the letter of §4.1, having been ~87% a year earlier and 78% a year later. **On the operative remedy — a constitutional bar on politically motivated removal — the category is Unpolled**, and the nearest mechanism-level reading (PPC's 40%, 42R/37D/42I) points the other way.
- **Failure modes (§4.3):**
  - *Incumbent / regime coding* — **applies, and is the category's defining feature.** The 2026 wave supplies the second half of the proof: the Republican line moved 87 → 66 → 78 while the presidency did not change hands, and the mirror item moved 37% → 60% R across the same interval. §11's "same principle, different incumbent" trap applies directly — a floor measured in either 2025 or 2026 reads the current alignment, not the principle. The Democratic line moved in the same direction (88 → 81), the tell that this is salience rather than a durable partisan divide.
  - *Framing dependency* — **applies.** The framing that clears the bar most comfortably (merit hiring, 95% floor) is not what would be drafted, because it is not what is contested; the contested framing (removal protection) is unmeasured.
  - *Abuse-vs-remedy gap* — **applies, in an unusual form.** There is no consensus that an abuse occurred: 79% of Republicans favored the 2025 workforce changes and 87% of Democrats opposed them (Partnership, March 2025). Elsewhere in the corpus this failure mode means agreement on the abuse and a split on the remedy; here the parties disagree about the predicate.
  - *Staleness* — does not apply; evidence runs to spring 2026. *High-topline / low-floor* — does not apply (2-point gap in 2024; in 2026 Republicans at 78% ran above the 76% topline).
  - *Depth (trade-off collapse)* — **untested and at risk.** No survey names the cost — that a constitutional protection makes a poor performer harder to remove. OPM's rule rests entirely on that argument, and the 40% PPC reading suggests the public is receptive to it.
- **Non-polling tests (§5):**
  - *Existential-problem (§5.1)* — **open, and genuinely arguable.** For: an executive branch staffed by loyalty rather than merit removes an internal check on unlawful orders, and tenure protection is what lets an official refuse one — a Rule of Law concern, not a personnel-policy one. Against: the dispute covers roughly 50,000 of about two million positions on OPM's own estimate, the rule preserves merit hiring and veterans' preference by its terms, and the country ran an openly partisan spoils system for its first ninety-four years without ceasing to be a republic. Same shape as the qualified-immunity §5.1 finding: defensible either way, and heard by one party as a policy dispute.
  - *Symmetry (§5.2)* — **passes on construction, fails on presentation today.** A merit clause binds every future president identically, and the 2024 numbers (95R/96D) are what the principle looks like when nobody's side holds the power. But the mission requires pairing both sides' abuses in the same sentence, and no matched pair has been identified: the contemporary record is one-sided, and the older record (the pre-Pendleton spoils system; the 1993 travel-office dismissals) is too distant or too small to pair honestly. Until a pairing exists, this cannot be sold behind the veil.
  - *Why-an-amendment (§5.3)* — **fails as currently framed; this most constrains the recommendation.** None of the four accepted answers applies. *Courts struck the statutes*: no — the removal cases ([*Myers*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/272/52) 1926, [*Humphrey's Executor*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/295/602) 1935, [*Free Enterprise Fund*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/08-861) 2010, [*Seila Law*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/19-7) 2020, and the stay in [*Trump v. Wilcox*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/24A966), May 22, 2025) all concern **officers**, not career employees. *Free Enterprise Fund* footnote 10 says so expressly: "Nothing in our opinion, therefore, should be read to cast doubt on the use of what is colloquially known as the civil service system within independent agencies" ([opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/08-861.ZO.html)); footnote 9 distinguishes the many government employees who are not "Officers of the United States." *Congress structurally cannot self-bind*: no — Congress bound the executive in 1883 and 1978, and the Schedule Policy/Career rule rests on a statutory exception Congress itself wrote into the Civil Service Reform Act and can repeal. *The text is silent and that silence is what defiant actors cite*: no — the actors here cite a statute and a regulation. This is the strongest **statutory** case in the candidate list and the weakest constitutional one. The one argument that could change it — that Article II doctrine is trending toward a reading that will eventually reach career employees, so a statute would be struck — is a prediction, not a holding, and the Court has twice said the opposite in terms.

The gap between the first row and the last is the whole problem: a 95% floor exists for a sentence nobody is arguing about, while the sentence that would be drafted has never been put to anyone. And unlike qualified immunity — where the popular framing (accountability) and the drafted mechanism (liability) at least point the same direction — the one available mechanism-level reading here (40%) points away from the principle. On §4.3's framing-dependency test, the framing that clears the bar is a different proposal wearing the name.

## 5. What would resolve it, and the recommendation

**Evidence that would move it in.** A national poll from an independent house (Pew, Marquette, AP-NORC, Economist/YouGov) with 2027-or-later fieldwork, asking the operative clause in forward-looking, both-parties wording naming no incumbent — e.g.: *"Would you favor or oppose a constitutional amendment providing that career federal employees — those not appointed by the president — may be hired, promoted, and dismissed only on the basis of merit and conduct, and never because of their political beliefs or party, binding every future president of either party?"* Two mandatory follow-ups: (i) **costs named** — "even if this makes it harder for a president to remove career officials who are performing poorly or resisting lawful policy"; and (ii) a **symmetry probe** asking the same of a president of the respondent's own party and of the other party. **Gate: ≥60% in both parties on the base question and the costs-named follow-up, with less than a 10-point swing across the symmetry probe.** Because the category is regime-coded, one wave will not do: the number must hold across an administration that changes hands, or the §11 trap repeats. A second, independent route: a majority of *both* caucuses for a statutory merit-protection bill — which the December 2025 roll call (R 20–195) is far short of.

**Evidence that would move it out.** A repeat collapse in the next wave — Republicans back under 60% on the nonpartisan-civil-service item, or the mirror "fire any civil servant for any reason" item above 55% in either party — would show the 2026 recovery was noise and the principle does not survive contact with power, failing the regime-coding test conclusively. More likely: a costs-named poll landing near the PPC 40% band would fail the depth test the way the balanced budget did. And §5.3 can fail the category without any polling — if the why-an-amendment test is applied as written, the *Free Enterprise Fund* footnote and the 1883/1978 statutes are a complete answer, and it moves to the statutory list.

**Recommendation: hold as a candidate — gate: a forward-looking both-parties poll of the operative protection, plus an answer to why a statute cannot do this.** Two reasons, in order of weight. First, §5.3: the Court has twice said its removal jurisprudence does not reach the civil service system, Congress created these protections by statute twice and retains the power to restore them, and the current dispute runs on a statutory exception Congress wrote — so on the method's four accepted answers this is a statute, not an amendment (§3). Second, §4.3: the only framing with a Strong floor is the merit-entry principle the contested rule itself claims to honor, the operative remedy has never been polled, and the one mechanism-level number in the record is 40% (§2, §4). Against rejection: the 87 → 66 → 78 trace is a salience swing both parties rode rather than a partisan divide, the 2024 baseline (95R/96D) shows what the principle measures when it is not in play, and the §5.1 argument — that tenure protection is what lets an official refuse an unlawful order — is the strongest of any candidate held on non-polling grounds. Holding is also cheap: the poll above resolves the regime-coding and depth questions in one instrument. If the founder wants a constitutional answer to the removal-doctrine trend, the natural home is a companion clause in the **FCC/independent-agency** candidate, where the officer-level cases actually bite, rather than a standalone civil-service amendment. Founder decides.

**Searched for and not found (2026-08-18):** any national poll, from any pollster, asking whether merit or tenure protections for career federal employees should be constitutional; any poll using the terms "Schedule F" or "Schedule Policy/Career" with a party breakdown; any 2024–2026 Pew, Gallup, Marquette, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, or Economist/YouGov item on civil-service removal protections with party splits; any state ballot measure on merit-system protections; any published party breakdown for the Partnership's 2026 nonpartisan-civil-service item beyond the Republican figure.

## Review Additions (2026-08-18)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion.*

- **The Constitution is not silent on political firing.** [*Elrod v. Burns*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/427/347), 427 U.S. 347 (1976), held that dismissing non-policymaking public employees solely for their party affiliation violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments; [*Branti v. Finkel*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/445/507), 445 U.S. 507 (1980), set the test — party affiliation may be required only where the hiring authority "can demonstrate that party affiliation is an appropriate requirement for the effective performance of the public office involved." Bearing on the gate: this strengthens the §5.3 conclusion that a statute (plus existing First Amendment doctrine) already reaches patronage dismissal of career employees, and it sharpens what an amendment would add — protection for the *policy-influencing* tier that *Elrod*/*Branti* leave exposed and that Schedule Policy/Career targets. No change to the verdict or recommendation.
- **Cosponsor counts and party composition for the civil-service bills (congress.gov, retrieved 2026-08-18).** [H.R. 492](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/492/cosponsors), *Saving the Civil Service Act* — sponsor Rep. Connolly (D-VA-11), first sponsorship later assumed by Rep. Walkinshaw; **108 cosponsors, 104 Democratic and 4 Republican** (Reps. Fitzpatrick and Bacon as original cosponsors, later Reps. Lawler and Van Orden). [S. 134](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/134/cosponsors), the Senate companion — sponsor Sen. Kaine (D-VA); **24 cosponsors, 22 Democratic and 2 independent (Sens. Sanders and King), no Republicans**. [S. 2837](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2837/cosponsors), *Protect America's Workforce Act* — sponsor Sen. Warner (D-VA); **48 cosponsors, 44 Democratic, 2 independent, and 2 Republican (Sens. Murkowski and Collins)**. All three remain in committee with no floor action. Bearing on the gate: §5 names "a majority of *both* caucuses for a statutory merit-protection bill" as a second route that would move this category in, and these are the bills that route would run through. Four House Republicans and two Senate Republicans across three bills does not meet §4.2's third tier (cross-party legislative votes), and it is consistent with — not a correction to — the memo's finding that the parties disagree about the predicate rather than only the remedy. **No change to the verdict or recommendation**, but the §5 gate is now evidenced rather than asserted: the legislative route is as far from opening as the polling route.

## Site summary

Put merit protection for career federal employees in the Constitution. The principle polls at 94–96% in both parties when nobody's side holds the power — but the "nonpartisan civil service" question fell 87% to 66% among Republicans in one year and recovered to 78% the next, the remedy itself has never been polled, and the Supreme Court has said its removal cases do not reach the civil service, which is what Congress already protects by statute.
