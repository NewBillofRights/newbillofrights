# Striking the 13th Amendment's Exception Clause — Research Memo

Research supporting **Target Category 9 (Striking the Thirteenth Amendment's Exception Clause)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md); companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md) and to the candidate memo that carried the category through the gate, [THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_CANDIDATE.md](candidates/THIRTEENTH_EXCEPTION_CANDIDATE.md). **Accepted as a target on 2026-08-18.** The bipartisan floor is **66% (Republicans)**, against 85% D and 69% I, on a state-measure question — "ensures the state constitution doesn't legalize slavery and involuntary servitude as punishments for crime" ([YouGov, Oct. 23–25, 2024](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/50840-poll-2024-state-ballot-measures-workers-rights-abortion-drugs)) — a **Strong** verdict under [SELECTION_METHOD.md](SELECTION_METHOD.md) §4.1, and the narrowest party gap in the corpus at 19 points, with Republican *opposition* at 11%. Four Republican-voting states have passed single-issue versions at the ballot box: **Utah 80.48% (2020)**, **Tennessee 79.53% (2022)**, **Nebraska 68.23% (2020)**, **Nevada 60.60% (2024)**. The question this memo researches is no longer whether the category clears the bar — it does — but what the amendment should say, given that two defeats (California 2024, Louisiana 2022) and one near-miss turned into a two-thirds majority (Colorado 2016 → 2018) show that on this category, **wording is not cosmetic; it is the whole result.**

**The headline findings:**

1. **This is the narrowest edit in the pipeline, and the purest "why an amendment" case in the corpus.** The proposal deletes fourteen words from the Constitution. It creates no institution, grants no new power, and binds no officeholder. The exception is *constitutional text*, so no statute can reach it — every one of the nine states that removed its own version had to amend its own constitution. That is answer (4) in §5.3 (the text is the problem) in its purest form.
2. **The state precedent is a red-state precedent.** Utah, Tennessee, Nebraska, and Alabama all removed their exception clauses through legislatures with Republican majorities that had to vote yes to refer the measure at all, and then at the ballot box by margins of 68–80%. Nevada's four required legislative roll-calls were **unanimous and fully bipartisan — 42–0, 42–0, 21–0, 21–0, with no legislator of either party voting no in either session**.
3. **The word "slavery" is worth roughly fourteen points.** On the same night in November 2024, Nevada's measure — whose ballot question used the word "slavery" — passed 60.60%, while California's — whose official title and summary used only "involuntary servitude" and never once said "slavery" — lost 46.66%. Colorado ran the same substance twice: 49.68% in 2016, 66.21% in 2018. This is the sharpest natural experiment in the corpus on any category, and it dictates a drafting choice.
4. **The savings clause does not cost votes — it is the highest-performing version.** Tennessee ratified text that removes the exception *and* expressly preserves inmate work ("Nothing in this section shall prohibit an inmate from working when the inmate has been duly convicted of a crime") and scored **79.53%, the highest single-issue result in a Republican-voting state, with every county voting yes.** Louisiana ran the opposite experiment — text that preserved the carve-out under the phrase "lawful administration of criminal justice" — and lost 39.15%, disowned by its own sponsor.
5. **What the deletion removes is compulsion, not work — and there is now one squarely on-point ruling proving it.** In *Mortis v. Polis*, decided February 2026, a Denver District Court held that Colorado's corrections department violated the amended Article II, §26 by compelling work under threat of solitary confinement and disciplinary charges. The order bars segregation and disciplinary charges for work refusal. **It did not shut down a single work program** — Colorado Correctional Industries continued operating sixteen businesses across eight facilities. That is the operational answer to the "will this end prison jobs?" objection, and it is now evidence rather than prediction.
6. **The blast radius is smaller than it looks, and the Supreme Court has already drawn the lines.** [*Butler v. Perry* (1916)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/240/328/) holds that "involuntary servitude" was never meant "to interdict enforcement of those duties which individuals owe to the state, such as services in the army, militia, on the jury, etc."; the [*Selective Draft Law Cases* (1918)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/245/366/) uphold conscription against a Thirteenth Amendment challenge. Deleting the *punishment* exception does not disturb either holding. The genuine open questions are narrower: work requirements, disciplinary consequences, and pay.
7. **The federal record is the one place the bipartisan pattern does not hold — and that gap is the strategic problem.** The Abolition Amendment drew 11 Republican House cosponsors in the 117th Congress, 5 in the 118th, and **zero Republican Senate cosponsors in either**. It has never received a committee or floor vote. No 119th Congress reintroduction could be located (verified 2026-08-18). Public opinion is at 66% among Republicans; Republican officeholders are at zero in the Senate. Sequencing follows from that divergence: states first.
8. **Every peer democracy already prohibits the status and regulates the work separately — and does it better than our exception does.** ILO Convention 29 (1930) excludes prison labour from "forced labour" **only** where it is "carried out under the supervision and control of a public authority" and the person "is not hired to or placed at the disposal of private individuals, companies or associations." The Thirteenth Amendment's exception has no such limit. The international norm is not "no prison work"; it is "prison work, but never as private property."

## Amendment Framework

### Core provisions

The amendment has three candidate layers, in descending order of consensus and ascending order of drafting risk:

1. **The strike (the core).** Remove "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted" from the Thirteenth Amendment's first section, leaving an unqualified prohibition: neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction. This is the whole of the federal Abolition Amendment's approach ([Merkley draft text, 117th Congress](https://www.merkley.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/imo/media/doc/21.06.17%20Abolition%20Amendment.pdf)) and the whole of what the 66% floor was measured on.
2. **The work-programs savings clause.** An express statement that the article does not prohibit a person confined under lawful sentence from working voluntarily, or in a work program authorized by law with compensation and conditions provided by law. Tennessee's ratified text is the tested model, and it carries the highest single-issue number in the set (79.53%). This layer is where "principles, not machinery" does its work: the *principle* — no one may be compelled — goes in the Constitution; the *machinery* — wages, hours, program design, sentence credits — goes to legislatures by law.
3. **The enforcement clause.** "Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation" — carried forward from the Thirteenth Amendment's existing §2, whose reach [*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.* (1968)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/409/) construed broadly as the power "to determine what are the badges and incidents of slavery, and the authority to translate that determination into effective legislation." Preserving this clause verbatim is a *conservative* drafting choice: it changes nothing, and omitting it would be read as a silent narrowing of an existing congressional power.

What the amendment deliberately does **not** do: it does not set a wage, does not ban work requirements as such, does not create a cause of action beyond what §2 and ordinary constitutional litigation already supply, and does not disturb the duties-to-the-state line drawn in *Butler v. Perry*.

### Challenges

- **The prison-labor operational question is real, and the amendment answers only part of it.** Three distinct things travel under "prison labor": (a) *compulsion* — being made to work on pain of punishment; (b) *pay* — wages ranging from nothing to cents per hour; (c) *work requirements* — programs conditioning privileges, classification, or sentence credits on participation. The strike reaches (a) directly and squarely. It does not set wages, and it does not by itself resolve whether a *non-punitive* incentive (earned sentence credit for voluntary work) is compulsion. *Mortis* is the map: the court barred segregation and disciplinary charges for refusal and left the programs running. Expect the operative fights after ratification to be about the boundary between an incentive and a penalty — and expect them in legislatures and trial courts, not in the amendment's text.
- **Wording sensitivity is documented, not theorized, and it cuts in a specific direction.** [California Prop 6 (2024)](https://ballotpedia.org/California_Proposition_6,_Remove_Involuntary_Servitude_as_Punishment_for_Crime_Amendment_(2024)) lost 46.66–53.34 with a ballot title reading "Eliminates constitutional provision allowing involuntary servitude for incarcerated persons" — the word "slavery" appears nowhere in the title or summary. [Nevada Question 4](https://ballotpedia.org/Nevada_Question_4,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2024)) passed 60.60% the same night asking whether to "remove language authorizing the use of slavery and involuntary servitude as a criminal punishment." PPIC's pre-election readings show California's measure was already losing and falling — 46% yes / 50% no in September 2024, then **41% yes / 56% no** in the October 7–15 wave ([PPIC, October 2024](https://www.ppic.org/publication/ppic-statewide-survey-californians-and-their-government-october-2024/)). PPIC's survey director observed that when voters are uncertain "the default is to vote 'no'" ([KQED, Nov. 2024](https://www.kqed.org/news/12013392/californians-voted-against-outlawing-slavery-why-is-prop-6-failing)). The 17% "not sure" share in the YouGov crosstab — 23% among Republicans — is the same phenomenon visible in a survey rather than at a ballot box. **Drafting consequence: the operative sentence must contain the word "slavery," and the savings clause must be visible enough that no voter has to guess whether prison jobs survive.**
- **The federal ask has never been polled.** Every national number in the file is a *state-measure* proxy. No independent national poll with a party breakdown has asked about amending the **federal** Thirteenth Amendment. §11's trap — attaching a broad number to a specific mechanism — applies with real force, and the mechanism-specific poll is this category's first commissioned-research priority.
- **Depth is untested.** No pollster has run a costs-named version (corrections budgets, wages for incarcerated workers, ended work programs). California is the only measure that faced a sustained public campaign, and even there PPIC's question stem records that **"No opponents submitted"** ballot arguments — so the loss is a wording result, not a campaign result, and the contested-campaign condition remains untested everywhere.
- **"Duly convicted" interacts with several adjacent categories, and the interactions must be checked rather than assumed.** Community service, work-release, pre-trial detention, civil commitment, the military draft, and jury duty all involve compelled or semi-compelled labor. The good news is that the Supreme Court has already placed most of them outside "involuntary servitude" on grounds that have nothing to do with the punishment exception — see the blast-radius analysis under **Constitutional Abstraction Candidates**. The category that *does* move is pre-trial detainees and others not "duly convicted," who are protected by the current text's own terms and remain protected after the strike.

### Strategic read

This belongs in a New Bill of Rights for a reason that is different in kind from the other eight targets. The other eight *add* a rule the Constitution lacks — a term limit, a disclosure duty, a districting calendar. This one *removes* an authorization the Constitution still grants. The Thirteenth Amendment is the document's own statement that slavery shall not exist, and it contains, in its own sentence, the exception under which slavery may exist. A project that proposes to finish the Bill of Rights' unfinished business has an unusually direct answer to "why this?": the founding charter still authorizes the thing it was written to abolish.

The full-spectrum case is unusually clean because the clause **binds no party, branch, or incumbent, and creates no weapon for either side to surrender.** Every other category in the corpus requires the veil-of-ignorance sell — asking each side to give up something at the moment the other side does. This one requires nothing of the kind: there is no partisan advantage in the exception clause, no officeholder whose power it protects, and no "your side holds it now" flip available. It is symmetric by construction rather than by drafting. That is why the floor holds at 66% where it collapses in the executive-accountability categories: there is no enforcement machinery to poll, because the remedy *is* the deletion.

The sequencing is dictated by the one place the evidence is bad. Public opinion is at 66% among Republicans and 79.5% at a Tennessee ballot box; Republican Senate cosponsorship of the federal amendment is zero. That divergence between voters and officeholders is the finding. The path runs through the states — fifteen still carry an exception clause, and each removal is a legislative roll-call and a ballot result that makes the federal ask harder to refuse — and through a mechanism-specific national poll that puts the *federal* question, with the savings clause, to both parties. The messaging discipline follows from the ballot record and is narrow: say **slavery**, and say in the same breath that **work programs continue**. Tennessee proved those two sentences can travel together to 79.5% in a Republican-voting state. California proved that omitting the first one costs the measure.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

**The text, and the fourteen words at issue.** The Thirteenth Amendment, §1: *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, **except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted**, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction."* §2: *"Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."* The amendment passed the Senate in April 1864 and the House on January 31, 1865 by 119–56, was submitted to the states February 1, 1865, and was ratified **December 6, 1865** ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment)).

**The exception's origin is the Northwest Ordinance, and the Supreme Court says so in the same breath as the duties-to-the-state rule.** The Ordinance of 1787 for the government of the Northwest Territory declared: *"There shall be neither slavery nor involuntary servitude in the said territory otherwise than in punishment of crimes whereof the party shall have been duly convicted."* *Butler v. Perry* records that "utilizing the language of the Ordinance of 1787, the Thirteenth Amendment declares that neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist," and notes that the states formed from the Northwest Territory "prohibited involuntary servitude substantially in the language of the 1787 ordinance" ([240 U.S. 328, 331–32](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/240/328/)). This matters for the drafting record: the exception was **inherited boilerplate**, carried forward from a 1787 territorial ordinance into the 1865 amendment, not a provision the Reconstruction Congress designed for the purpose it later served. That is a fact about legislative history, and it is worth stating factually rather than rhetorically.

**Convict leasing — stated factually, with sources.** After 1865, Southern legislatures enacted Black Codes criminalizing conduct such as vagrancy and loitering, and states leased the resulting prisoners to private railways, mines, and plantations. The Equal Justice Initiative's account: "After the Civil War, slavery persisted in the form of convict leasing, a system in which Southern states leased prisoners to private railways, mines, and large plantations," under a Thirteenth Amendment that "prohibited slavery and involuntary servitude, but explicitly exempted those convicted of crime"; prisoners "faced inhumane, dangerous, and often deadly work conditions," and the practice was ended by "industrialization, economic shifts, and political pressure … by World War II" ([EJI](https://eji.org/news/history-racial-injustice-convict-leasing/)). Douglas Blackmon's Pulitzer-winning *Slavery by Another Name* is the standard modern history ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_by_Another_Name)). The relevant point for drafting is structural rather than moral: **the exception's historical failure mode was the transfer of convict labor to private parties for profit** — precisely the transfer that ILO Convention 29 forbids and that the Thirteenth Amendment's exception does not.

**Modern doctrine — what "involuntary servitude" means now.**

- [***United States v. Kozminski* (1988)**](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/487/931/) — the controlling definition. For purposes of criminal prosecution under 18 U.S.C. §§241 and 1584, "involuntary servitude" means "a condition of servitude in which the victim is forced to work for the defendant by the use or threat of physical restraint or physical injury or by the use or threat of coercion through law or the legal process." Purely psychological or economic coercion does not suffice. **Two features of the opinion bear directly on this amendment.** First, the Court reasoned *from the exception clause itself* to the meaning of the prohibition: "The fact that the Amendment excludes from its prohibition involuntary servitude imposed 'as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted' indicates that the Amendment's drafters thought that involuntary servitude generally includes situations in which the victim is compelled to work by law." That is the strongest available authority that **removing the exception removes an authorization for legally compelled labor** — the Court has already said that legal compulsion is what the exception was carved out of. Second, *Kozminski*'s "coercion through law or the legal process" is exactly the mechanism a disciplinary write-up for work refusal employs, which is why *Mortis* came out as it did under identical state language.
- [***Butler v. Perry* (1916)**](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/240/328/) — the duties-to-the-state line, and the single most important blast-radius authority in this file. "Involuntary servitude" covers "those forms of compulsory labor akin to African slavery which, in practical operation, would tend to produce like undesirable results." It "introduced no novel doctrine with respect of services always treated as exceptional, and certainly was not intended to interdict enforcement of those duties which individuals owe to the state, **such as services in the army, militia, on the jury, etc.** The great purpose in view was liberty under the protection of effective government, not the destruction of the latter by depriving it of essential powers." The Court upheld a Florida statute requiring able-bodied men to work six ten-hour days a year on public roads. Note carefully what this holding does *not* depend on: it rests on the meaning of "involuntary servitude," **not** on the punishment exception. Deleting the exception therefore leaves it untouched.
- [***Selective Draft Law Cases* (1918)**](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/245/366/) — conscription upheld against a Thirteenth Amendment challenge; compelled military service is "a duty of citizenship," and the Amendment's protection against involuntary servitude does not prevent the federal government from implementing a draft. Again independent of the exception clause.
- [***Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.* (1968)**](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/409/) — the §2 enforcement power, 7–2, Stewart, J. Congress has power under the Thirteenth Amendment "to determine what are the badges and incidents of slavery, and the authority to translate that determination into effective legislation," reaching private conduct; the decision overruled *Hodges v. United States* and secured the Civil Rights Act of 1866's modern application. Relevant here because §2 is the clause the draft carries forward unchanged: the enforcement power that already exists is broad, and the amendment should not appear to narrow it.
- **The Trafficking Victims Protection Act of 2000** is Congress's response to *Kozminski*'s narrowness — after the Court confined §1584 to physical and legal coercion, Congress legislated forced labor offenses reaching coercion by "serious harm," abuse of legal process, and schemes intended to cause a victim to believe they would suffer serious harm (18 U.S.C. §1589). Two lessons for this file: statutory law already regulates coerced labor far beyond the constitutional floor, and **§2 has proven adequate to that job** — an argument that the amendment needs no new enforcement machinery of its own.

**The current legal status of prison labor — the three-part answer.**

1. **Constitutional.** The exception clause is the authorization. Where a state has removed its own version, compelled work is unlawful under state law (Colorado, per *Mortis*); where it has not, the federal exception remains available.
2. **Statutory wage law.** Prisoners are generally **not "employees" under the Fair Labor Standards Act**, so the federal minimum wage does not reach prison work. Courts have reasoned that the prisoner–prison relationship is custodial rather than economic, and that the FLSA was enacted to maintain a standard of living for workers rather than for wards of the state; the circuits have produced "a patchwork of conflicting standards" in the absence of a Supreme Court holding or a congressional directive ([Penn State Law Review](https://www.pennstatelawreview.org/the-forum/why-incarcerated-workers-should-be-protected-by-the-fair-labor-standards-act/); [Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2007/may/15/prisoner-not-covered-by-fair-labor-standards-act/)). Congress has repeatedly declined to change this — see the [Fair Wages for Incarcerated Workers Act of 2023, S. 516](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/516). **This is the load-bearing point for the "will this raise wages?" question: it will not, by itself. The FLSA exclusion is statutory and survives the strike untouched.**
3. **Pay scales and compulsion in practice.** The ACLU / University of Chicago Global Human Rights Clinic report *Captive Labor* (June 15, 2022) found that **seven state prison systems — Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Mississippi, South Carolina, and Texas — pay nothing for the vast majority of prison work**, while other states "pay on average between 15 and 52 cents per hour for non-industry jobs"; that roughly 65% of incarcerated people work, about 800,000 people, producing "at least $2 billion in goods and $9 billion worth of prison maintenance services annually"; and that **"more than three quarters of incarcerated workers surveyed (76 percent) report facing punishment — such as solitary confinement, denial of sentence reductions, or loss of family visitation — if they decline or are unable to work"** ([ACLU, *Captive Labor*](https://www.aclu.org/publications/captive-labor-exploitation-incarcerated-workers)). *(Advocacy source — flagged as such per §4.2 tier 4; the wage figures are consistent with the independent range reported in the FLSA literature above, and the 76% compulsion figure is the specific number a costs-named poll should test.)*

**The red-state precedents, called out explicitly.** Four Republican-voting states passed single-issue removals — **Utah at 80.48%, Tennessee at 79.53%, Nebraska at 68.23%, Nevada at 60.60%** — and a fifth, **Alabama at 76.49%**, ratified a recompiled constitution that removed the exception among other racist language (bundled corroboration only; it cannot be attributed to this clause alone). Three of those four single-issue results sit *above* the 66% Republican floor measured nationally. Every one of them was legislatively referred, meaning Republican legislative majorities voted to put it on the ballot. **Nevada's roll-calls were unanimous in all four required votes** — Assembly 42–0 (26 D, 16 R) April 13, 2021 and 42–0 (26 D, 16 R) February 16, 2023; Senate 21–0 (12 D, 9 R) May 17, 2021 and 21–0 (12 D, 9 R) February 23, 2023 — **not one legislator of either party voted no in either session** ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Nevada_Question_4,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2024))). This is the cleanest §4.2 tier-3 evidence in the corpus: a fully bipartisan, unanimous legislative record on the exact mechanism.

**Tennessee — the model text, and the proof that a savings clause costs nothing.** The ballot question asked whether to delete Article I, §33 and substitute: *"Section 33. Slavery and involuntary servitude are forever prohibited. Nothing in this section shall prohibit an inmate from working when the inmate has been duly convicted of a crime."* The prior text had read: *"That slavery and involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime, whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, are forever prohibited in this state"* ([Ballotpedia, ballot text](https://ballotpedia.org/Tennessee_Constitutional_Amendment_3,_Remove_Slavery_as_Punishment_for_Crime_from_Constitution_Amendment_(2022))). Result: **79.53%, every county yes** — the highest single-issue number in a Republican-voting state in the set. The savings sentence did not depress the number; it accompanies the best number. Note also what the Tennessee sentence does *not* say: it does not require pay, and it does not define what makes work voluntary. That silence is the gap our draft should close, and the reason our §2 improves on the model rather than copying it.

**Colorado — the sixteen-point swing, and the only post-amendment litigation record.** Amendment T lost 49.68–50.32 in 2016; Amendment A passed 66.21% in 2018 on *identical substance*. The difference was presentation. Then, in the first squarely on-point ruling anywhere, **Denver District Judge Sarah Wallace held in February 2026 that the Colorado Department of Corrections violates the amended Article II, §26 by compelling work under threat of punishment.** The court entered "a declaratory judgment that Defendants are in violation of the prohibition against involuntary servitude under Section 26, Article II, of the Colorado Constitution," found that "the machinery of coercion is not isolated but is a pervasive and actively operationalized feature of CDOC's labor management," and held that incarcerated people "have a constitutional right to be free from slavery and involuntary servitude without exception." The order requires CDOC to stop threatening or using segregation for work refusal and to cease "double-charging" for refusal; defendants had **28 days to appeal**. **Crucially, it did not end prison work: Colorado Correctional Industries continued operating sixteen businesses across eight facilities.** ([CBS Colorado, Feb. 17, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/denver-judge-colorado-department-corrections-violates-state-constitution-prison-labor/); case page: [ACLU of Colorado, *Mortis v. Polis*](https://www.aclu-co.org/cases/mortis-v-polis-amicus/), which records the class action as filed June 16, 2022, No. 2022CV30421, Hon. Sarah Wallace.) *(Dating note: the ACLU case page gives February 13, 2026 for the ruling and CBS reports it as issued the preceding Friday in a February 17 article; the sources agree on substance and differ by a few days on the order's date. No appellate filing by CDOC could be located as of 2026-08-18.)*

**The defeats, and what each one teaches.**

- **California 2024 — the wording defeat.** The official title read "Eliminates constitutional provision allowing involuntary servitude for incarcerated persons. Legislative constitutional amendment," and the summary "Amends the California Constitution to remove current provision that allows jails and prisons to impose involuntary servitude to punish crime (i.e., forcing incarcerated persons to work)." Neither contains the word "slavery." It lost 46.66–53.34 in the state with the largest Democratic presidential margin in the set, on the same night Nevada's "slavery"-worded measure passed at 60.60%. Its legislative history also runs opposite to Nevada's: ACA 8 cleared the Senate **33–3 (D 32–0; R 1–3, 4 not voting)** and the Assembly 68–0 (D 61–0; R 7–0, 10 not voting) on June 27, 2024 — the only recorded Republican no votes in the entire set, on the only measure that omitted "slavery."
- **Louisiana 2022 — the boundary case.** Text prohibiting involuntary servitude "except as it applies to the otherwise lawful administration of criminal justice" was read as preserving or broadening the carve-out; **its own legislative sponsor urged a no vote**, and it lost 39.15–60.85 ([Louisiana Illuminator, Nov. 8, 2022](https://lailluminator.com/2022/11/08/louisiana-voters-reject-ban-on-slavery-involuntary-servitude-author-also-opposed-it/)). Lesson for drafting: text that looks like a repeal but preserves the exception fails badly **and poisons the ground for a later attempt.** Our savings clause must be unmistakably a *work-programs* clause, not a *criminal-justice-administration* clause — a distinction Louisiana proves voters can detect.
- **Colorado 2016 — the near-miss.** Same substance, sixteen points lower, two years earlier. The sharpest demonstration available that presentation carries this category.

**What the state record does and does not establish.** It establishes that the mechanism passes in both parties' strongholds, that legislatures of both parties will refer it, and that a savings clause is compatible with the highest numbers. It does **not** establish that support survives organized, funded opposition: PPIC's question stem records that supporters of Prop 6 submitted ballot arguments while **"No opponents submitted."** No passing measure in this set has faced a funded opposition campaign. That is the one condition the state record leaves untested, and it belongs in the held-back reasoning rather than in the confidence.

## International Models

Peer democracies converge on a structure the United States does not have: **the status is prohibited absolutely, and the work is regulated separately by statute, subject to conditions.** No peer system authorizes slavery as a punishment. Several permit — some require — prison work, but always as a regulated activity with limits the American exception clause does not impose.

### ILO Convention 29 (1930) — the prison-labour exception, and its two limits

The [Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (No. 29)](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/instree/n0ilo29.htm) (39 U.N.T.S. 55, entered into force May 1, 1932) defines forced or compulsory labour in Article 2(1) as "all work or service which is exacted from any person under the menace of any penalty and for which the said person has not offered himself voluntarily." Article 2(2) excludes five categories, of which (c) is the prison-labour carve-out:

> "(c) Any work or service exacted from any person as a consequence of a conviction in a court of law, **provided that the said work or service is carried out under the supervision and control of a public authority and that the said person is not hired to or placed at the disposal of private individuals, companies or associations**."

The other exclusions are instructive for our blast-radius analysis, because they track *Butler v. Perry* almost exactly: (a) compulsory military service of a purely military character; (b) "normal civic obligations of the citizens of a fully self-governing country"; (d) emergencies — war, fire, flood, famine, epidemic; (e) minor communal services performed in the direct interest of the community.

**The comparative point is sharp and belongs in the campaign.** The international standard's prison carve-out is *narrower* than the Thirteenth Amendment's in two specific ways: it requires public supervision and control, and it flatly forbids hiring convicts out to private parties. **The historical abuse the American exception enabled — convict leasing to private railways, mines, and plantations — is precisely what ILO 29 prohibits and what our Constitution's exception does not.**

### European Convention on Human Rights, Article 4 — and its prison-work exclusion

[Article 4](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/convention_ENG) reads in full: "1. No one shall be held in slavery or servitude. 2. No one shall be required to perform forced or compulsory labour. 3. For the purpose of this Article the term 'forced or compulsory labour' shall not include: (a) any work required to be done in the ordinary course of detention imposed according to the provisions of Article 5 of this Convention or during conditional release from such detention; (b) any service of a military character or, in case of conscientious objectors in countries where they are recognised, service exacted instead of compulsory military service; (c) any service exacted in case of an emergency or calamity threatening the life or well-being of the community; (d) any work or service which forms part of normal civic obligations."

Note the structure: **4(1) is absolute and non-derogable — "no one shall be held in slavery or servitude," with no exception whatever, including for convicted persons.** The carve-out at 4(3)(a) applies only to "forced or compulsory labour" under 4(2), and only to work "in the ordinary course of detention." Europe thus does exactly what our draft proposes: **it prohibits the status without exception and treats the work as a separate, regulated question.**

### Germany — Basic Law Article 12

The most precise constitutional formulation found anywhere, and the closest available model for a savings clause ([Basic Law, official English translation](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/englisch_gg/englisch_gg.html)):

> **Art. 12(2):** "No person may be required to perform work of a particular kind except within the framework of a traditional duty of community service that applies generally and equally to all."
>
> **Art. 12(3):** "**Forced labour may be imposed only on persons deprived of their liberty by the judgment of a court.**"

Germany's drafters reached the same judgment ours must: the constitutional text names *who* may be subject to compelled work and leaves the conditions to statute. Two differences from the American exception are worth the drafting record. First, Germany's trigger is "deprived of their liberty by the judgment of a court" — an incarceration test, not a conviction-plus-punishment authorization, and one that cannot be satisfied by an administrative act. Second, Germany does not use the word "slavery" at all in this clause; Article 1's human-dignity guarantee does that work. Our draft cannot borrow that structure, because the word "slavery" is precisely the word the ballot evidence says must appear.

### Canada, the United Kingdom, France, Australia — prison work in practice

- **United Kingdom — work is *required* of convicted prisoners, and voluntary for the unconvicted.** [Prison Rules 1999, rule 31](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1999/728/article/31/made): "A convicted prisoner shall be required to do useful work for not more than 10 hours a day"; medical officers may excuse prisoners on health grounds; no prisoner may be set to work not authorised by the Secretary of State, nor "to work for the private benefit of any person" without such authorisation; and "an unconvicted prisoner shall be permitted, if he wishes, to work as if he were a convicted prisoner." Prisoners "may be paid for their work at rates approved by the Secretary of State." **The UK is the clearest demonstration that a jurisdiction bound by ECHR Article 4 — with its absolute ban on servitude — still lawfully requires convicted prisoners to work.** Whatever else the strike does, this is the comparative evidence that abolishing the *status* exception does not, by international practice, abolish work obligations.
- **Canada — statutory pay, frozen since 1981.** Federal inmate pay levels were set by a parliamentary committee in 1981 by reference to the then-minimum wage less an 85% deduction for room and board; the top of the scale is **$6.90 per day**, with most prisoners receiving less, and the rates **have not changed in more than four decades** ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/prison-inmates-take-federal-government-to-court-over-pay-cuts-1.2732382); [Law360 Canada](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1757442/work-pay-in-canadian-prisons-part-one-david-dorson)). Prisoners rated as not complying with their correctional plan — including by refusing to work — receive the lowest allowance. **Lesson: putting a pay rate in a fixed instrument freezes it.** Canada's rate has lost most of its real value to four decades of inflation. This is a direct argument for delegating compensation to "law" rather than fixing anything in constitutional text.
- **France and Australia.** Both operate paid prison-work regimes under ordinary legislation and administrative regulation rather than constitutional command, with rates set by decree or corrective-services policy and adjusted over time. The comparative pattern across all four is uniform and is the lesson worth abstracting: **no peer democracy sets prison wages or work rules in its constitution.** They are statutory everywhere.

### The evasion file — where "voluntary" prison work is coerced

The rule this amendment writes can be evaded in one specific way, and the evidence that it is evaded comes from both American and European sources.

- **Disciplinary consequences convert "voluntary" into compelled.** The ACLU/GHRC survey found **76% of incarcerated workers reported facing punishment — solitary confinement, denial of sentence reductions, or loss of family visitation — if they decline or are unable to work** ([*Captive Labor*, 2022](https://www.aclu.org/publications/captive-labor-exploitation-incarcerated-workers)) *(advocacy source, flagged)*. This is the exact mechanism *Mortis* found unlawful under Colorado's amended text, and the exact mechanism *Kozminski* describes as "coercion through law or the legal process." **A savings clause that says "voluntary" without saying what defeats voluntariness invites this evasion.**
- **Europe shows the softer form: unpaid work counted as "ordinary."** The European Court's own [Guide on Article 4](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_4_eng) records that "Article 4 does not contain any provision concerning the remuneration of prisoners for their work," and that "the mere fact that a prisoner was not paid for the work he did, did not in itself prevent work of this kind from being regarded as 'work required to be done in the ordinary course of detention'" (*Floroiu v. Romania* (dec.), 2013, §33). In *Floroiu* the Court accepted work compensated by **sentence reduction rather than money** as not "entirely unpaid" (§§35–37). In *Stummer v. Austria* [GC], 2011, obligatory prison work without old-age pension affiliation fell within 4(3)(a) and outside "forced labour" — the violation the Grand Chamber found was of the non-discrimination and property provisions, not Article 4. **Lesson: a prohibition on servitude, standing alone, has not compelled European states to pay prisoners.** Anyone claiming this amendment guarantees wages is claiming more than the text or the comparative record supports.
- **The private-hire evasion is the historical one.** ILO 29 forbids placing convicts "at the disposal of private individuals, companies or associations" precisely because that is how compulsory prison labour becomes commercially valuable — the convict-leasing pattern. The American exception contains no such limit, and the draft below does not add one, because it has not been polled and would be a new substantive rule rather than a deletion. It is logged in **Held back** with the evidence that would bring it in.

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Prohibit the status absolutely; regulate the work separately.** ECHR 4(1) ("no one shall be held in slavery or servitude" — no exception, none for convicted persons) sits above 4(3)(a), which excludes ordinary detention work from the definition of *forced labour*. The two-tier structure is the international norm and the structure our draft adopts: an unqualified prohibition, plus a clause clarifying that authorized work programs are not what is prohibited.
2. **Distinguish paid or voluntary work from servitude in the text itself, not by implication.** Tennessee did it in one sentence and scored 79.53%; Louisiana blurred the distinction and lost at 39.15%. Germany's Art. 12(3) does it by naming the class of persons rather than authorizing a status. The distinction has to appear on the face of the instrument, because voters and courts both read the face.
3. **Put pay and conditions in statute, never in the constitution.** Canada's constitutionally-uninvolved but legislatively frozen $6.90-a-day scale, unchanged since 1981, is the cautionary case: any fixed number decays. Every peer democracy sets prison compensation by statute or regulation. This is "principles, not machinery" with a comparative proof.
4. **A prohibition on servitude does not by itself produce wages.** *Stummer* and *Floroiu* establish that in Europe, unpaid or sentence-credit-compensated prison work survives an absolute servitude ban. In the US, the FLSA exclusion is statutory and survives the strike. **Claiming the amendment raises prison wages would be a claim the evidence does not support**, and honesty about this is also the answer to the corrections-cost objection.
5. **The dangerous carve-out is not "work" — it is "hired to private parties."** ILO 29's 2(2)(c) permits prison work only under public supervision and control and never for private hire. The exception clause's worst historical use — convict leasing — is exactly what that proviso blocks. Any future strengthening of this amendment should go here first.
6. **Duties owed to the state are a separate category everywhere, and every instrument says so expressly.** ILO 29 excludes military service, normal civic obligations, emergencies, and minor communal services; ECHR 4(3) excludes military service, emergencies, and "normal civic obligations"; *Butler v. Perry* excludes "services in the army, militia, on the jury, etc." The convergence across a 1930 treaty, a 1950 convention, and a 1916 American opinion is strong evidence that the strike does not reach jury duty, the draft, or community obligations — and it is the citation set to have ready when someone claims it does.
7. **Compulsion hides in consequences, not in commands.** No modern system says "you must work or be whipped." It says refusal costs you visitation, good-time credit, or classification. Both the American survey evidence (76%) and the European case law (sentence-reduction-as-compensation) show the pressure migrating into the consequence structure. A rule about compulsion that does not speak to consequences will be evaded by consequences.
8. **An incarceration trigger is cleaner than a punishment authorization.** Germany's "persons deprived of their liberty by the judgment of a court" identifies *who* may be required to work without ever declaring that forced labour is a permissible *punishment*. It is the difference between authorizing a status and defining a population — and it is why our §2 should be framed as "a person confined under lawful sentence" rather than as any residue of "as a punishment for crime."

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. Both below clear it — the strike itself at a **66% Republican floor** (YouGov Oct. 2024, 73% overall / 85% D / 69% I, with 11% Republican opposition) corroborated by single-issue passage at **80.48% (Utah)**, **79.53% (Tennessee)**, **68.23% (Nebraska)**, and **60.60% (Nevada)**; and the work-programs savings clause at **79.53%**, the Tennessee result, which is the only ballot measure in the set whose ratified text contains such a clause and which produced the highest single-issue number in a Republican-voting state. Three candidates are held back at the end.*

1. **The strike — an unqualified prohibition.**
   Proposed formulation: *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction."*
   - This is the Thirteenth Amendment's own sentence with the exception removed — no new language, no new concept, no new grant of power. It is what the Abolition Amendment proposes and what the 66% floor was measured on. Retaining the original phrasing verbatim is deliberate: every word has 160 years of construction behind it, and *Kozminski*'s definition of "involuntary servitude" attaches to these exact words.
   - ⚠️ **Rigidity trap — banning all prison work.** The nightmare reading is that an unqualified prohibition forbids prisons to operate work programs at all. Two things answer it: the Colorado experience (the ruling barred compulsion and left sixteen prison industries running) and the comparative record (the UK *requires* convicted prisoners to work while bound by an absolute servitude ban). But neither is in the text, which is why Principle 2 is not optional garnish — **it is the clause that keeps this principle from being read as a rigidity trap**, and it is also the clause associated with the best ballot number.
   - ⚠️ **Wording trap — "slavery" must survive into the operative sentence.** The single most robust empirical finding in this file is that measures using the word "slavery" pass and the one that omitted it failed, on the same night, roughly fourteen points apart. Any drafting elegance that replaces "slavery nor involuntary servitude" with a tidier synonym would discard the finding. Keep both nouns.
   - ⚠️ **Blast-radius pressure test (§6.3): does this change ordinary expectations of how government functions?** Run the list. *Jury duty, militia service, and military conscription* — expressly outside "involuntary servitude" per *Butler v. Perry* ("duties which individuals owe to the state, such as services in the army, militia, on the jury, etc.") and the *Selective Draft Law Cases*; both rest on the meaning of "involuntary servitude," not on the punishment exception, so deleting the exception does not touch them. *Community service and work-release* — imposed as an alternative to or condition of a sentence and performed by people under lawful sentence; reached by the savings clause, and Oregon's ratified text expressly authorizes court-ordered programs in lieu of incarceration. *Civil commitment* — not a criminal punishment and not premised on the exception; any labor question there is governed by the general prohibition today and would be governed by it identically tomorrow. *Pre-trial detention* — people not "duly convicted" are already outside the exception's terms and therefore already protected; the strike changes nothing for them, and this is the answer to "does this reach jails?" *Prison work programs* — preserved on the face of §2. **The residual real-world change is compulsion backed by punishment — which is the point of the amendment, not an unintended radius.**
   - ⚠️ **"Duly convicted" and what disappears with it.** Deleting the phrase deletes a *condition on an authorization*, not a protection. Nobody currently gains rights from being "duly convicted"; the phrase limits who may be subjected to the exception. Removing the whole exception removes the need for the limiter. Drafting note: do not preserve "duly convicted" anywhere in the operative sentence, or courts will hunt for the residue of an authorization that was meant to be gone. It may appear in the savings clause only as a description of who is lawfully confined — and even there, "confined under lawful sentence" is cleaner.

2. **The work-programs savings clause.**
   Proposed formulation: *"Nothing in this article shall prohibit a person confined under lawful sentence from working voluntarily, or in a work program authorized by law with compensation and conditions provided by law."*
   - Tennessee's ratified sentence is the tested ancestor ("Nothing in this section shall prohibit an inmate from working when the inmate has been duly convicted of a crime") and carries the 79.53% result with every county voting yes — direct evidence that an express savings clause does not cost votes and plausibly buys them, by removing the uncertainty that PPIC's director identified as the driver of "no" votes. Our version improves on Tennessee's in three ways: it says **voluntarily** (Tennessee's does not, leaving the compulsion question to inference); it routes **compensation and conditions** to law, which is where every peer democracy puts them and where they can be adjusted; and it uses **"confined under lawful sentence"** rather than "duly convicted," so no fragment of the old authorization survives in the text.
   - ⚠️ **Evasion trap — "voluntary" defined by consequences.** If the clause says only "voluntarily," a corrections department can maintain that work is voluntary while a refusal costs visitation or good-time credit — the pattern 76% of surveyed workers reported and the pattern *Mortis* found unlawful. The draft's answer is the second half of §1 in the package below, which states the operative test in the Constitution (compulsion by punishment) while leaving every incentive question to Congress and the states. See the alternatives discussion in the draft section: this is the most consequential open choice in the package and the one the founder should rule on.
   - ⚠️ **Mercy-of-the-legislature trap.** "As provided by law" hands the conditions to legislatures — including legislatures that today pay nothing. That is a real cost, honestly stated: the clause does not guarantee a wage. It is nonetheless the right allocation, because a constitutional wage floor is both unpolled and, on Canada's evidence, self-decaying. The principle is constitutional; the number is statutory.
   - ⚠️ **Louisiana trap — never write a criminal-justice-administration carve-out.** A savings clause phrased around "the lawful administration of criminal justice" reads as preserving the exception and lost 39.15% with its own sponsor opposing it. The savings clause must be visibly about *work programs*, not about the administration of punishment generally.

**Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar:**

- **A constitutional wage floor for prison work** (*"…who shall be compensated at not less than the minimum wage established by law"* or similar). Never polled in any form; would convert a deletion into a fiscal mandate on all fifty states, which is exactly the material a costs-named depth test would attack; and the FLSA exclusion it would override is a live legislative question Congress has repeatedly declined to resolve ([S. 516, 118th Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/516)). Canada's frozen 1981 scale is the further argument against fixing any number in durable text. **Path back:** independent national polling with party crosstabs on a compensation requirement clearing a 60% Republican floor, *and* a costs-named version that holds — plus at least one state adopting a constitutional or statutory pay standard after a contested campaign.
- **A ban on work requirements as such** (*"no person shall be required to work as a condition of confinement, classification, or release"*). This is the maximal reading of the amendment and is not what any ballot measure asked. The UK's rule 31 shows a jurisdiction with an absolute servitude ban that still *requires* convicted prisoners to work; *Floroiu* shows Europe accepting sentence-credit compensation. Constitutionalizing a ban on requirements would go beyond both the polled proposition and the international norm, and would make the rigidity trap real rather than rhetorical. **Path back:** a resolved answer, in litigation under the nine amended state constitutions, on where incentive ends and compulsion begins — then polling on that specific line.
- **A private-hire prohibition** (the ILO 29 2(2)(c) proviso: no person confined under sentence to be "hired to or placed at the disposal of private individuals, companies or associations"). Intellectually the strongest addition available — it targets the exception's actual historical abuse, convict leasing, and it is the international standard. But it is a **new substantive rule**, not a deletion; it would reach existing private-partnership prison industries in many states; and it has never been polled in the United States in any form. It also breaks the category's greatest asset, which is that the proposal creates nothing and merely removes. **Path back:** national polling with party crosstabs above the 60% Republican floor, plus a state adopting the private-hire limit in its own constitution and surviving the operational aftermath.

**The package skeleton.** Minimal viable amendment = **Principle 1 (the strike) + Principle 2 (the work-programs savings clause) + the existing enforcement clause carried forward**. Three short sections, no new institution, no new power, judicially self-executing, resting on a 66% Republican floor and a 79.53% red-state ballot result for the savings-clause version specifically. Section order matters for the campaign as much as the law: the prohibition comes first and contains the word "slavery"; the savings clause comes immediately after, so that no reader reaches the end of the article wondering whether prison jobs survive — the uncertainty that PPIC identified as the mechanism of California's defeat. The held-back layers (wage floor, work-requirement ban, private-hire prohibition) return only with polling proof, and each would be a separate ask.

**Inherently statutory** (belongs in companion legislation, not the amendment): prison wage rates, pay scales, and deduction rules; whether incarcerated workers are "employees" under the FLSA and the amendment of 29 U.S.C. to say so; sentence-credit and good-time structures tied to program participation; the design, staffing, and safety regulation of prison industries; classification and housing consequences of program participation; the boundary between a permissible incentive and an impermissible penalty for work refusal, in the first instance; occupational safety and workers'-compensation coverage for incarcerated workers; vocational training and reentry program requirements; contracting rules for private participation in prison industries; restitution and victim-compensation deductions from earnings; and transition funding for corrections departments in states that currently rely on unpaid labor for facility maintenance.

## Draft text proposal (for the founder)

> **⚠️ THIS SECTION IS A PROPOSAL.** Draft text enters [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) only when the founder adopts it. The formulations below are written in the register of the eight existing articles — short sections, "as Congress shall by law provide," express savings clauses, prospective effect where needed — and each carries the explanation and the trap it avoids, which become the page's `<Note>` annotations.

**Section 1.** *Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction.*

> **Why this wording.** This is the Thirteenth Amendment's own sentence with the punishment exception removed, and nothing else changed. Keeping the original words preserves 160 years of judicial construction, including the settled meaning of "involuntary servitude" as compulsion by physical restraint, injury, or legal process. It avoids the **wording trap**: measures that used the word "slavery" passed in eight states while the one measure that omitted it failed the same night, so "slavery" stays in the operative sentence. It also avoids the **"duly convicted" residue trap** — no fragment of the old authorization survives anywhere in the operative text, so no court can hunt for one.

**Section 2.** *Nothing in this article shall prohibit a person confined under lawful sentence from working voluntarily, or in a work program authorized by law, with compensation and conditions as Congress or the States shall by law provide. No person shall be compelled to labor by the infliction or threat of punishment.*

> **Why this wording.** The first sentence preserves prison work programs on the face of the article, which is the drafting choice associated with the highest single-issue ballot result in a Republican-voting state (Tennessee, 79.53%, every county yes), and it sends wages, hours, and program design to legislatures, where every peer democracy puts them and where they can be adjusted over time. The second sentence states the operative test the amendment actually enforces — the difference between a work program and servitude is punishment for refusal — which avoids the **evasion trap** in which work is called voluntary while refusal costs solitary confinement or family visitation. It avoids the **rigidity trap** by saying plainly that programs continue, and the **Louisiana trap** by being visibly about work programs rather than about the administration of criminal justice generally.

**Section 3.** *Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.*

> **Why this wording.** This carries forward the Thirteenth Amendment's existing Section 2 verbatim rather than writing a new enforcement clause. The existing power has been construed broadly, reaching private conduct and permitting Congress to define and legislate against the incidents of slavery, and it is the authority under which federal anti-trafficking and forced-labor statutes already operate. Restating it unchanged is the conservative choice: any new phrasing would invite an argument that the enforcement power had been narrowed, and this amendment is meant to remove an exception, not to disturb a power.

**Alternatives the founder should weigh, with the trade-off in each:**

- **Section 2, second sentence — keep, cut, or soften.** *Keep* (as drafted) states the compulsion test in the Constitution and forecloses the consequence-based evasion that 76% of surveyed incarcerated workers report and that the Colorado court found unlawful. *Cut it* and the article is a pure deletion plus Tennessee's savings sentence — maximally minimal, matching exactly what was polled, and leaving the incentive/penalty line entirely to litigation and statute. *Soften* to "No person shall be compelled to labor by the infliction or threat of punishment, except as Congress shall by law provide" would gut it. **Recommendation: keep, and flag it as the one clause in the package that goes beyond the literal polled proposition** — it is a definition of the prohibition rather than a new rule, but a reviewer could fairly call it an addition, and it should be named in the mechanism-specific poll as a split-sample arm.
- **Section 2, first sentence — "as Congress or the States shall by law provide" vs. "as provided by law."** The former is explicit that state legislatures set terms for state prisoners, which is where the overwhelming majority of incarcerated workers are and which answers a federalism objection before it is raised. The latter is shorter. **Recommendation: the explicit version**, matching the campaign-finance article's "Congress and the States" register.
- **A prospective effective date.** Unlike redistricting or term limits, there is no incumbent to grandfather and no map to freeze — the amendment binds no officeholder and confers no advantage, so the veil-of-ignorance problem the other articles solve with delayed effect does not arise here. What does arise is operational: corrections departments in states that currently rely on unpaid, compelled labor for facility maintenance would need time to convert programs to a voluntary basis. A clause such as *"This article shall take effect two years after its ratification"* would answer that and would cost nothing in principle. **Recommendation: offer it as an optional Section 4 and let the founder decide** — it is the single most likely concession to be asked for by corrections-state legislators during ratification, and conceding it in advance is cheaper than conceding it under pressure. Against it: a delayed effective date on a slavery prohibition is a bad sentence to have to defend in public, and the Colorado experience suggests the transition is manageable without one.
- **Should the article replace the Thirteenth Amendment's Section 1 or stand as a new article?** As drafted, Section 1 restates the prohibition in full, which operates as a replacement by implication. An express repealer — "The exception in the first section of the thirteenth article of amendment is hereby stricken" — is more precise but reads as legislative housekeeping rather than as a constitutional statement, and the ballot evidence favors text a voter can read aloud. **Recommendation: the restatement, as drafted**, matching the Abolition Amendment's approach and Tennessee's.

*Founder decisions 2026-08-18: compulsion sentence kept (to be polled as a split-sample arm); Section 4 two-year effective date adopted; restatement form and "Congress or the States" wording adopted.*

## Implementation: the law today and the day after (2026-08-18)

*Appended for the amendment page's "If it passes" section. Applies the general law of ratification in [RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md](RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md) to Article 9 as adopted. Every URL below is either already in this memo's Sources list or was fetched and confirmed on 2026-08-18.*

### Federal law today

**The authorization is constitutional, and only constitutional text reaches it.** The Thirteenth Amendment's §1 exception — "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted" — is the sole federal authorization for compelled labor by convicted persons ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment)). *Kozminski* construes the prohibition's own words to confirm this reading: the exception "indicates that the Amendment's drafters thought that involuntary servitude generally includes situations in which the victim is compelled to work by law" ([487 U.S. 931](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/487/931/)). Section 1 of the draft removes the authorization and nothing else.

**The federal work requirement is a regulation, and its enforcement mechanism is a disciplinary code.** The Bureau of Prisons provides that "each sentenced inmate who is physically and mentally able is to be assigned to an institutional, industrial, or commissary work program" ([28 C.F.R. §545.23](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/545.23)). The consequence for refusal is supplied separately: the BOP's prohibited-acts table makes "refusing to work or to accept a program assignment" Code 306, a moderate-severity offense whose available sanctions include forfeiture of up to 25% or 30 days of earned good time, disciplinary segregation of up to three months, loss of privileges, housing change, and job loss ([28 C.F.R. §541.3, Table 1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/541.3)). The BOP states that "sentenced inmates are required to work if they are medically able" and that they "earn 12¢ to 40¢ per hour for these work assignments" ([Bureau of Prisons, Work Programs](https://www.bop.gov/inmates/custody_and_care/work_programs.jsp)). **This regulation-plus-disciplinary-code pairing is the precise structure the draft's Section 2 second sentence reaches: the assignment rule is not compulsion by punishment, but the Code 306 sanction attached to refusal is.**

**Federal Prison Industries is statutory and its authorizing statutes do not compel anyone.** UNICOR is chartered as "a government corporation of the District of Columbia" ([18 U.S.C. §4121](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/4121)); its board is directed to "provide employment for the greatest number of those inmates … who are eligible to work as is reasonably possible" and to diversify so as to "reduce to a minimum competition with private industry or free labor" ([18 U.S.C. §4122](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/4122)); and federal agencies "shall purchase at not to exceed current market prices" its products where they meet requirements and are available, subject to a best-value determination ([18 U.S.C. §4124](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/4124)). None of these provisions requires any individual to work. They are program-authorizing statutes of exactly the kind Section 2 preserves.

**Federal law already conditions private-sector prison work on voluntariness and prevailing wages.** 18 U.S.C. §1761(a) makes it an offense to transport in interstate commerce goods "manufactured, produced, or mined, wholly or in part by convicts or prisoners." The Prison Industry Enhancement exemption at §1761(c) lifts that ban only for certified projects in which inmates "have participated in such employment voluntarily and have agreed in advance to the specific deductions made from gross wages," and receive "wages at a rate which is not less than that paid for work of a similar nature in the locality in which the work was performed" ([18 U.S.C. §1761](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1761); program overview: [Bureau of Justice Assistance, PIECP](https://bja.ojp.gov/program/piecp/overview)). This is the closest existing federal analogue to the draft's design — voluntary participation plus compensation set by law — and it already governs the private-hire channel that ILO 29 targets.

**Forced-labor statutes stand untouched and already exceed the constitutional floor.** 18 U.S.C. §1589 punishes obtaining labor by force, "serious harm or threats of serious harm," or "the abuse or threatened abuse of law or legal process," defining the latter as use of a legal process "in any manner or for any purpose for which the law was not designed, in order to exert pressure on another person" ([18 U.S.C. §1589](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1589)). Congress enacted this reach under the Thirteenth Amendment's existing enforcement clause, which the draft's Section 3 carries forward verbatim ([*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/409/)).

**The wage exclusion is statutory and survives the strike.** The FLSA defines "employee" as "any individual employed by an employer" and "employ" as "to suffer or permit to work" ([29 U.S.C. §203](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/203)); it says nothing about prisoners, and courts have read the custodial relationship out of the Act, producing a patchwork across the circuits ([Penn State Law Review](https://www.pennstatelawreview.org/the-forum/why-incarcerated-workers-should-be-protected-by-the-fair-labor-standards-act/); [Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2007/may/15/prisoner-not-covered-by-fair-labor-standards-act/)). Congress has repeatedly declined to change it ([S. 516, 118th Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/516)). **Nothing in the draft alters this.** Anyone claiming the amendment raises prison wages is claiming more than the text supports.

**What the amendment leaves alone by settled doctrine.** *Butler v. Perry* places "services in the army, militia, on the jury, etc." outside "involuntary servitude" as duties owed to the state ([240 U.S. 328](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/240/328/)); the *Selective Draft Law Cases* uphold conscription on the same ground ([245 U.S. 366](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/245/366/)). Both rest on the meaning of "involuntary servitude" and not on the punishment exception, so deleting the exception does not disturb either.

### State law today

**Nine states have removed their exception clause; fifteen retain one; twenty-six never had constitutional language on the subject.** The nine, with the certified result and year: Colorado 66.21% (2018), Utah 80.48% (2020), Nebraska 68.23% (2020), Alabama 76.49% (2022, bundled recompilation), Tennessee 79.53% (2022), Vermont 88.71% (2022), Oregon 55.59% (2022), Nevada 60.60% (2024), and Rhode Island, whose constitution has carried an unqualified prohibition since before the modern wave. The fifteen retaining an exception are Arkansas, California, Georgia, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, and Wisconsin ([Wikipedia, *Penal exception clause*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_exception_clause), cross-checked against the certified results in **State Models** above and against contemporaneous reporting that seven states permit both slavery and involuntary servitude as punishment and eight permit involuntary servitude alone — [ABC News, Nov. 2024](https://abcnews.go.com/Politics/slavery-involuntary-servitude-ballot-states/story?id=115270058)). **Ratification of Article 9 would make every one of the fifteen exception clauses unenforceable at once**, on the ordinary supremacy rule; none would need to be repealed to lose force, and each would remain in the state's printed constitution until repealed or judicially declared void.

**State work-requirement statutes are the operative layer, and they are separate from the constitutional clauses.** Texas requires the department to "require each inmate … to work in an agricultural, industrial, or other work program to the extent that the inmate … is physically and mentally capable of working," permits waivers for security or rehabilitative programming, and authorizes an incentive pay scale with deductions of up to 80% for restitution and dependent care ([Tex. Gov't Code §497.099](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_497.099)). Statutes of this shape exist in states that removed their exception clauses and in states that retain them; a requirement to assign work is not by itself compulsion by punishment, and the amendment's line runs through the enforcement mechanism rather than the assignment.

**Pay levels.** The ACLU/GHRC *Captive Labor* survey found seven state systems — Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Mississippi, South Carolina, and Texas — paying nothing for the vast majority of prison work, with other states averaging 15 to 52 cents per hour for non-industry jobs ([ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/publications/captive-labor-exploitation-incarcerated-workers)) *(advocacy source, flagged per §4.2 tier 4)*. Colorado's incarcerated workers earned between $0.33 and $1.61 per hour as of 2022, and state minimum-wage law does not reach them ([Colorado Newsline, Feb. 18, 2026](https://coloradonewsline.com/briefs/denver-judge-says-colorado-prisons-cannot-punish-incarcerated-people-for-refusing-to-work/), citing the same survey).

**The Colorado litigation is the only test of an amended clause in operation.** In *Mortis v. Polis*, Denver District Judge Sarah B. Wallace held in February 2026 that CDOC's practices violate the amended Article II, §26, finding that "the machinery of coercion is not isolated but is a pervasive and actively operationalized feature of CDOC's labor management." The order bars the department from threatening or imposing isolation for more than two days and from charging people under the prison disciplinary code for refusing to work ([Colorado Newsline, Feb. 18, 2026](https://coloradonewsline.com/briefs/denver-judge-says-colorado-prisons-cannot-punish-incarcerated-people-for-refusing-to-work/); [CBS Colorado, Feb. 17, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/denver-judge-colorado-department-corrections-violates-state-constitution-prison-labor/); case page: [ACLU of Colorado](https://www.aclu-co.org/cases/mortis-v-polis-amicus/)). **Work programs continued: Colorado Correctional Industries kept operating sixteen businesses across eight facilities.** No appellate filing could be located as of 2026-08-18. The ruling is a state trial-court decision and binds no other jurisdiction; its value here is as the only operational evidence of what an amended clause does and does not shut down.

### The day after

**What falls.** Sections 1 and 2 state flat rules and need no enabling legislation; they bind on the date the thirty-eighth state ratifies ([*Dillon v. Gloss*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/256/368)). From that day, any regime that compels labor "by the infliction or threat of punishment" is unlawful, federal and state alike. The clearest federal casualty is the pairing of the work-assignment rule with Code 306 sanctions: disciplinary segregation, good-time forfeiture, and job or privilege loss imposed **for refusing to work** could no longer be applied ([28 C.F.R. §§545.23](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/545.23), [541.3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/541.3)). The fifteen remaining state exception clauses lose force, as do state disciplinary rules that punish work refusal — the exact mechanism the Colorado court reached under identical state language. Nothing is erased from the books automatically; the regulations and clauses stay in print until repealed or declared void ([*Leser v. Garnett*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/258/130); [*United States v. Chambers*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/291/217)).

**What stands.** Section 2 preserves, on the face of the article, work by a person confined under lawful sentence that is voluntary or in "a work program authorized by law." UNICOR and its authorizing statutes stand ([18 U.S.C. §§4121](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/4121), [4122](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/4122), [4124](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/4124)), as do state correctional-industry programs, work-release, and court-ordered community service performed by people under sentence. The PIECP framework stands and already requires voluntariness and prevailing wages ([18 U.S.C. §1761(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1761)). The FLSA exclusion stands: **the amendment sets no wage.** Jury duty, militia service, and conscription stand on *Butler v. Perry* and the *Selective Draft Law Cases*. Pre-trial detainees and people not "duly convicted" are unaffected, because the exception never reached them.

**What Section 2 delegates.** The clause routes "compensation and conditions" to "Congress or the States … by law." That piece does not exist until a legislature acts: no wage, hour, benefit, or program-design rule follows from ratification itself. The explicit naming of the States is a federalism choice, since the overwhelming majority of incarcerated workers are in state custody and state legislatures set their terms.

**What Section 3 lets Congress do.** The enforcement clause is the Thirteenth Amendment's existing §2, carried forward unchanged. Its reach is broad — Congress may "determine what are the badges and incidents of slavery" and legislate accordingly ([*Jones*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/409/)) — bounded by the congruence-and-proportionality limit ([*City of Boerne v. Flores*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-2074.ZO.html)). Plausible enforcement legislation: a statutory definition of what consequences defeat voluntariness in a work program; conforming amendments to 28 C.F.R. §§541.3 and 545.23 removing work refusal from the disciplinary code; and, separately and not required by the text, a wage statute of the kind already introduced ([S. 516, 118th Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/516)).

**Conforming and trigger legislation.** States may enact conforming statutes now that take effect on certification — the ordinary contingent-legislation mechanism described in [RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md](RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md) §4. No state has yet enacted a trigger statute keyed to this amendment; the nearest real example is negative rather than positive — Colorado amended its constitution in 2018 and its legislature enacted no conforming statute, so the question was resolved in court eight years later. That sequence is the argument for conforming legislation rather than an example of it.

**What the courts will decide.** Three questions, candidly. **(1) Where the line falls between an incentive and a penalty.** Section 2 forbids compulsion "by the infliction or threat of punishment." Losing eligibility for a sentence credit one never earned is not obviously the infliction of punishment; forfeiting good time already earned, as Code 306 permits, more plainly is. The Colorado court drew the line at disciplinary charges and segregation and left the programs running; a federal court construing this text would start there but is not bound by it. **(2) What "a work program authorized by law" requires** — whether a departmental regulation suffices or a statute is needed, and whether unpaid facility-maintenance assignments qualify as a "work program." **(3) Whether any consequence short of formal discipline counts** — housing classification, visitation, or parole-eligibility effects. European practice under an absolute servitude ban has accepted sentence-credit compensation and unpaid ordinary detention work ([ECHR Guide on Article 4](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_4_eng)), which is evidence that a prohibition of this kind does not by itself resolve these questions. The draft leaves them open deliberately: the principle is constitutional, and the machinery is statutory.
