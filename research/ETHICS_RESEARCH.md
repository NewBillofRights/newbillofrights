# Ethics Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

This document supports **Target Category 3 (Ethics)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md) — covering the President, the Supreme Court / federal judiciary, and Congress, with special focus on banning conflicts of interest via direct involvement in stocks, crypto, and prediction markets while in office. It is a companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md), which established that a congressional stock-trading ban is **the single most bipartisan reform ever polled** (86% overall, 87R/88D, UMD 2023), that extending it to the President, VP, and Supreme Court polls even higher (87%, 87R/90D), and that SCOTUS ethics-code enforcement hits 90% (88R/93D, Marquette 2024). Corruption is seen as a "big problem" by 92% of Americans (90R/93D). This document maps the legal terrain that polling sits on: what current law actually does (and conspicuously doesn't), what Congress has tried, what states and peer democracies have built, and what an amendment could abstract from all of it.

**The headline findings:**

1. **The three offices the public most wants covered are precisely the ones current law exempts.** The federal criminal conflict-of-interest statute, [18 U.S.C. § 208](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/208), binds every executive-branch officer and employee — *except* the President and Vice President, whom Congress expressly excluded, and it never applied to Congress or federal judges at all ([CRS LSB10250](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/LSB/PDF/LSB10250/LSB10250.2.pdf)).
2. **The US President is nearly unique among peer democracies in being legally exempt from conflict-of-interest law**, and **the US Supreme Court is the only apex institution — foreign or domestic — that polices its own ethics with no enforcement mechanism.** All 50 states have judicial conduct commissions covering their top courts; Canada's Chief Justice can be the subject of an ordinary misconduct complaint ([Canadian Judicial Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Judicial_Council)); the US Supreme Court's November 2023 Code of Conduct binds no one ([CRS LSB11078](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11078)).
3. **Disclosure-only regimes demonstrably fail.** The STOCK Act's $200 late-filing fine has been violated by scores of members every Congress with zero prosecutions ([Campaign Legal Center](https://campaignlegal.org/update/congressional-stock-trading-and-stock-act); [Business Insider's "Conflicted Congress"](https://news.yahoo.com/64-members-congress-violated-law-125839548.html?fr=sycsrp_catchall)); [Unusual Whales](https://unusualwhales.com/congress-trading-report-2024) found both parties' members beating the S&P 500 in 2024 (D +31%, R +26%, vs. 24.9%).
4. **The legislative fix is perpetually 90% done and never finished.** Bipartisan bills with cross-spectrum sponsors (Hawley + Merkley + Ossoff; Spanberger + Roy + AOC + Cloud) have advanced repeatedly — a Senate committee marked one up for the first time in history, and the House passed a (weakened, rider-laden) restriction in July 2026 — but no ban has become law in the 14 years since the STOCK Act. This is the classic case for constitutionalization: Congress structurally cannot police itself.
5. **Constitutional ethics enforcement already exists and works — just not federally.** Rhode Island (1986), Oklahoma (1990), and Florida (1976) put ethics commissions or disclosure mandates directly into their constitutions by popular vote, with margins (2-to-1 and better) that match the national polling on this issue.
6. **The breadth of the ban is the design question that decides everything.** Public support targets *elected and appointed power*: extending trading bans to all federal employees craters support to 40%, and divestment mandates were seen as "going too far" by 69% in 2016 polling (see POLLING_RESEARCH.md). The amendment must aim at the President, VP, Congress, and the Court — and stop there.

## Amendment Framework — What an Ethics Amendment Might Cover

### Core provisions

An ethics amendment consistent with the polling and the legal gap analysis would have up to five moving parts:

1. **A trading ban**: no buying, selling, or holding of individual securities, digital assets, or event/prediction-market contracts by the President, Vice President, Members of Congress, and Justices/federal judges while in office (diversified funds and Treasuries exempt; qualified blind trusts as the compliance path).
2. **Ending the chief-executive exemption**: the President and Vice President become subject to conflict-of-interest law like every other executive officer, reversing the § 208 carve-out.
3. **An enforceable judicial ethics floor**: the Supreme Court and federal judiciary bound by a code of conduct with a real complaint-investigation-sanction process, resolving the *Alito question* ("no authority") in the Constitution itself.
4. **A disclosure floor**: constitutional status for financial disclosure by covered officials, so no future Congress or Court can quietly gut it.
5. **An enforcement locus**: some body — existing (Judicial Conference, DOJ, congressional ethics offices) or new (an independent commission on the Rhode Island model) — with constitutional standing to investigate and enforce.

### Challenges

- **The who-enforces problem** is the hardest design question in the whole package (developed fully in the trap notes below). Every enforcement design either leaves the fox guarding the henhouse or creates a body with power over the President and the Court that one side will call a lawfare machine.
- **Separation of powers**: an amendment *solves* the constitutional-authority debate (that's its purpose), but the drafting must not accidentally create a fourth branch with vague jurisdiction.
- **The Speech or Debate problem in miniature**: Rhode Island's experience — its supreme court held in 2009 that the state speech-in-debate clause shielded legislators from the Ethics Commission, requiring a second amendment in [2016 (Question 2, passed overwhelmingly)](https://ballotpedia.org/Rhode_Island_Ethics_Commission_Amendment,_Question_2_(2016)) to close the loophole — is a direct warning: an ethics amendment must expressly override internal-immunity doctrines or courts will find the gap.
- **Grandfathering and transition**: the July 2026 House bill's core weakness (ban on *buying* only; existing holdings — including a sitting President's — untouched) shows how transition rules become the loophole ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/23/house-stock-trading-ban-trump-exemption-vote/)). The Senate HONEST Act's phased divestment-by-2027 approach is the better template ([HSGAC](https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/media/dems/peters-merkley-hawley-and-ossoff-announce-historic-bipartisan-agreement-to-advance-legislation-to-ban-member-stock-trading/)).
- **Politicization risk**: the SCOTUS-ethics conversation was precipitated by the [ProPublica reporting on Justice Thomas](https://www.propublica.org/article/clarence-thomas-gift-disclosures-harlan-crow) and is coded, on the right, as an attack on one justice. The amendment framing must be prospective, symmetric, and name-neutral: it binds the next Democratic-appointed Court and the next Democratic President identically.

### Strategic read

Ethics is the **strongest pillar in the entire platform** on the numbers: it is the only category where every sub-component polls above 70% with both parties, and the only one where the R number sometimes *exceeds* the D number. The Hawley–Merkley–Ossoff sponsorship line is the full-spectrum test passing *inside the Senate itself*. Three strategic implications:

- **Lead with Congress, extend upward, never downward.** The 86%-to-40% cliff between "ban Congress from trading" and "ban all federal employees" defines the amendment's outer boundary. The public's theory is anti-self-dealing-by-the-powerful, not anti-government-worker.
- **The failure of the statutory route is the argument for the amendment.** Fourteen years of 85%+ polling, dozens of bipartisan bills, one House passage sabotaged by a rider — the case that "Congress will never bind itself by statute" writes itself. The 27th Amendment (congressional pay, ratified 1992) is the on-point precedent: the last amendment actually ratified was exactly this species of anti-self-dealing rule.
- **Guard the SCOTUS component against partisan coding.** It polls at 90% now, but its origin story (Thomas/Crow) gives opponents a "this is about one justice" attack. Pair it rhetorically with the presidential exemption (which currently codes as a Trump-protection issue for the left) so the package visibly gores both oxen.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

### The holes in current law

**18 U.S.C. § 208 — the exemption at the top.** The criminal conflict-of-interest statute requires executive-branch officials to recuse from any particular matter in which they have a financial interest — on pain of prison. But when Congress codified the definitions in 1989, it [expressly excluded the President and Vice President](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43365.html), and the statute has never covered Members of Congress or federal judges ([CRS LSB10250](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/LSB/PDF/LSB10250/LSB10250.2.pdf); [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/208)). The traditional rationale — the President can't recuse from being President — justifies at most an exemption from *recusal*, not from *divestment or anti-trading rules*, a distinction the [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/policy-solutions/strengthening-presidential-ethics-law) *(advocacy)* presses in its presidential-ethics proposals. Every modern President before 2017 handled the gap by voluntary blind trust or divestment; the last decade proved voluntary norms are not law ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/08/31/politics/trump-conflict-of-interest); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/conflict-interest-rules-apply-differently-president)).

**The STOCK Act — disclosure without teeth.** The 2012 STOCK Act affirmed that insider-trading law applies to Congress and required periodic transaction reports within 30–45 days. In practice: the late-filing penalty is **$200**, waivable by the ethics committees; fines are not systematically disclosed and payment is hard to verify; and **no one has ever been prosecuted under the Act** ([Campaign Legal Center](https://campaignlegal.org/update/congressional-stock-trading-and-stock-act) *(advocacy)*; [Government Accountability Project](https://whistleblower.org/blog/stock-act-and-insider-trading-in-congress/) *(advocacy)*). Business Insider's "Conflicted Congress" investigation found [dozens of members (64+, later 78) in violation](https://news.yahoo.com/64-members-congress-violated-law-125839548.html?fr=sycsrp_catchall); CLC complaints alone cover [$14.3M–$52.1M in undisclosed trades](https://campaignlegal.org/cases-actions/we-need-stronger-oversight-congressional-stock-trades); [Unusual Whales](https://unusualwhales.com/congress-trading-report-2024) *(market-data firm; its congressional reports drove much of the public anger)* documents members of both parties beating the market year after year, often trading in sectors their committees oversee, with 2025 setting a record for STOCK Act violations ([2025 report](https://unusualwhales.com/congress-trading-report-2025)). The [Brennan Center's explainer](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/congressional-stock-trading-explained) *(advocacy)* is the best single summary of why disclosure-only failed.

**Ethics as internal chamber rules — self-policing by design.** Congressional ethics enforcement lives in House and Senate rules, enforced by committees of members judging their colleagues. The results are what you'd predict: the Senate Ethics Committee found evidence of a violation in only **3%** of its investigations, versus **43%** for the independent Office of Congressional Ethics ([CLC](https://campaignlegal.org/update/congressional-ethics-enforcement-which-chamber-closer-meeting-bar) *(advocacy)*). The OCE itself — created in 2008 precisely because self-policing failed — has been repeatedly targeted: a 2017 attempt to gut it, 2023 rules limiting its staffing and board ([CLC](https://campaignlegal.org/update/clean-act-will-preserve-and-protect-office-congressional-ethics); [CRS R40760](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R40760)). Internal rules can be rewritten by simple majority on day one of any Congress — which is the structural argument that only constitutional status makes ethics enforcement durable.

**The judiciary — a code that binds everyone but the top.** Lower federal judges are covered by the Code of Conduct for United States Judges *and* an enforcement statute, the Judicial Conduct and Disability Act of 1980. **Neither applies to Supreme Court justices** ([CRS LSB11078](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11078)). There is no procedure — other than impeachment — for filing a misconduct complaint against a justice.

### The current bill landscape (2023–2026)

- **TRUST in Congress Act** ([H.R. 345, Spanberger (D) / Roy (R)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/345/text)): members, spouses, and dependent children must place covered investments in a **qualified blind trust** for their entire tenure. Cosponsors ran from Matt Gaetz to AOC-adjacent progressives — the House's full-spectrum proof point ([Spanberger release](https://spanberger.house.gov/posts/at-start-of-new-congress-spanberger-roy-reintroduce-trust-in-congress-act-to-ban-members-of-congress-their-families-from-trading-individual-stocks)).
- **ETHICS Act → HONEST Act** ([Merkley, with Peters, Hawley, Ossoff](https://www.merkley.senate.gov/peters-merkley-hawley-and-ossoff-announce-historic-bipartisan-agreement-to-advance-legislation-to-ban-member-stock-trading/)): the landmark. Immediate ban on buying; sales barred 90 days post-enactment; **divestment of covered investments by Members, the President, and the Vice President starting in 2027**. First stock-trading ban ever marked up and advanced by a Senate committee ([HSGAC](https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/media/dems/in-historic-first-committee-advances-peters-merkley-hawley-ossoff-and-rosen-bipartisan-legislation-to-ban-member-stock-trading/); [Hawley](https://www.hawley.senate.gov/hawley-peters-bipartisan-legislation-banning-congressional-stock-trading-passes-through-committee)). The Hawley–Merkley pairing is the single best evidence that this issue passes the MAGA-to-socialist test.
- **PELOSI Act** ([Hawley](https://www.hawley.senate.gov/hawley-reintroduces-pelosi-act-to-ban-congress-from-trading-stocks)): the same ban, with a name chosen to weaponize the issue against a Democrat — a reminder that each side believes the *other* side's self-dealing is the problem, which is exactly why the reform polls at 87/88.
- **The July 2026 House bill** ([H.R. 7008, Stop Insider Trading Act — passed 232–198 on July 22, 2026](https://rollcall.com/2026/07/22/congressional-stock-trading-bill-passes-the-house/)): bars members, spouses, and dependents from *buying* stocks, requires 7–14 days' public notice before sales — but **lets members keep everything they already own**, does not touch the President, and Republicans attached a **voter-ID (SAVE Act) rider** that collapsed bipartisan support to 13 Democratic votes ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/house/5983948-house-passes-stock-trading-bill/); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/23/house-stock-trading-ban-trump-exemption-vote/) noted it exempts the President's thousands of disclosed trades). Lesson for the foundation: even the most popular reform in America gets hollowed and rider-poisoned inside Congress — the statutory route bends toward self-protection.
- **Supreme Court Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act** ([S. 359, Whitehouse](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/359)): requires the Court to adopt a code, **creates an investigation mechanism for violations**, mandates disclosure of party/amicus connections and dark-money interests, and requires public explanations of recusal decisions ([Whitehouse release](https://www.whitehouse.senate.gov/news/release/whitehouse-blumenthal-hirono-leahy-feinstein-schatz-booker-introduce-supreme-court-ethics-recusal-and-transparency-act/)). Advanced out of Judiciary Committee in July 2023 on party lines — unlike the trading ban, **SCOTUS ethics legislation currently codes partisan in Congress even though it polls 88R/93D with voters**. That gap between elite and mass polarization is a key strategic fact.
- **Crypto and prediction markets — the new frontier with no rules at all.** The Trump family's crypto holdings (memecoin, World Liberty Financial, a [$1.4B+ portfolio](https://fortune.com/2026/07/01/inside-trumps-1-4-billion-crypto-empire-altcoins-bitcoin-strategy/)) and prediction-market ties (Trump Jr. as paid adviser to Kalshi and investor/adviser to Polymarket, plus the family's own "Truth Predict" venture) have produced self-dealing allegations with no applicable statute ([DL News](https://www.dlnews.com/articles/people-culture/trump-family-crypto-empire-under-pressure-as-self-dealing-and-corruption-allegations-mount/)). House Oversight has opened an investigation into whether officials with non-public information trade event contracts ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/15/kalshi-and-polymarket-congress-regulation-washington-influence.html)); Kalshi has already had to fine congressional candidates for betting on their own races. Polling context: a crypto self-dealing ban draws 71% (67R) — lower than the stock ban but still comfortably full-spectrum. Amendment language should use an asset-neutral term ("any financial instrument or contract whose value the officer's official actions may affect") so it covers whatever 2040's version of a memecoin is.

### The Supreme Court's November 2023 Code of Conduct — an analysis

Adopted November 13, 2023, under pressure from the ProPublica revelations, the [Code](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11078) "gathers in one place" existing principles — and changes essentially nothing:

- **No complaint process.** Unlike lower courts (Judicial Conduct and Disability Act), there is no way to file, investigate, or adjudicate an alleged violation ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-supreme-courts-code-of-conduct-enforcement-confusion-extrajudicial-activism/)).
- **No sanctions.** Each justice self-applies the code; recusal remains an individual, unreviewable, usually unexplained decision.
- **Softened language.** Commentators noted the Court's version weakened "shall" duties from the lower-court code into discretionary formulations ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2023/11/the-new-scotus-code-of-conduct/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/11/13/1212708142/supreme-court-ethics-code)).

The precipitating scandal — [ProPublica's "Friends of the Court" series](https://www.propublica.org/series/supreme-court-scotus) documenting two decades of undisclosed luxury travel, yacht and jet trips, and gifts to Justice Thomas from Harlan Crow, later [partially conceded in amended disclosures](https://www.propublica.org/article/clarence-thomas-gift-disclosures-harlan-crow) as "inadvertently omitted" — must be handled carefully. ⚠️ *Politicization flag*: because the reporting centered on a conservative justice, the right reads SCOTUS-ethics reform as pretextual court-attack; a Senate probe found [additional undisclosed jet trips](https://www.propublica.org/article/clarence-thomas-harlan-crow-private-jet-flights-senate-investigation-scotus), but Justices Sotomayor's and Jackson's book-income and staff-promotion controversies give symmetric material. The foundation's framing should be institutional (the *only* unaccountable court in the country) rather than personal.

### Constitutional dimensions

**Emoluments Clauses — real prohibitions, no working remedy.** The Foreign Emoluments Clause (Art. I, § 9, cl. 8) bars officeholders from accepting anything of value from foreign states without congressional consent; the Domestic Emoluments Clause (Art. II, § 1, cl. 7) fixes the President's compensation and bars other federal or state payments ([CRS IF11086](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF11086/IF11086.15.pdf)). All three Trump-era enforcement suits died on procedure, not merits: *CREW v. Trump* was [dismissed as moot by the Supreme Court on January 25, 2021](https://thehill.com/regulation/court-battles/535668-supreme-court-dismisses-emoluments-lawsuits-against-trump-as-moot/) when Trump left office, with lower-court rulings vacated — so no precedent survives ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2021/01/justices-vacate-rulings-on-trump-and-emoluments/); [CREW case page](https://www.citizensforethics.org/legal-action/lawsuits/crew-v-donald-j-trump/) *(advocacy)*); the D.C./Maryland suit was [dismissed for lack of standing](https://www.npr.org/2019/07/10/740258653/appeals-court-dismisses-emoluments-lawsuit-against-trump); *Blumenthal v. Trump* (215 members of Congress) likewise fell on standing. **The lesson: constitutional ethics text without a designated enforcer and standing rule is a parchment barrier.** Any new amendment must build the remedy into the text.

**Impeachment — the only current constitutional ethics remedy, and a dead letter for this purpose.** One Supreme Court justice has ever been impeached (Samuel Chase, 1804 — acquitted); no President has been removed. A two-thirds Senate supermajority as the *only* sanction means the effective standard for judicial and presidential ethics is "anything short of what 67 senators will remove you for."

**Can Congress regulate SCOTUS ethics by statute?** Justice Alito, to the Wall Street Journal: ["No provision in the Constitution gives them the authority to regulate the Supreme Court — period."](https://thehill.com/regulation/court-battles/4125436-samuel-alito-on-congress-authority-to-regulate-supreme-court/) Justice Kagan, publicly disagreeing: ["Of course Congress can regulate"](https://news.yahoo.com/were-not-imperial-elena-kagan-185703263.html?fr=sycsrp_catchall) — Congress already sets the Court's size, budget, quorum, terms, and (since 1948) its recusal statute, 28 U.S.C. § 455. The scholarly consensus, reflected in the [Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court](https://abcnews.go.com/Politics/justice-alito-rejects-congress-power-ethics-public-break/story?id=101911956) and [CRS LSB10255](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10255), is that Congress has substantial but not unlimited power — disclosure and gift rules are safely within it; an enforcement body that could override a justice's participation in a case is genuinely contested. **This unresolved contest is itself an argument for the amendment: only constitutional text can settle whether SCOTUS ethics enforcement is legitimate, and settle it in a way the Court itself cannot strike down.**

### Prior Amendment Proposals

A Congress.gov sweep shows ethics has been pursued almost entirely by statute; the joint-resolution (amendment) traffic in adjacent space is dominated by **term limits** ([H.J.Res.5, 119th](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/5); [H.J.Res.11, 118th](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-joint-resolution/11)). Notable data points:

- **No ethics-enforcement or trading-ban amendment has ever advanced out of committee.** The field is genuinely open — the foundation would not be relitigating a failed amendment, unlike (say) balanced-budget or flag-burning proposals.
- **The 27th Amendment is the governing precedent.** The most recently ratified amendment (1992) is precisely an anti-self-dealing rule for Congress (no pay raise takes effect until after an election). It proves the public will ratify — enthusiastically — an amendment whose entire content is "Congress may not enrich itself."
- **The omnibus exists in embryo:** [H.R. 9222 (119th)](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr9222.xml) bundles a stock/prediction-market ban for the President, VP, and Congress with SCOTUS term limits and ethics rules, emoluments strengthening, pardon reform, and campaign-finance provisions — statutorily. It is a one-member messaging bill, but its table of contents is nearly the foundation's platform, and its existence shows the bundling instinct is already in the water.
- CRS maintains a current inventory of member-financial-activity proposals: [R48641](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48641).

## State Models

**Rhode Island — the constitutional ethics commission with real teeth.** A [1986 constitutional amendment](https://ethics.ri.gov/code-ethics/constitutional-authority) created an independent Ethics Commission with the constitutional power to **adopt and enforce** a code of ethics — conflicts of interest, use of position, financial disclosure — over *all* state and municipal elected officials, appointed officials, and employees. When the state supreme court held (2009) that the speech-in-debate clause shielded legislators from the commission's jurisdiction, voters responded with [2016's Question 2](https://ballotpedia.org/Rhode_Island_Ethics_Commission_Amendment,_Question_2_(2016)), expressly subjecting the General Assembly to the commission — passed overwhelmingly. Rhode Island is the closest existing analogue to a federal ethics amendment: rule-making + investigation + prosecution + penalties, all constitutionally insulated from the legislature it polices. (Note the tension with the federal polling: RI covers *all* public employees, the design that polls at 40% federally — but RI's commission was created and re-ratified by landslide votes. Enforcement against ordinary employees is rare and uncontroversial; the political fights are all about top officials. Still, the federal amendment should stay narrow.)

**Oklahoma — Article XXIX.** Added by [citizen initiative in 1990, approved 2-to-1](https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry.php?entry=OK043): a five-member constitutional [Ethics Commission](https://oksenate.gov/sites/default/files/2022-05/oc29.pdf) that promulgates and enforces rules of ethical conduct for state officers, employees, and campaigns, with civil penalties. Deep-red-state proof that constitutional ethics machinery is not a progressive project.

**Florida — the Sunshine Amendment.** Florida's Art. II, § 8 ("Ethics in Government"), adopted by citizen initiative in 1976, constitutionalized **full public financial disclosure** for elected officials and candidates, breach-of-trust forfeiture, and post-office lobbying restrictions, with a Commission on Ethics to investigate. Florida voters strengthened it again in 2018 (expanded lobbying bans). Fifty years of operation in the third-largest state.

**Judicial conduct commissions — all 50 states.** California created the first commission empowered to investigate and discipline judges in 1960; **by 1980 every state had one**, covering their supreme courts ([State Democracy Research Initiative](https://statedemocracy.law.wisc.edu/our-work/state-judicial-conduct-commissions); [State Court Report](https://statecourtreport.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/judicial-ethics-and-discipline-states)). Senator Whitehouse's office compiled a [50-state survey](https://www.whitehouse.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-11-26-Judicial-Ethics-50-State-Survey.pdf) *(partisan office, but the underlying survey is factual)*; [Fix the Court](https://fixthecourt.com/2024/09/several-states-enforceable-ethics/) *(advocacy)* catalogs how states handle the delicate case of complaints against their own supreme court justices — several route them to special tribunals so justices never judge their own colleagues. **The US Supreme Court is the outlier not just internationally but domestically: it is the only court of last resort in America with no conduct commission behind it.** This is arguably the single most powerful talking point in the whole ethics category.

**Governors — disclosure and blind trusts.** Nearly every state requires gubernatorial financial disclosure; several (and political practice in many more) push wealthy governors into blind trusts. The state pattern confirms the polling instinct: rules aim at elected power, are administered by commissions, and have not produced the "lawfare against officeholders" spiral that opponents of federal enforcement predict.

## International Models

### Canada — Conflict of Interest Act (2006) + Ethics Commissioner

The gold standard among peers. The [Conflict of Interest Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-36.65/section-27.html) requires the PM, ministers, and senior officials ("reporting public office holders") to **divest all controlled assets — publicly traded securities, commodities, options — within 120 days of appointment**, by arm's-length sale or blind trust, policed by an independent, statutory [Conflict of Interest and Ethics Commissioner](https://ciec-ccie.parl.gc.ca/en/rules-reglements/Pages/INDivestmentBlindTrust-AIDessaisissementTrusts.aspx). "Controlled assets" is defined functionally: "assets whose value could be directly or indirectly affected by government decisions or policy" ([CIEC backgrounder](https://ciec-ccie.parl.gc.ca/en/rules-reglements/Documents/Backgrounder/Backgrounder%20-%20blind%20trusts.pdf)) — a definition ready-made for crypto and prediction markets. Notably, a 2025 Commons committee reviewing PM Carney's blind trust concluded blind trusts are *insufficient* for the top job and recommended the PM be required to **fully divest by sale**, "since placement in a blind trust does not constitute true divestment" ([ETHI Committee](https://www.ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/45-1/ETHI/report-5/page-24); [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/mark-carney-ethics-blind-trust-9.7174747)). Canada's debate has moved a full generation past America's: they're arguing about whether blind trusts are strict *enough* for the chief executive the US wholly exempts.

### United Kingdom — Ministerial Code + Independent Adviser (+ the ACOBA cautionary tale)

The [Ministerial Code](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/independent-adviser-ministerial-standards) requires that "no conflict arises, or appears to arise, between their public duties and their private interests," with an Independent Adviser on Ministers' Interests maintaining a published [List of Ministers' Interests](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672a43cb541e1dfbf71e8be7/List_of_Ministers__Interests_-_December_2023.pdf); divestment is treated as "the gold standard" for avoiding perceived conflicts ([parliamentary evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/71505/html/)). The cautionary half: both the Adviser and ACOBA (revolving-door regulator) were purely advisory — parliamentary witnesses called ACOBA "a toothless regulator," and it was abolished in 2025 with functions folded into the adviser's office. **UK lesson: an ethics body without sanction power decays into a formality — exactly the failure mode of the SCOTUS Code of Conduct.**

### OECD — Managing Conflict of Interest in the Public Service (2003)

The [OECD Guidelines](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/public/doc/130/130.en.pdf) are the international benchmark: officials must "identify and disclose relevant private interests" on taking office and periodically thereafter, with the organization obliged to resolve or manage the conflict. On average, OECD members have adopted [76% of the OECD's conflict-of-interest criteria](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/conflict-of-interest.html); the US executive branch scores well for *employees* — the gap is confined to the top.

### UNCAC Article 8 — a treaty the US already ratified

The [UN Convention against Corruption](https://www.congress.gov/treaty-document/109th-congress/6/document-text) (US ratified 2006) commits parties to "apply... codes or standards of conduct for the correct, honourable and proper performance of public functions" (Art. 8(2)) and to establish asset-declaration systems (Art. 8(5)) ([UNODC](https://www.unodc.org/unodc/en/corruption/WG-Prevention/codes-of-conduct.html)). Framing value: an ethics amendment implements obligations America already accepted before the world — useful with sovereignty-minded conservatives (we're enforcing *our own* treaty, on *our own* elites).

### South Africa — Public Protector and the Chapter 9 model

The 1996 Constitution's [Chapter 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_nine_institutions) creates independent "state institutions supporting constitutional democracy" — including the Public Protector — "independent, subject only to the Constitution and the law... without fear, favour or prejudice," which all organs of state must assist and protect ([UCT analysis](https://constitutionnet.org/sites/default/files/chapter_6._chapter_9_institutions.pdf)). In the Nkandla judgment (2016), the Constitutional Court held the Protector's remedial orders **binding even on the President**. Chapter 9 is the strongest existing template for *constitutionalizing* an integrity institution — and also a caution: the office's credibility later suffered under a politicized officeholder, showing that appointment design matters as much as independence.

### Singapore CPIB and Hong Kong ICAC — independent enforcement works

Both agencies turned famously corrupt jurisdictions into world leaders, on three ingredients researchers consistently identify: **broad mandate, genuine operational independence, and adequate resources** ([MIT Daedalus](https://direct.mit.edu/daed/article/147/3/202/27206/Combating-Corruption-in-Asian-Countries-Learning); [Emerald/PAP](https://www.emerald.com/pap/article/25/2/109/317430/It-takes-a-whole-society-why-Hong-Kong-s-ICAC)). Neither is a constitutional-democracy model to copy wholesale (both operate under strong-executive systems), but they are the standing empirical answer to "independent anti-corruption enforcement can't work."

### Brazil — Ficha Limpa: popular-initiative anti-corruption at scale

The 2010 [Clean Record Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ficha_Limpa) — barring candidates with certain convictions for eight years — was forced onto Congress's agenda by a **popular petition of 2M+ signatures** organized by 40+ civil-society groups, and passed "against the wishes of most politicians" ([AS/COA](https://www.as-coa.org/articles/explainer-brazils-clean-record-law); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/clean-slate-law-raising-accountability-in-brazil/)). The relevance is less the substance than the *mechanism*: a mass citizen campaign making legislators vote, visibly, against their own self-interest — the exact political physics an Article V ethics amendment requires.

### Peer supreme courts — everyone else has enforceable judicial ethics

Canada's [Canadian Judicial Council](https://cjc-ccm.ca/cmslib/general/news_pub_judicialconduct_Principles_en.pdf) investigates complaints against all federally appointed judges and can recommend removal — and "Canada is one of the very few countries where a complaint can be made against the Chief Justice in the same way as any other judge" ([Justice Canada](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/pl/jc-cj/index.html)). Germany's constitutional-court judges sit within the statutory judicial-service and disciplinary framework, and the UK's senior judiciary answers to a Judicial Conduct Investigations Office; the Federal Judicial Center's [Judiciaries Worldwide](https://judiciariesworldwide.fjc.gov/judicial-ethics) resource and [Fix the Court's survey](https://fixthecourt.com/2024/09/several-states-enforceable-ethics/) *(advocacy)* confirm the pattern: **among peer democracies, a top court answerable to no ethics process is essentially unknown.** Pair with the § 208 point: *the American President is nearly alone among democratic chief executives in being exempt from conflict-of-interest law ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/08/31/politics/trump-conflict-of-interest); [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/policy-solutions/strengthening-presidential-ethics-law)), and the American Supreme Court is alone among peer top courts in self-policing without enforcement.* The two exceptionalisms make one slogan: **"No one above the rules."**

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Divest-or-blind-trust within a fixed clock is the proven mechanic.** Canada's 120-day divestment of "controlled assets" (sale or blind trust) has run for two decades across governments of both parties without constitutional crisis or talent flight. An amendment can safely constitutionalize the *duty* and leave the clock and mechanics to statute.
2. **Define assets functionally, not by instrument.** "Assets whose value could be affected by government decisions" (Canada) automatically covers stocks, crypto, memecoins, and prediction-market contracts — and whatever comes next. Enumerating instruments in constitutional text guarantees obsolescence.
3. **Advisory bodies decay; empowered bodies endure.** UK ACOBA (advisory → "toothless" → abolished) versus Rhode Island's commission (constitutional enforcement power → still feared 40 years on). The dividing line is sanction authority, not prestige.
4. **Independence must be structural — appointment, budget, and tenure — not merely declared.** South Africa's Chapter 9 text ("without fear, favour or prejudice," with a duty on all organs of state to protect the institutions) plus the CPIB/ICAC research triad (mandate, independence, resources) define the checklist. A commission the President appoints at will, or Congress can defund, is not independent.
5. **Cover the top or lose the public.** Every peer regime covers the chief executive; Canada's parliament is now debating whether blind trusts are strict *enough* for the PM. Regimes that visibly exempt the most powerful (US § 208, SCOTUS code) are precisely the ones with collapsing public trust.
6. **Judicial ethics enforcement is compatible with judicial independence — the evidence is 50 states and every peer democracy.** Sixty-plus years of state conduct commissions and the Canadian Judicial Council have produced no discernible loss of judicial independence. The empirical burden now sits on the objectors.
7. **Constitutional entrenchment is what stops the day-one rollback.** Congressional ethics offices get gutted by rules votes (OCE 2017, 2023); ministerial codes get rewritten by each PM. Rhode Island's and Oklahoma's commissions survive because repeal requires going back to the voters.
8. **Treaty and comparative framing neutralizes "radical" objections.** The US already promised codes of conduct and asset declarations under UNCAC Art. 8; the amendment asks American leaders to accept what Canadian ministers, UK ministers, and every state governor already live under. This is catch-up, not experiment.
9. **Popular-initiative energy is the natural engine for anti-self-dealing law.** Ficha Limpa (2M signatures), Rhode Island 1986/2016, Oklahoma 1990 (2-to-1), Florida 1976 — ethics rules over legislators' objection routinely win landslides when put to the people. Article V has no national initiative, but the campaign design (make every legislator publicly choose between voters and their portfolios) transfers.
10. **One enforcement body per branch may beat one super-body.** Peer systems separate ministerial ethics (commissioner), parliamentary ethics (chamber commissioners), and judicial ethics (judicial councils). A single commission over all three US branches maximizes both separation-of-powers objections and weaponization fears; branch-tailored enforcement inside a common constitutional standard is the lower-risk architecture.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. The five below clear it — the trading ban at 86–87% (87R/88–90D), the presidential extension at 87% (87R/90D), judicial ethics enforcement at 90% (88R/93D), the independence principle backed by 2-to-1 state constitutional ratifications (Rhode Island, Oklahoma, Florida), and disclosure as a universally 80%+ valence issue. One candidate is held back at the end.*

**1. The anti-self-dealing rule (the core, and the poll-tested heart of the package).**
Proposed formulation: *"No person holding the office of President or Vice President, no Senator or Representative, and no Justice or judge of the United States shall, during their continuance in office, purchase, sell, or hold any individual security, digital asset, or contract of speculation whose value their official conduct may affect, other than through a diversified fund or qualified blind trust as Congress shall by law provide."*
- Directly implements the 86–87% full-spectrum consensus; mirrors the ETHICS/HONEST Act architecture (ban + blind-trust safe harbor) and Canada's controlled-assets definition; "contract of speculation" reaches prediction markets and whatever succeeds them.
- ⚠️ **Breadth trap**: the covered class must stop at elected/appointed constitutional officers — extending to civil servants collapses support from 86% to 40%. Resist every "why not all federal employees?" amendment-by-committee impulse; the RI model's universal coverage is the wrong federal template.
- ⚠️ **Wealth-penalty framing trap**: forced *sale* of existing holdings polled as "goes too far" for 69% in 2016 (see POLLING_RESEARCH.md); the blind-trust/diversified-fund safe harbor is what separates "no self-dealing" (wildly popular) from "punish successful people for serving" (a real attack line, and a genuine deterrent to non-wealthy candidates who own a business). Keep the safe harbor in the constitutional text, not just the implementing statute.
- ⚠️ **Definition trap**: "whose value their official conduct may affect" is doing heavy lifting — too precise and it's a statute, too loose and courts fill the gap. Follow the Constitution's own style (cf. "emolument," "high crimes and misdemeanors"): broad principle in the text, express delegation to Congress for definitions, so drafting fights happen in legislation rather than ratification.

**2. End the chief-executive exemption.**
Proposed formulation: *"The President and Vice President shall be subject to the laws of the United States governing conflicts of interest and financial disclosure to the same extent as other civil officers, except that recusal shall not be required of the President where the duty to execute the office admits of no substitute."*
- Closes the [§ 208 carve-out](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/208) while honoring the one legitimate rationale for it (the President can't recuse from the presidency) — divestment and disclosure apply; only recusal bends. Polls at 87% (87R/90D) when framed as extending the congressional ban upward.
- ⚠️ **Who-enforces trap (presidential edition)**: criminal enforcement against a sitting President collides with DOJ's non-indictment doctrine and post-*Trump v. United States* immunity for official acts. Options: civil enforcement, enforcement deferred until after the term, or forfeiture-of-gains remedies. The amendment should make the *obligation* constitutional and leave the remedy menu to Congress — but must say *someone* can enforce it, or it becomes Emoluments Clause 2.0 (see Principle 5).

**3. Enforceable ethics for the Supreme Court and federal judiciary.**
Proposed formulation: *"The judicial power shall be exercised subject to a code of conduct, which Congress shall have power to prescribe by law, binding on all Justices and judges of the United States; complaints of violation shall be heard through a process established by law in which no judge shall finally judge their own cause."*
- Settles the [Alito–Kagan dispute](https://abcnews.go.com/Politics/justice-alito-rejects-congress-power-ethics-public-break/story?id=101911956) in constitutional text; brings the US in line with all 50 states and every peer democracy; polls at 90% (88R/93D) — the highest number in the platform.
- ⚠️ **Who-enforces trap (judicial edition)**: a body that can sanction justices is a body that can be aimed at justices — conservatives hear "lawfare against the Court we finally won," and reform-minded Democrats will reject anything toothless. The state-model answer: enforcement *within* the judiciary (a conduct panel of Article III judges, no justice judging their own case), sanctions short of removal, removal still requiring impeachment. Teeth without a political trigger-puller.
- ⚠️ **Politicization trap**: the Thomas/ProPublica origin story codes this as anti-conservative. Draft and message prospectively and symmetrically; consider a delayed effective date so no one can compute which current justice it "targets."

**4. A constitutionally independent ethics process (the Rhode Island abstraction).**
Proposed formulation: *"Ethics rules for covered officers shall be administered and enforced by processes independent of the officers they govern; no chamber, court, or officer shall be the final judge of their own compliance."*
- Abstracts the common failure across all three branches — House/Senate committees at 3% violation-findings, the self-applied SCOTUS code, the self-exempted President — into one neutral principle with deep resonance in both natural-justice tradition ("no man a judge in his own cause") and the polling on independent enforcement.
- ⚠️ **Who-enforces trap (structural edition)**: a single national Ethics Commission over President, Congress, and Court is the maximal separation-of-powers provocation and the maximal weaponization target ("unelected fourth branch"). The safer architecture the text above permits: branch-specific mechanisms (independent congressional office with OCE-plus powers; judicial conduct panels; statutory presidential enforcement) unified only by the constitutional *principle* of independence. South Africa's Chapter 9 shows entrenched independence works; its later politicized Public Protector shows appointment design is the whole game.

**5. The disclosure floor.**
Proposed formulation: *"Covered officers shall annually make public disclosure of their finances, and of every gift or benefit above a minimal value, as Congress shall by law provide; willful concealment shall be punishable by law."*
- Constitutionalizes the one thing the STOCK Act got right (transparency) while curing what it got wrong (the $200 fine, per [CLC](https://campaignlegal.org/update/congressional-stock-trading-and-stock-act)); directly responsive to the Thomas gift-disclosure failures; Florida's Sunshine Amendment proves 50-year durability.
- ⚠️ **Breadth trap again**: "covered officers" must track Principle 1's narrow class. ⚠️ Also a **redundancy check**: if Principles 1–4 pass, much of this is belt-and-suspenders — in a trimmed package, disclosure can live in the implementing statutes instead. Keep it as a fallback provision if the trading ban gets negotiated down.

**Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar:**

- **Emoluments with a remedy** (*"The prohibitions of this article and of the emoluments clauses of this Constitution shall be enforceable by law…"*) — structurally sound (the Trump-era suits died on standing and mootness, not the merits), but the polling on every real-world emoluments framing splits 40–60 points by party (Qatar jet: 40R/85D — see [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md) §4), and no neutral polling on emoluments *enforcement* exists. Path back: commission polling on a both-parties framing ("no president may accept payments from foreign governments — enforceable in court"); if it clears the bar, this clause folds into the enforcement section at low drafting cost.

---

**The package skeleton.** A ratifiable ethics amendment is Principles 1–3 fused into a short article: (i) the trading/self-dealing ban on the President, VP, Congress, and the Court with a blind-trust safe harbor; (ii) the end of the presidential conflict-of-interest exemption; (iii) an enforceable judicial code with no-self-judging process — wrapped in (iv) the independence principle from Principle 4 as the enforcement clause, and (v) a one-sentence enforcement/standing authorization for the article. Everything else delegates to Congress "by law." (The held-back emoluments-remedy clause rejoins here if polling clears it.) The campaign is the 27th Amendment's logic at scale — *the people, not the officeholders, set the rules for the officeholders* — carried by the only reform in America that polls 86%+ with both parties, against the backdrop of a Congress that has proven for fourteen years it cannot pass the statutory version cleanly.

**Inherently statutory:** (leave out of the constitutional text; cite as implementing legislation the amendment authorizes)

- Dollar thresholds, de minimis exemptions, filing deadlines, and penalty schedules (the STOCK Act's $200 fine is the cautionary tale for hard-coding numbers anywhere durable)
- Qualified-blind-trust mechanics: trustee qualifications, communication firewalls, divestment clocks (Canada's 120 days), capital-gains rollover/tax-deferral relief for compelled divestment
- Precise asset taxonomies (securities vs. digital assets vs. event contracts) and the diversified-fund definition
- Recusal procedures and disclosure formats for judges; the composition, staffing, and budget of conduct panels
- Spouse/dependent coverage details and hardship waivers
- Gift valuation rules, personal-hospitality definitions, and travel-disclosure specifics
- Revolving-door/lobbying cooling-off periods (the Lobbying category — keep the ethics amendment clean of it)
- Crypto/prediction-market regulatory plumbing (CFTC/SEC jurisdiction, platform duties like Kalshi's self-dealing rules)
- Whistleblower channels and referral procedures between ethics bodies and DOJ
- OCE-style congressional-office design (board structure, anonymous complaints, subpoena power) — protected in principle by the amendment's independence clause, specified by rule and statute

## Review Additions (2026-08-17)
*Added after an outside completeness review; every item below was verified against the linked source before inclusion. Items are grouped by the memo section they extend.*

### Extends: The holes in current law

- **The 2013 STOCK Act rollback is the historical smoking gun for "the statutory route bends toward self-protection."** [S. 716](https://www.congress.gov/bill/113th-congress/senate-bill/716/all-actions) was introduced by Sen. Reid on April 11, 2013, and passed the Senate by unanimous consent *the same day*; the House passed it "without objection" on April 12; President Obama signed it April 15 as [Pub. L. 113-7](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-113publ7/html/PLAW-113publ7.htm). Its text makes the STOCK Act's online-posting sections (§§ 8(a), 11(a)) "not … effective" for everyone except the President, VP, Members of Congress, congressional candidates, and Senate-confirmed Executive Schedule Level I–II officers — i.e., it repealed searchable online disclosure for congressional staff and most senior executive-branch officials one year after the Act passed, in two days, with no recorded vote. Memo relevance: this is the pre-2026 proof (alongside the July 2026 rider) that disclosure rules living in ordinary statute get quietly narrowed when attention fades — the core argument for the **disclosure floor (Principle 5)** carrying constitutional status.
- **The origin of the presidential § 208 exemption is a 1974 OLC opinion, not just the 1989 codification.** Deputy Attorney General Laurence Silberman's August 1974 memo on the Rockefeller vice-presidential nomination ([OLC opinion, via Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/documents/5194db92f6/20200428_Conflict-of-Interest-Problems-Arising-out-of-the-President-s-Nomination-of-Nelson-A-Rockef.pdf)) reasoned that the criminal conflict statutes did not reach the President because applying them would "disempower him from performing some of the functions prescribed [in] the Constitution"; Congress then wrote the exclusion into the statute in 1989, reasoning that recusal of the President from policy decisions was "inconceivable" ([Harvard Journal on Legislation](https://journals.law.harvard.edu/jol/2018/03/25/it-is-all-about-the-money-presidential-conflicts-of-interest/)). Memo relevance: the exemption rests on a *separation-of-powers* theory about recusal — which is exactly why **Principle 2**'s formulation (divestment and disclosure apply; only recusal bends) is the right cut, and why an amendment rather than a statute is needed to overcome an executive-branch constitutional objection.
- **The one Member imprisoned for insider trading was not prosecuted under the STOCK Act — which sharpens, not weakens, the memo's point.** Rep. Chris Collins (R-NY) was sentenced to 26 months in January 2020 for tipping his son about a failed drug trial; the [DOJ release](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/former-congressman-christopher-collins-sentenced-insider-trading-scheme-and-lying) grounds the case in duties he owed *as an Innate Immunotherapeutics board member*, not in congressional information, and never invokes the STOCK Act. Memo relevance: the memo's "no one has ever been prosecuted under the Act" stands; the Collins case is the pre-emptive answer to the counterargument that existing law already catches congressional insider trading — it caught a corporate insider who happened to be a Member.
- **The Supreme Court narrowed statutory anti-corruption law again in *Snyder v. United States* (June 26, 2024, 6–3, Kavanaugh).** The Court held that 18 U.S.C. § 666 "proscribes bribes to state and local officials but does not make it a crime for those officials to accept gratuities for their past acts" — a $13,000 post-contract payment to a mayor was not covered ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-108)). Memo relevance: joins *McDonnell* and *Percoco* in a line of decisions reading federal corruption statutes narrowly; supports the memo's structural argument that ethics floors written only in statute can be interpreted down, and that a constitutional text with express delegation to Congress is more durable.

### Extends: Constitutional dimensions

- **The federal Speech or Debate Clause (Art. I, § 6, cl. 1) is the Rhode Island loophole at national scale.** In *United States v. Helstoski*, 442 U.S. 477 (1979), the Court held that "evidence of a legislative act of a Member of Congress may not be introduced by the Government in a prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 201" (bribery), even to show motive, and that waiver "can be found only after explicit and unequivocal renunciation of the protection" ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/442/477)). Memo relevance: an independent ethics enforcer (Principle 4) that cannot examine legislative acts cannot investigate committee-oversight-adjacent trading; the amendment must expressly subordinate the Speech or Debate privilege for ethics enforcement, exactly as Rhode Island had to do by second referendum in 2016.
- ***Nevada Commission on Ethics v. Carrigan*, 564 U.S. 117 (2011), removes the First Amendment counterargument.** Unanimous in judgment (Scalia), the Court held that a legislator's vote is not protected speech — "a legislator's vote is the commitment of his apportioned share of the legislature's power" — and upheld a state ethics commission's recusal rule, noting that "within 15 years of the founding, both the House and the Senate adopted recusal rules" and that "federal conflict-of-interest rules applicable to judges also date back to the founding" ([LII syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/10-568.ZS.html)). Memo relevance: conflict-of-interest restrictions on officeholders have founding-era pedigree and are constitutionally uncontroversial as applied to legislators; the strongest legal objection to Principles 1 and 4 is already foreclosed by a 9–0 judgment.

### Extends: The Supreme Court's November 2023 Code of Conduct — an analysis

- **The disclosure regime, unlike the conduct code, was actually tightened — by the Judicial Conference, under statute.** In March 2023 the Judicial Conference narrowed the "personal hospitality" exemption in its Ethics in Government Act disclosure regulations: personal-hospitality non-reporting now applies only to stays at a personal home; food or lodging at a commercial facility, or lodging owned by an entity rather than an individual, must be disclosed, as must free transportation by private jet ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2023/03/29/federal-courts-tweak-ethics-rule-for-travel-meals-and-more/); [Fix the Court](https://fixthecourt.com/2023/03/new-judicial-disclosure-rules-its-a-low-bar-but-well-give-you-some-credit/) *(advocacy)*), and the rules apply to Supreme Court justices as well as lower-court judges. Memo relevance: this is the counterexample to "nothing changed" — a statutory delegation (EIGA → Judicial Conference) *did* close the specific loophole the Thomas/Crow travel used. It supports the memo's split: **disclosure** can be fixed by ordinary law and delegated machinery; **enforcement** for the Court (Principle 3) is what remains structurally missing.

### Flagged corrections (verified — founder to apply)

- **UK Supreme Court judicial-conduct oversight.** The memo (Peer supreme courts section) says "the UK's senior judiciary answers to a Judicial Conduct Investigations Office," and the headline finding says the US Supreme Court is "the only apex institution — foreign or domestic — that polices its own ethics with no enforcement mechanism." The UK Supreme Court's own [Judicial Complaints Policy (revised 03/07/2026)](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/Judicial_Complaints_Policy_2026_03_07_26_b8424d0f8e.pdf) shows complaints against UKSC Justices are *not* routed through the JCIO: they go to the Court's Chief Executive, who refers valid complaints to "the President or the Deputy President of the Supreme Court" (¶15). So the UKSC is, formally, self-administering. But it is not "no enforcement mechanism": the policy provides for formal advice/warnings published on the Court's website (¶31), and for a tribunal of the Lady Chief Justice of England and Wales, the Lord President of the Court of Session, the Lady Chief Justice of Northern Ireland, and two independent persons (¶26), whose report may be sent to the Lord Chancellor to begin removal under s. 33 of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 (¶27). Suggested fix: change the JCIO reference to describe the UKSC's own published complaints procedure with an external tribunal and statutory removal path, and soften the "only apex institution … no enforcement mechanism" line to "the only peer apex court with no written complaint procedure, no sanction short of impeachment, and no external review." The comparative point survives — it just needs the more precise phrasing.

## Implementation: the law today and the day after (2026-08-18)

*Amendment-specific companion to [RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md](RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md) (general law of supremacy, self-execution, enforcement clauses, trigger laws). Applies that law to the five-section Ethics draft in [PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md](PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md) § 3. Every URL below was opened and checked on 2026-08-18 unless it already appears earlier in this memo.*

### 1. Federal law today

**Section 1 (trading ban) — no federal law does this now.** CRS testimony prepared for a November 19, 2025 House Administration hearing states flatly: "Neither current law nor House or Senate rules prohibit Members of the House of Representatives, Senators, and most covered congressional employees from owning or trading specific assets," and Members "are also not required to divest themselves of assets or holdings upon taking office" ([CRS TE10119](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/TE/PDF/TE10119/TE10119.1.pdf)). What exists is disclosure plus an insider-trading rule:
- The STOCK Act (Pub. L. 112-105, 2012) requires periodic transaction reports "not later than 30 days after receiving notification of any transaction ... but in no case later than 45 days after such transaction," from the President, Vice President, Members, judicial officers, and senior staff ([5 U.S.C. § 13105(l)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13105)); a report filed more than 30 days late carries "a filing fee of $200," waivable by the supervising ethics office ([5 U.S.C. § 13106(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13106)).
- The Act's insider-trading section provides that "each Member of Congress or employee of Congress owes a duty arising from a relationship of trust and confidence ... with respect to material, nonpublic information derived from such person's position" ([15 U.S.C. § 78u-1(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/78u-1)). It restricts trading *on* information; it does not restrict *ownership*.
- For executive-branch officers, [18 U.S.C. § 208](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/208) is a *participation* (recusal) statute, not an ownership ban; OGE's regulation exempts holdings in "a diversified mutual fund or a diversified unit investment trust" from it ([5 C.F.R. § 2640.201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/5/2640.201)) — an existing federal definition of "diversified" that a Section 1 safe-harbor statute could adopt.
- For judges, [28 U.S.C. § 455(b)(4)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/455) requires "any justice, judge, or magistrate judge" to recuse when they hold "a financial interest in the subject matter in controversy or in a party," defined as an ownership interest "however small," with an exception for "a mutual or common investment fund" (§ 455(d)(4)(i)). Again recusal, not divestment.
- The Ethics in Government Act already defines "qualified blind trust" ([5 U.S.C. § 13104(f)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13104)) and exempts "a widely held investment fund (whether such fund is a mutual fund, regulated investment company, pension or deferred compensation plan, or other investment fund)" from itemized reporting (§ 13104(f)(8)). Section 1's two safe-harbor terms therefore have existing statutory anchors, though neither statute was written as a *ban with exceptions*.

**Section 2 (chief-executive exemption) — the exclusion is one sentence of statute plus one regulation.** [18 U.S.C. § 202(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/202): "Except as otherwise provided in such sections, the terms 'officer' and 'employee' in sections 203, 205, 207 through 209, and 218 of this title shall not include the President, the Vice President, a Member of Congress, or a Federal judge." OGE's Standards of Ethical Conduct likewise provide that, "for purposes other than subparts B and C of this part," the term "employee" "does not include the President or Vice President" ([5 C.F.R. § 2635.102(h)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/5/2635.102)). Financial *disclosure*, by contrast, already binds the President and Vice President by name ([5 U.S.C. § 13103(f)(1)–(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13103)). Controlling doctrine on the enforcement side: *Trump v. United States* (decided July 1, 2024, 6–3) holds a President "absolutely immune from criminal prosecution for conduct within his exclusive sphere of constitutional authority" and "entitled to at least presumptive immunity" for other official acts, with no immunity for unofficial acts ([LII, No. 23-939](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-939)). Holding or trading assets and filing disclosures are personal, not official, acts on the Court's framework — but the line will be litigated (see § 3(f) below).

**Section 3 (judicial code and complaint process) — the statute stops one rung below the top.** The Judicial Conduct and Disability Act lets "any person" complain that "a judge has engaged in conduct prejudicial to the effective and expeditious administration of the business of the courts," but defines "judge" as "a circuit judge, district judge, bankruptcy judge, or magistrate judge" ([28 U.S.C. § 351(a), (d)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/351)) — Supreme Court justices are outside it (also [CRS LSB11078](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11078)). The recusal statute does reach "any justice" (§ 455(a)) but has no review mechanism. The Court's November 2023 Code of Conduct is analyzed earlier in this memo (no complaint process, no sanctions).

**Section 4 (independent enforcement) — the constitutional text it must be read against.** Article I, § 5, cl. 2: "Each House may determine the rules of its proceedings, punish its members for disorderly behavior, and, with the concurrence of two thirds, expel a member" ([LII, Article I](https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution/articlei)); Article I, § 6, cl. 1: "for any speech or debate in either House, they shall not be questioned in any other place" (same page; *Helstoski* application earlier in this memo). Today's machinery: House and Senate ethics committees of Members with final say; the Office of Congressional Ethics with referral power only ([CRS R40760](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R40760)); the Court's self-applied code; DOJ/OGE for the executive branch. *Nevada Commission on Ethics v. Carrigan* (2011) (earlier in this memo) confirms that conflict-of-interest and recusal rules on legislators have founding-era pedigree and no First Amendment problem.

**Section 5 (disclosure floor) — the draft's terms already track the statute.** [5 U.S.C. § 13103(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13103) requires reports from "the President," "the Vice President," "a Member of Congress," and "a judicial officer." Gifts must be reported when "aggregating more than the minimal value as established by section 7342(a)(5) of this title, or $250, whichever is greater" ([5 U.S.C. § 13104(a)(2)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13104)); "minimal value" is a defined term — "a retail value in the United States at the time of acceptance of $100 or less," redefined "at 3 year intervals" for inflation ([5 U.S.C. § 7342(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/7342)). So the draft's phrase "above a minimal value" is an existing term of art, not a new one. Penalties today: a civil penalty "not to exceed $50,000" for knowing and willful falsification or failure to file, and criminal liability of "imprisonment for not more than 1 year, or both" for knowing and willful falsification ([5 U.S.C. § 13106(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/13106)); the $200 late fee is § 13106(d). The 2013 online-posting rollback (Pub. L. 113-7) is documented in the Review Additions above.

### 2. State law today

**No state law regulates the officers this amendment covers.** The draft names only federal officers (President, Vice President, Senators, Representatives, Justices and judges of the United States). State ethics codes, disclosure statutes, and conduct commissions bind state and local officials. Consequently no state constitutional provision or statute becomes unenforceable on ratification, and no state conforming legislation is required. State law matters here as evidence that each mechanism in the draft already runs somewhere:

- **Legislator financial disclosure — 49 states.** When Michigan enacted its Public Officers Financial Disclosure Act (2023), it left Idaho as "the only" state not requiring top elected officials to publicly disclose their financial interests, per the Citizens Research Council of Michigan citing NCSL ([CRC Michigan, Dec. 1, 2023](https://crcmich.org/new-financial-disclosure-law-shines-a-half-light-on-state-officials-potential-conflicts)). (Section 5's counterpart.)
- **State ethics commissions — 45 states.** NCSL's 50-state table (updated December 16, 2024) shows a state ethics commission in every state except Alaska, Arizona, Idaho, New Hampshire, and Wyoming, and notes that "in some states ethics commissions do not oversee legislators and legislative staff," where internal legislative committees serve instead ([NCSL, State Ethics Commissions](https://www.ncsl.org/ethics/state-ethics-commissions)). (Section 4's counterpart — with the same self-policing gap in a subset of states.)
- **Judicial conduct commissions — all 50 states**, covering each state's highest court (State Democracy Research Initiative and State Court Report, cited earlier in this memo). (Section 3's counterpart.)
- **Constitutional ethics text — Rhode Island (1986, 2016), Oklahoma (1990), Florida (1976)** (State Models section above). Rhode Island's 2016 amendment expressly subjecting the General Assembly to the commission after the 2009 speech-in-debate ruling is the closest state analogue to Section 4's "no chamber ... shall be the final judge of their own compliance."
- **Legislator stock-ownership bans — count not established.** I could not locate an NCSL, Ballotpedia, or CRS tally of states that bar state legislators from owning individual securities; the CRS 2025 testimony addresses only federal law. Do not cite a state count for Section 1's mechanism.

### 3. The day after ratification

**(a) What becomes unenforceable or moot.**
- The President/Vice President half of [18 U.S.C. § 202(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/202) and the parallel sentence in [5 C.F.R. § 2635.102(h)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/5/2635.102). Section 2 states a flat rule ("shall be subject to the laws ... to the same extent as other civil officers"); it has no "by law" hook, so on the general law in RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md § 2 the exclusion is inoperative to the extent of the conflict from certification. The words stay in the U.S. Code until Congress amends them; a court would apply § 208 to the President and Vice President notwithstanding § 202(c) (subject to Section 2's own recusal exception for the President "where the duty to execute the office admits of no substitute"). OGE can conform its regulation without legislation. Note § 202(c) also excludes Members and federal judges; Section 2 does not touch that part, so it stands.
- The permission, by silence, for covered officers to own and trade individual securities. Section 1 is phrased as a prohibition on the officer ("shall ... [not] purchase, sell, or hold"), which under the general law is self-executing on certification. There is no federal statute *authorizing* such holdings to strike; what changes is that the conduct becomes unconstitutional for the covered class.
- Chamber ethics committees as the *final* judge of a Member's compliance, and the justices as the final judge of their own. Section 4 is a flat rule with no "by law" hook. Article I, § 5, cl. 2 is not repealed — each House keeps its power to punish and expel — but the later-in-time amendment controls to the extent the two conflict, so an ethics process that ends with the chamber (or the Court) judging its own compliance no longer satisfies the Constitution. Whether courts would enforce Section 4 directly, or only Congress can give it shape, is the largest open question (see (f)).

**(b) What stands.** All of Title 5, chapter 131 (Ethics in Government Act disclosure, qualified-blind-trust definitions, penalties); the STOCK Act's insider-trading duty (15 U.S.C. § 78u-1(g)) and transaction reports; § 208 as applied to other executive officers; § 455 recusal; the Judicial Conduct and Disability Act for lower courts (Section 3's "no judge shall finally judge their own cause" is already how that Act works — a chief circuit judge and judicial council, not the complained-of judge, decide); bribery and gratuities statutes as narrowed by *Snyder* (above); the Emoluments Clauses; impeachment. Section 5 *confirms* the existing disclosure statute rather than displacing it — the statute now satisfies a constitutional duty and cannot be repealed or narrowed below the floor (the 2013 rollback would have been tested against it).

**(c) The draft's own transition/enforcement/effective-date clauses — there are none.** The five sections contain no effective date, no grandfather clause for existing holdings or sitting officers, no divestment window, and no general enforcement clause of the "Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation" type. The "by law" hooks are: **Section 1** — the safe harbor exists "as Congress shall by law provide"; **Section 3** — a code "which Congress shall have power to prescribe by law" (a power, not a duty) and complaints "heard through a process established by law"; **Section 5** — disclosure "as Congress shall by law provide" and concealment "punishable by law." **Sections 2 and 4** have no hook and operate of their own force. Consequence: on certification a covered officer holding an individual security is in breach unless the holding is inside a diversified fund or qualified blind trust — and whether the existing statutory definitions (5 U.S.C. § 13104(f)(3), 5 C.F.R. § 2640.201) count as what "Congress shall by law provide," or whether the safe harbor is closed until Congress legislates anew, is a first-day question. The memo's earlier suggestion of a delayed effective date is not in the draft.

**(d) Enabling legislation Congress would need or likely pass, and what already exists.**
- *Section 1 safe harbor and divestment mechanics.* [S. 1498, the HONEST Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/s/BILLSTATUS-119s1498.xml) (introduced April 28, 2025; ordered reported by Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs July 30, 2025; reported with amendment and placed on the calendar December 10, 2025) covers the President, Vice President, Members, spouses and dependents; bars individual stocks and digital assets; permits "diversified investment funds, Treasury securities, or certain other holdings"; and sets civil penalties. As reported it *prohibits* the use of qualified blind trusts and requires sitting Members to divest before their next term ([CRS TE10119](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/TE/PDF/TE10119/TE10119.1.pdf), Table 1) — stricter than the amendment's blind-trust route; whether "as Congress shall by law provide" lets Congress narrow the safe harbor that far is a litigation question. [H.R. 1908](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr1908.xml) (introduced March 6, 2025) and the House-passed [H.R. 7008](https://rollcall.com/2026/07/22/congressional-stock-trading-bill-passes-the-house/) (July 22, 2026; new purchases only, existing holdings kept, rider attached) are the House vehicles. CRS counted "at least 25 bills or resolutions" on Member financial activity in the 119th Congress through November 14, 2025 ([CRS TE10119](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/TE/PDF/TE10119/TE10119.1.pdf)). None covers judges; a post-ratification statute would have to add them, and define "digital asset" and "contract of speculation."
- *Section 3 code and complaint process for the justices.* [S. 1814 / H.R. 3513](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/s/BILLSTATUS-119s1814.xml), the Supreme Court Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act of 2025 (introduced May 20, 2025; referred to Judiciary; 32 cosponsors) — code within 180 days, complaint procedure with a five-judge panel, published recusal explanations (per the sponsor's release cited earlier in this memo). Alternative: amend 28 U.S.C. § 351(d)(1) to add "justice." Either would need a no-self-judging structure to meet Section 3's text.
- *Section 4 independent processes.* No bill on file builds all three; components exist — statutory footing for the Office of Congressional Ethics (CLEAN Act, cited above), the SCERT panel for the Court, and, for the President, a designated civil enforcer with standing (the Brennan Center presidential-ethics proposal, cited above). Without a statute, Section 4 leaves the choice of machinery to each branch subject to judicial review.
- *Section 5.* Existing chapter 131 largely satisfies it; Congress would likely revisit § 13106 penalties and could not repeal online posting or reporting for covered officers.
- *Technical conforming.* Strike "the President, the Vice President," from § 202(c); OGE amends 5 C.F.R. § 2635.102(h).

**(e) State trigger or conforming legislation.** None is plausible for this amendment: its covered class is federal, and states have no power to regulate the in-office conduct of federal officers, so there is nothing for a state to pre-enact contingent on ratification. States participate through ratification only. No actual example exists for this subject; do not cite one.

**(f) Litigation questions the wording leaves to the courts.**
1. *Scope of "whose value their official conduct may affect."* For a President, arguably every listed security; for a district judge, few. Courts will decide whether the phrase narrows the ban office-by-office or reads as a general description of the covered asset class. Treasury securities are not expressly exempted in the text (S. 1498 exempts them by statute).
2. *"As Congress shall by law provide" (Section 1).* Whether existing definitions satisfy it; whether Congress may abolish the blind-trust route (as S. 1498 would); whether an officer may rely on a diversified fund with no statute at all.
3. *Spouses and dependents.* Section 1 binds "no person holding the office" — spouses and dependent children are not covered by the text; every pending bill covers them by statute. Whether a statute may extend the constitutional ban to family, and whether family holdings can be attributed to the officer, will be litigated.
4. *Who enforces Sections 1, 2, and 4, and with what standing.* No enforcement clause, no designated enforcer, no remedy for a Section 1 violation (fine? forfeiture? disqualification?). The Emoluments Clause suits (dismissed on standing and mootness, above) are the cautionary precedent; *Raines v. Byrd*-type legislator-standing limits apply.
5. *Section 2 and presidential immunity.* Whether divestment and disclosure duties are enforceable against a sitting President in a civil action; whether any criminal enforcement is barred as to official acts under *Trump v. United States*, or deferred until after the term.
6. *Section 3 sanctions vs. Article III and impeachment.* The text authorizes a "process" but names no sanction; whether removal remains exclusively by impeachment (Article II, § 4; Article III "good Behaviour") or a statutory process may impose lesser sanctions on a justice; whether "no judge shall finally judge their own cause" is met by a panel of other Article III judges (the state model) or requires a non-judicial body.
7. *Section 4 vs. Article I, § 5 and Speech or Debate.* Whether an independent process may compel a chamber to act on its findings; whether *Helstoski* still bars use of legislative-act evidence in an ethics proceeding when the later-in-time amendment requires independent enforcement.
8. *"Covered officers" (Sections 4 and 5).* The term is not defined in the draft; presumably the Section 1 class, but courts (or Congress by law) will have to say so.
9. *Retroactivity.* No effective date and no grandfather clause; the general rule (RATIFICATION_EFFECTS_RESEARCH.md § 2) is that silence is resolved by the courts, so a sitting officer's pre-ratification holdings are covered from certification unless a court reads in a reasonable compliance period.
