# Campaign Finance Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

Research supporting Target Category 1 (Campaign Finance) in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md). Compiled 2026-08-11. Companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md) (public-opinion evidence). Doctrine, bill-history, state-model, and international research from primary sources linked throughout; advocacy-organization sources are flagged as such.

**The headline findings:**

1. Unlike privacy, campaign finance has **a real failed amendment vote to learn from**: the Democracy For All Amendment (S.J.Res. 19) got a Senate floor vote on September 11, 2014 and failed cloture **54–42 on straight party lines** — every Democrat yes, every Republican no. Yet today the *public* is not partisan on this: spending limits poll at 72% (71% R / 76% D, Pew 2023), an amendment at 71–79%, dark-money disclosure at 85% (85% R / 88% D). The strategic fact of this category is the gap between elite party-line behavior and full-spectrum public consensus — and that gap is now closing at the state level (Oklahoma and Idaho became the 24th and 25th states to call for an amendment in spring 2026, and a **Republican** introduced the aligned H.J.Res. 191 in June 2026).
2. **"Overturn Citizens United" is the slogan, but *Buckley* is the target.** *Citizens United* (2010) only governs corporate independent expenditures. The rule that no one — individual, candidate, or group — may be subject to *spending* limits comes from *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976), and super PACs come from *SpeechNow* (D.C. Cir. 2010). An amendment that touches only corporate money changes little; the load-bearing text must reach the expenditure side of *Buckley*'s contribution/expenditure line.
3. There are **two rival drafting camps** with very different risk profiles: the "restore authority" approach (Democracy For All, American Promise's For Our Freedom Amendment — empower Congress and the states to set reasonable limits) and the "end corporate personhood" approach (Move to Amend's We the People Amendment — strip constitutional rights from artificial entities and declare spending is not speech). The first polls full-spectrum; the second has radical spillover onto nonprofits, unions, and newspapers.
4. **The US is a genuine doctrinal outlier.** Every peer democracy treats electoral fairness/equality as a *legitimate* basis for limiting campaign money — Canada's Supreme Court calls it the "egalitarian model" (*Harper*, 2004), the UK caps party and third-party spending by statute, Germany constitutionalizes party-finance transparency, France bans corporate donations outright — while *Buckley* holds that equalizing voice is "wholly foreign to the First Amendment." Roughly 46% of countries cap candidate spending and ~70% provide public party funding ([International IDEA](https://www.idea.int/data-tools/data/political-finance-database)). The amendment's core abstraction is to make political equality a constitutionally *permissible* purpose, as it is everywhere else.
5. **Disclosure is the 85% floor and the safest constitutional ground** — *Citizens United* itself upheld disclosure 8–1 — while **public financing is the weak remedy (~50% support)** and belongs in statute, not constitutional text.
6. The ratification math already has proof points: Montana's I-166 passed with **75%** and Colorado's Amendment 65 with **74%** in 2012 — both instructing their delegations to pursue exactly this amendment — and 25 state legislatures (including red ones) have formally called on Congress to act.

## Amendment Framework — What a Campaign Finance Amendment Might Cover

A constitutional amendment on money in politics would restore to Congress and the states the authority the Supreme Court removed in *Buckley*, *Citizens United*, *SpeechNow*, and *McCutcheon*: the power to set reasonable, enforceable limits on the raising and spending of money to influence elections, and to require full disclosure of who is spending it. Its constitutional work is narrow and structural — it does not prescribe a campaign finance system; it re-opens the legislative space every other democracy already occupies. ([CRS RL30669 — Buckley and its progeny](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL30669.html), [Cornell LII — Citizens United](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/citizens_united_v._federal_election_commission_(2010)))

### Core provisions

1. **Authority to limit contributions *and* expenditures** — the direct answer to *Buckley*'s expenditure holding and *SpeechNow*'s super-PAC corollary. *Tested models:* S.J.Res. 19 §1 ("Congress and the States may regulate and set reasonable limits on the raising and spending of money by candidates and others to influence elections" — [as reported, GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-113sjres19rs/html/BILLS-113sjres19rs.htm)); American Promise's For Our Freedom text ("Nothing in this Constitution shall be construed to forbid Congress or the States… from reasonably regulating and limiting contributions and spending in campaigns, elections, or ballot measures" — [American Promise](https://americanpromise.net/), advocacy source). Polling: 72% for spending limits (71 R / 76 D, Pew 2023 — see [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)).
2. **A named, legitimate purpose: political equality / electoral integrity** — the clause that overrules *Buckley*'s rationale rather than just its result. S.J.Res. 19 opened with exactly this move: "To advance democratic self-government and political equality, and to protect the integrity of government and the electoral process…" This is the internationally settled ground (*Harper v. Canada*, 2004; *Bowman v. UK*, 1998 — see International Models).
3. **Disclosure of election spending** — the 85%-support provision (85 R / 88 D) and the one already blessed 8–1 by the *Citizens United* Court itself; the DISCLOSE Act has passed the House and died by Senate filibuster repeatedly since 2010 ([Durbin release on the 2022 filibuster](https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/senate-republicans-filibuster-disclose-act)). *Tested model:* Montana's 2015 disclosure law, upheld in federal court ([The Fulcrum](https://thefulcrum.us/electoral-reforms/montana-dark-money)).
4. **Power to distinguish natural persons from artificial entities** — the scoped version of the corporate question: not "corporations have no rights," but Congress and the states *may* treat corporate and human election spending differently (restoring *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* (1990) as a permissible legislative choice). *Tested model:* S.J.Res. 19 §2 ("may distinguish between natural persons and corporations or other artificial entities created by law, including by prohibiting such entities from spending money to influence elections").
5. **A press savings clause** — S.J.Res. 19 §3: "Nothing in this article shall be construed to grant Congress or the States the power to abridge the freedom of the press." Non-optional for ratification politics (see traps below).

### Challenges in drafting and adoption

- **The partisan-history problem.** The only floor vote ever held (2014) was pure party line, and the reintroductions (H.J.Res. 2 in the 116th, H.J.Res. 1 in the 117th, Schiff's 118th version) have been overwhelmingly Democratic vehicles — Rep. John Katko (R-NY) was for years the lone Republican cosponsor ([Raskin release, 2021](https://raskin.house.gov/2021/1/raskin-cosponsors-bipartisan-constitutional-amendment-overturn-citizens-united)). The countervailing facts: bipartisan polling, 25 state resolutions including deep-red states, and Rep. Tom Barrett (R-MI)'s [H.J.Res. 191](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/191/text) (June 2026) — the first Republican-introduced version. The category passes the full-spectrum test with *voters*; the work is converting that into elite permission.
- **The civil-liberties split on the left.** The ACLU itself opposed S.J.Res. 19, warning it would "empower Congress and the states to regulate even non-partisan political debate" — while a group of *former* ACLU leaders wrote the Senate that "the ACLU is mistaken" and endorsed "generous, content neutral political spending limits" ([Forbes on the split](https://www.forbes.com/sites/danielfisher/2014/09/11/campaign-finance-amendment-drives-wedge-between-aclu-public-citizen/), [former-leaders letter, via Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/sites/default/files/analysis/Former_ACLU_Leader_Letter_2014.pdf)). Amendment text must be drafted to answer the ACLU's objection, not dismiss it.
- **The incumbent-protection trap.** Spending limits are set by incumbents, and low limits systematically favor incumbents (name recognition is free; challengers buy theirs). This was a core *Buckley*-era objection and remains the strongest good-faith conservative argument. Text needs "reasonable" limits plus preserved judicial review (see Candidates below).
- **The press-exemption problem.** Media corporations spend money on election-relevant speech constantly. Any power to limit "corporate" spending must not reach editorials, news coverage, or documentaries — but a press exemption written too broadly becomes the loophole (every billionaire buys a "news outlet"), and written too narrowly it licenses regulating journalism.
- **The corporate-personhood trap.** "Corporations aren't people" text (We the People Amendment §1) strips *all* constitutional rights from *all* artificial entities — which on its face reaches the NAACP, churches, unions, the New York Times Company, and Planned Parenthood. It converts a 79%-consensus issue into a radical restructuring fight.
- **High political bar.** Article V requires two-thirds of both houses and three-fourths of states. Mitigants: 25 states on record, 74–75% state ballot results, and single-digit partisan polling gaps.

### Strategic read

Constitutionalize **authority and purpose, not policy**: (1) political equality and electoral integrity as legitimate constitutional purposes (the *Buckley* rationale-overrule), (2) power to set reasonable limits on raising and spending, (3) a disclosure principle, (4) power to distinguish artificial entities *in election spending only*, (5) a press savings clause and judicial-review preservation. Leave every dollar figure, the public-financing question (the ~50% weak remedy), and enforcement architecture to statute. Follow the "restore authority" camp, not the "corporate personhood" camp — the American Promise model is the one actually collecting red-state signatures. **Sequencing:** the state-resolution campaign is this category's equivalent of the privacy doc's Brazil lesson — the amendment should ratify a consensus the states are already formalizing; near-term work (state resolutions, ballot initiatives like Montana's I-194, disclosure statutes) is stage-setting for ratification.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

### The doctrine an amendment must overturn or permit

- ***Buckley v. Valeo* (1976)** — the foundation. Upheld *contribution* limits (a contribution is "a general expression of support" — marginal speech interest, real corruption interest) but struck all *expenditure* limits: because "virtually every means of communicating ideas in today's mass society requires the expenditure of money," spending caps restrict "the quantity and diversity of political speech." The line an amendment must erase is *Buckley*'s rationale: "the concept that government may restrict the speech of some elements of our society in order to enhance the relative voice of others is wholly foreign to the First Amendment." ([Brennan Center doctrinal chapter](https://www.brennancenter.org/sites/default/files/legacy/d/ch.1%202004.final.pdf) — advocacy-adjacent; [Briffault, *Buckley's Dubious yet Durable Contribution-Expenditure Distinction*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5018463); [CRS RL30669](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL30669.html))
- ***Austin v. Michigan Chamber* (1990) and *McConnell v. FEC* (2003)** — the overruled cases an amendment would re-permit. *Austin* upheld bans on corporate treasury spending under an "anti-distortion" rationale (the corrosive effect of "immense aggregations of wealth… that have little or no correlation to the public's support for the corporation's political ideas"); *McConnell* upheld BCRA's electioneering-communication limits. Both were the law until 2010.
- ***Citizens United v. FEC* (2010)** — overruled *Austin* and the relevant part of *McConnell*: "the Government may not suppress political speech on the basis of the speaker's corporate identity," and independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." Critically, it **upheld disclosure and disclaimer requirements 8–1**. ([Justia, 558 U.S. 310](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/558/310/), [FEC case page](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/court-cases/citizens-united-v-fec/), [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/citizens_united_v._federal_election_commission_(2010)))
- ***SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (D.C. Cir. 2010, en banc)** — the case that actually created super PACs: if independent spending can't corrupt (*Citizens United*), then *contributions to* independent-expenditure-only committees can't be limited either. Unlimited money into super PACs follows. ([MTSU First Amendment Encyclopedia on the line of cases](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/mccutcheon-v-fec/))
- ***McCutcheon v. FEC* (2014)** — struck aggregate contribution limits 5–4; Roberts' controlling opinion narrowed the only cognizable government interest to "*quid pro quo* corruption" — outright vote-buying — excluding influence, access, and gratitude. ([Justia, 572 U.S. 185](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/572/185/), [Brennan Center case page](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/court-cases/mccutcheon-v-fec) — advocacy-adjacent)
- ***American Tradition Partnership v. Bullock* (2012)** — the per curiam summary reversal that slammed the state-level door: Montana's century-old Corrupt Practices Act ban on corporate political spending was struck without briefing or argument because "there can be no serious doubt" *Citizens United* controls. States cannot experiment their way around this doctrine; only an amendment reaches it. ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/11-1179), [FEC](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/court-cases/american-tradition-partnership-inc-v-bullock/))
- **The through-line for drafters:** the Court closed, in sequence, expenditure limits (*Buckley*), corporate-spending bans (*Citizens United*), super-PAC contribution limits (*SpeechNow*), aggregate limits (*McCutcheon*), and state-level alternatives (*ATP v. Bullock*), while narrowing "corruption" to quid pro quo. An amendment must either restore legislative authority across that whole sequence or re-broaden the constitutionally cognizable purposes (equality, integrity, anti-dependence) — the two drafting strategies below.

### Prior Amendment Proposals (the real vote history)

**The Democracy For All Amendment — S.J.Res. 19 (113th), Udall (D-NM).** The only money-in-politics amendment ever to receive a Senate floor vote. Text as reported ([GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-113sjres19rs/html/BILLS-113sjres19rs.htm)):

> *Section 1.* To advance democratic self-government and political equality, and to protect the integrity of government and the electoral process, Congress and the States may regulate and set reasonable limits on the raising and spending of money by candidates and others to influence elections.
> *Section 2.* Congress and the States shall have power to implement and enforce this article by appropriate legislation, and may distinguish between natural persons and corporations or other artificial entities created by law, including by prohibiting such entities from spending money to influence elections.
> *Section 3.* Nothing in this article shall be construed to grant Congress or the States the power to abridge the freedom of the press.

- **The vote:** cloture failed **54–42 on September 11, 2014** (60 needed; roll call 261) — every yes a Democrat or Democratic-caucusing independent, every no a Republican. ([LCV roll-call page](https://www.lcv.org/roll-call-vote/democracy-for-all-constitutional-amendment/) — advocacy source; [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/113th-congress/senate-joint-resolution/19/text); [Congressional Record, Sept. 10, 2014](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2014-09-10/html/CREC-2014-09-10-pt1-PgS5468-3.htm))
- **Reintroductions:** H.J.Res. 2 (116th, [text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/house-joint-resolution/2/text)) and H.J.Res. 1 (117th) by Deutch/McGovern/Raskin + Katko (R-NY); S.J.Res. 51 (116th) by Udall with all Senate Democrats ([Warner release](https://www.warner.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/2019/7/udall-shaheen-all-senate-democrats-introduce-constitutional-amendment-to-overturn-citizens-united)); Schiff-led in the 118th ([Schiff summary](https://schiff.house.gov/download/democracy-for-all-amendment), [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/news/public-citizen-applauds-reintroduction-of-democracy-for-all-amendment/) — advocacy source).
- **The analytical point:** the 2014 party-line vote predates today's polling landscape. Pew's 2023 finding (72% for spending limits, 71 R / 76 D), the Ipsos/American Promise 2025 finding (71% for the amendment itself, 72 R / 76 D — advocacy-commissioned), and Brennan's 2026 finding (79%, 75 R / 84 D — advocacy-adjacent) all show the *electorate* never split the way the Senate did. The 2014 vote measured elite polarization under McConnell-era whipping ("a fundraising-season stunt" was the GOP frame), not public opinion. The state-level record since — unanimous or near-unanimous resolution votes in Oklahoma, Idaho, and Missouri's House — is the better predictor of ratification behavior.

**The We the People Amendment — H.J.Res. 48 (115th–118th), now H.J.Res. 54 (119th), Jayapal (D-WA); originally Nolan/Deutch.** The Move to Amend version: "the rights protected and extended by the Constitution are the rights of **natural persons only**"; artificial entities "shall have no rights under this Constitution"; and the judiciary shall not construe "the spending of money to influence elections to be speech under the First Amendment." Peaked around 90 House cosponsors; never received committee action. ([Congress.gov H.J.Res. 54 text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/54/text); [Move to Amend](https://www.movetoamend.org/) — advocacy source; [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Move_to_Amend))

**The two drafting approaches compared.** "Regulate money" (S.J.Res. 19 / For Our Freedom) is *enabling* — it changes who decides, restores pre-2010 legislative space, and leaves policy to politics; its risk is that it guarantees nothing by itself. "End corporate personhood" (We the People) is *self-executing* and emotionally resonant ("corporations aren't people" is why Montana's I-166 hit 75%) — but its text denies constitutional rights to *every* incorporated entity: newspapers (press-clause chaos), churches (free exercise), the ACLU and NAACP (association), unions. It also strips due-process and takings protections from businesses, which reads as expropriation-enabling to the right and dooms the full-spectrum test at the elite level even while polling well as a slogan. The foundation's frame should be the enabling model with a narrow, election-spending-only artificial-entity clause.

**The For Our Freedom Amendment (American Promise) — H.J.Res. 191 (119th), Barrett (R-MI), June 2026.** The first Republican-introduced version: Congress and the States "shall have certain authority to regulate and limit contributions and spending in campaigns for elections for public office… and ballot initiatives and referendums," framed by its sponsor as "protecting free speech and making clear the legal distinction between people and artificial entities." ([Congress.gov text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/191/text); [American Promise](https://americanpromise.net/) — advocacy source)

### The statutory graveyard (context, not targets)

The **DISCLOSE Act** (Van Hollen/Whitehouse) — donor disclosure above $10,000 for super PACs and 501(c)(4)s — passed the House in 2010 and has been filibustered in the Senate in 2010, 2012, and 2022 ([Whitehouse](https://www.whitehouse.senate.gov/news/release/senate-tees-up-vote-on-whitehouses-disclose-act-to-help-restore-americans-faith-in-democracy/), [Durbin](https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/senate-republicans-filibuster-disclose-act)). The pattern mirrors the privacy bills: 85% public consensus, zero Senate throughput — evidence that the blocking problem is institutional, which is precisely the case for a constitutional (rather than statutory) fix, and simultaneously a warning about the two-thirds bar.

## State Models

### Montana — the full arc, and the best single state story

- **1912:** voters pass the Corrupt Practices Act by initiative, banning corporate political spending, in direct response to the Copper Kings' purchase of the legislature (W.A. Clark's bribed Senate seat). The law stood for 98 years.
- **2010–2012:** Western Tradition Partnership (later American Tradition Partnership), a dark-money nonprofit, challenges the Act. The Montana Supreme Court upholds it 5–2 on a factual record of actual corruption; the US Supreme Court **summarily reverses** (*ATP v. Bullock*, June 25, 2012) — no argument, no briefing on the merits. ([Wikipedia case history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Tradition_Partnership,_Inc._v._Bullock), [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/court-cases/american-tradition-partnership-v-bullock) — advocacy-adjacent)
- **2012:** Montanans respond with **I-166**, a policy statement that "corporations are not entitled to constitutional rights because they are not human beings" and instructing the congressional delegation to pursue an amendment — it passes with **75%** in a state Romney carried by 13. Colorado's parallel Amendment 65 passes the same night with **74%**, winning every county. ([Ballotpedia I-166](https://ballotpedia.org/Montana_Corporate_Contributions_Initiative,_I-166_(2012)), [Ballotpedia Amendment 65](https://ballotpedia.org/Colorado_Amendment_65,_Federal_Campaign_Spending_and_Contribution_Amendment_Initiative_(2012)), [Reclaim Democracy](https://reclaimdemocracy.org/colorado-montana-initiatives-2012/) — advocacy source)
- **2015:** Gov. Bullock signs the Montana Disclose Act with bipartisan legislative support — any group spending $250+ on candidate-referencing ads in the final two months must register and disclose donors. Upheld by the Ninth Circuit; certiorari denied. Montana now has arguably the nation's strongest disclosure regime. ([The Fulcrum](https://thefulcrum.us/electoral-reforms/montana-dark-money), [OpenSecrets on Montana's disclosure fights](https://www.opensecrets.org/news/2018/07/montana-pols-launch-fight-against-nonprofit/))
- **2026:** the **"Montana Plan" (I-194)**, on this November's ballot, would ban corporate/artificial-entity spending and dark money in all elections in Montana — a deliberate vehicle to force the question back to the Supreme Court. Endorsers span former Gov. Marc Racicot (R), former Gov. Bullock (D), and former Sen. Tester (D). Cautionary wrinkle worth internalizing: the campaign itself has been funded largely through a 501(c)(4) that doesn't disclose its donors, drawing scrutiny from Montana's own commissioner of political practices — a live illustration that disclosure principles must bind reformers too. ([OpenSecrets, "Fifteen years after Citizens United, the Montana Plan…"](https://www.opensecrets.org/news/2025/08/fifteen-years-after-citizens-united-the-montana-plan-poses-the-latest-challenge-to-dark-money/), [Montana Free Press](https://montanafreepress.org/2026/06/11/how-unidentifiable-donors-are-funding-montanas-anti-dark-money-initiative/))

### The state resolution campaign — 25 states and counting

**25 state legislatures** have formally called on Congress to propose a money-in-politics amendment — half the country, and 13 short of the 38 needed to ratify. The recent additions are the story for the full-spectrum test: **Oklahoma (24th)** and **Idaho (25th)** passed with near-unanimous support in spring 2026; Wyoming's Senate and Arizona's and Indiana's Senates have passed resolutions awaiting their other chambers; **Missouri's House passed unanimously**. American Promise's method — recruiting in-state legislator champions rather than parachuting in, and framing the question as "who should decide campaign finance policy: elected lawmakers or judges?" — is the field-tested playbook for making this a cross-partisan structural issue rather than a progressive cause. ([Democracy Renovator, "25 States Want Congress to Act"](https://www.democracyrenovator.com/p/25-states-back-amendment-to-limit); [American Promise](https://americanpromise.net/) — advocacy source; [The Beacon on Missouri](https://thebeaconnews.org/stories/2026/02/26/missouri-bill-to-overturn-citizens-united/))

## International Models

### Canada — the egalitarian model, named and upheld

***Harper v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2004 SCC 33** — future PM Stephen Harper challenged federal third-party advertising limits (~$150k national / $3k per district). The Supreme Court upheld them 6–3, articulating the direct doctrinal opposite of *Buckley*: "The overarching objective of the spending limits is electoral fairness… This **egalitarian model of elections seeks to create a level playing field for those who wish to engage in the electoral discourse, enabling voters to be better informed**," and limits "prevent the most affluent from monopolizing election discourse." ([CanLII, 2004 SCC 33](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2004/2004scc33/2004scc33.html), [Centre for Constitutional Studies summary](https://www.constitutionalstudies.ca/2012/07/harper-v-canada-2004-third-party-election-advertising-limits-in-federal-election-campaigns/?print=print)) Canada pairs this with strict contribution rules (corporate and union contributions banned federally since 2006; individual contributions capped around $1,700) and short regulated campaign periods. **The counterweight case:** *Ontario (AG) v. Working Families Coalition*, 2025 SCC 5, struck Ontario's extension of third-party limits to a full *year* before elections as unconstitutional — even the egalitarian model polices overreach, and courts remain the check on incumbent self-dealing. ([CanLII, 2025 SCC 5](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2025/2025scc5/2025scc5.html))

### United Kingdom — comprehensive spending caps with a Strasbourg floor

The **Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000 (PPERA)** caps national party spending for the year before a general election, layered on candidate-level caps dating to 1883; non-party ("third party") campaigners face their own registration thresholds and caps, and paid political advertising on broadcast media is banned outright (upheld by the ECtHR in *Animal Defenders International v. UK*, 2013). ([legislation.gov.uk — PPERA](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/41/notes/data.xht?view=snippet&wrap=true), [Institute for Government explainer](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/election-spending-regulated-uk)) The constitutional boundary was set by ***Bowman v. United Kingdom* (ECtHR 1998)**: a £5 limit on independent expenditure about candidates was struck as "a total barrier" to Mrs. Bowman's expression — but the Court **accepted that spending limits pursue the legitimate aim of securing equality between candidates**. The PPERA regime was designed around *Bowman*: limits must be generous enough to permit meaningful participation, and then they are compatible with free expression. ([PPERA explanatory notes referencing Bowman](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/41/notes/data.xht?view=snippet&wrap=true), [Transparency International UK](https://www.transparency.org.uk/news/what-do-political-parties-politicians-and-political-campaigners-spend-money))

### Germany — constitutionalized transparency and equal opportunity

Germany writes party finance into the constitution itself. **Basic Law Article 21(1)**: parties "must publicly account for the sources and use of their funds and for their assets" — donor transparency is *constitutional text*, not statute. The Bundesverfassungsgericht polices the system through the principle of ***Chancengleichheit*** (equal opportunity of parties): public funding is substantial but tied to demonstrated public support (votes, membership dues, matched small donations) and capped by an "absolute limit" the Court itself enforces — in 2023 it voided a €190M funding increase as unconstitutional. Germany has no campaign spending caps, but the combination of constitutional transparency, party-centered (not candidate-centered) competition, and free broadcast time makes American-scale spending structurally pointless. ([BMI — Funding of Political Parties](https://www.bmi.bund.de/EN/topics/constitution/law-political-parties/funding-pol-parties/funding-pol-parties-node.html), [BVerfG press release, 2023 absolute-limit ruling](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/EN/2023/bvg23-009.html))

### France — the hard-limits regime

France bans what the US constitutionalizes: **corporate donations to candidates and parties have been prohibited outright since January 19, 1995**; individual contributions are capped (€7,500/year to parties, €4,600 per election to candidates); campaign spending is capped per constituency; paid broadcast political advertising is banned; and candidates winning ≥5% of the first-round vote are **reimbursed up to 47.5% of capped spending** by the state, contingent on a clean audit by the independent **CNCCFP** (Commission nationale des comptes de campagne et des financements politiques). Enforcement is real — a former President (Sarkozy) was criminally convicted over the 2012 Bygmalion overspending scheme. The French Constitutional Council has never treated spending caps as a speech violation. ([Library of Congress — France election-finance regulation](https://maint.loc.gov/law/help/elections/foreign-involvement/france.php), [France 24 explainer](https://www.france24.com/en/20100706-french-parties-politicians-funded-france-government-legislation), [INET, "Even in France, Money Rules Politics"](https://www.ineteconomics.org/perspectives/blog/even-in-france-money-rules-politics))

### The global picture — the US as doctrinal outlier

[International IDEA's Political Finance Database](https://www.idea.int/data-tools/data/political-finance-database) (all ~180 tracked countries): **over 46% limit candidate spending, ~32% limit party spending, and nearly 70% provide direct public funding to parties** — with limits most common among established European democracies. The [OSCE/ODIHR–Venice Commission Guidelines on Political Party Regulation](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/?pdf=CDL-AD(2020)032-e) treat spending limits, contribution limits, and disclosure as internationally accepted tools for protecting "equality of opportunity" in elections, compatible with free-expression guarantees when proportionate. The comparative bottom line for the foundation's argument: **every peer democracy holds that electoral fairness is a legitimate — often constitutionally mandated — basis for limiting campaign money; the United States alone holds that it is constitutionally forbidden.** The amendment does not import any country's system; it removes the uniquely American prohibition on having one. ([IPU comparative overview](https://www.ipu.org/file/6115/download))

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Name the purpose; overrule the rationale, not just the case.** *Harper* works because Canadian law openly embraces "electoral fairness"; *Buckley*'s "wholly foreign" line is the root of everything downstream. Amendment text that declares political equality and electoral integrity to be legitimate constitutional purposes (S.J.Res. 19 §1's opening clause) does more work than any case-specific reversal — courts rebuild doctrine from purposes.
2. **Limits must be generous, viewpoint-neutral, and judicially reviewable — or courts strike them, everywhere.** *Bowman* (a £5 cap is "a total barrier") and *Working Families* (a one-year Ontario window is too long) show even fairness-friendly courts police overreach. This is the built-in answer to the incumbent-protection trap: constitutionalize "reasonable" limits and preserve review, and courts do abroad exactly what skeptics fear they couldn't do here.
3. **Regulate amounts and channels, not speakers and content.** The UK caps what anyone may *spend*; France caps and reimburses; nobody licenses who may *speak*. The "restore authority to limit" frame survives international scrutiny; the "strip entities of rights" frame has no successful international analog.
4. **Disclosure is the universal, least-litigated baseline — Germany put it in the constitution itself.** Art. 21's "must publicly account" clause has operated for 75 years without a free-speech crisis, and matches the US's own 85% consensus and the 8–1 *Citizens United* holding. Transparency is the amendment's safest constitutional-text candidate.
5. **Public funding is machinery, not principle.** Germany's court polices funding *amounts*; France's reimbursement runs on audit mechanics; both systems revise constantly. With US support at only ~50%, public financing belongs in the "inherently statutory" bin — the amendment should merely not forbid it.
6. **Time-bound regulated periods make limits administrable.** UK and Canadian caps attach to defined pre-election windows, which is how they coexist with unrestricted everyday political speech. US drafting should leave window-setting to statute but the lesson kills the strawman that limits mean permanent speech rationing.
7. **Independent enforcement matters more than rule stringency — but design it in statute.** The CNCCFP, the UK Electoral Commission, and Germany's Bundestag-audit system all outperform the deadlock-by-design FEC; none is constitutionally specified. Constitutionalize the *duty* of disclosure and the *power* to limit; leave the agency's architecture to Congress.
8. **The press line every regime draws: paid placement vs. editorial content.** The UK bans *paid* broadcast political ads while newspapers editorialize freely (*Animal Defenders International v. UK*, 2013); no democracy treats news coverage as a campaign expenditure. This is the workable formulation of the press exemption: money spent to *purchase advertising or distribution* is regulable; journalism, commentary, and editorial content are not.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. All five below clear it — limits authority polls 71–72% with Republican majorities, disclosure at 85R/88D, the artificial-entity distinction won 75% on the ballot in red Montana, and the purpose and press clauses are structural components of the polled package rather than separate asks.*

1. **Restored authority to set reasonable limits** — the core enabling clause, and the 71–79% polling consensus. Proposed formulation: *"To secure political equality, the integrity of government, and free and fair elections, Congress and the States may regulate and set reasonable, viewpoint-neutral limits on the raising and spending of money to influence elections, including contributions to candidates and expenditures made in their support or opposition."*
   - **Why "and others… in their support or opposition":** reaching independent expenditures by name is what closes the *SpeechNow* super-PAC channel; text that stops at "contributions" or at "corporations" leaves the largest money flows untouched. "Viewpoint-neutral" is in the text, not just doctrine — it answers the ACLU objection directly.
   - ⚠️ **The incumbent-protection trap:** spending limits are written by incumbents, and low limits entrench them — challengers must buy the name recognition incumbents get free. "Reasonable" plus preserved judicial review is the mitigation, and it is field-tested: *Bowman* struck a £5 cap as a "total barrier" and *Working Families* (SCC 2025) struck an overlong regulated window, proving courts under fairness-friendly constitutions still invalidate self-dealing limits. Drafters should resist any urge to constitutionalize dollar figures — frozen numbers are how a protective clause becomes an incumbency machine.
2. **Political equality as a legitimate constitutional purpose** — the rationale-level overrule of *Buckley*'s "wholly foreign" sentence and *McCutcheon*'s quid-pro-quo-only rule. Proposed formulation: *"The equal opportunity of citizens to participate in and to influence elections, and the independence of government from concentrated wealth, are hereby declared to be compelling interests of the United States and of the several States, in furtherance of which laws may be enacted under this article."*
   - **Drafting note:** the sentence is built in operative amendment register — "are hereby declared" (cf. the 21st Amendment's "is hereby repealed") makes the declaration self-executing rather than aspirational, and "in furtherance of which laws may be enacted under this article" tethers the purpose to candidate 1's enabling clause so it can never operate as a freestanding power over speech.
   - **The comparative warrant:** this sentence states what *Harper* ("egalitarian model… level playing field"), *Bowman* ("equality between candidates" as a legitimate aim), German *Chancengleichheit*, and the [Venice Commission guidelines](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/?pdf=CDL-AD(2020)032-e) already hold — it imports the settled international norm rather than inventing one. "Dependence" language also captures the corruption the Roberts Court defined away: officeholders beholden to donors short of outright bribery.
   - ⚠️ **The leveling trap:** an unbounded equality rationale could justify muzzling *anyone* who speaks "too effectively" — including authors, celebrities, and newspapers — which is the legitimate kernel of *Buckley*'s worry. The purpose clause must operate only through candidate 1's "reasonable, viewpoint-neutral limits on the raising and spending of money," never as a freestanding power over speech itself; pairing it textually with the press clause (candidate 5) is what keeps it caged.
3. **The disclosure principle** — the 85% (85 R / 88 D) provision, already constitutional under *Citizens United*'s 8–1 holding but statutorily filibustered since 2010. Proposed formulation: *"The people shall have the right to know, in a timely manner, the source of substantial contributions and expenditures made to influence elections or the appointment of public officials, as provided by law."*
   - **Why constitutionalize something already permitted:** permission has not produced disclosure — the DISCLOSE Act has died by filibuster three times while 501(c)(4) dark money flows around FEC non-enforcement. Framing it as a *right of the people to know* (the Montana 2015 model, court-upheld) converts transparency from a regulatory option into an enforceable default, and it is the provision red-state legislatures pass most readily.
   - ⚠️ **The donor-privacy trap:** compelled disclosure has its own constitutional counter-tradition — *NAACP v. Alabama* (1958) protected membership lists from hostile officials, and *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* ([2021](https://www.oyez.org/cases/2020/19-251)) struck California's blanket donor-disclosure rule on association grounds; small donors to controversial causes face real harassment risk. "Substantial" and "as provided by law" keep thresholds, exemptions for threatened groups, and timing in statute — the amendment guarantees sunlight on big money, not a registry of every $25 giver.
4. **Power to distinguish natural persons from artificial entities — in election spending only.** Proposed formulation: *"In the regulation of contributions and expenditures to influence elections, Congress and the States may distinguish between natural persons and corporations or other artificial entities created by law."*
   - **The scoping decision is the whole game:** this restores *Austin* as a permissible legislative choice (a state *may* bar corporate treasury spending, as Montana did for 98 years) without adjudicating corporate constitutional status generally. It is S.J.Res. 19 §2's "may distinguish" rather than We the People's "shall have no rights" — enabling, not self-executing.
   - ⚠️ **The corporate-personhood trap:** broad "corporations aren't people" text has radical spillover — it strips First, Fourth, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendment protections from nonprofits (the NAACP, churches), unions, and *newspapers* (every major press organ is a corporation), and removes due-process/takings protection from all businesses. That draft fails the full-spectrum test at the elite level no matter how well the slogan polls; the election-spending-only scope plus the press clause is what keeps candidate 4 from detonating candidates 1–3.
5. **Press savings and enforcement clause.** Proposed formulation: *"Nothing in this article shall be construed to grant any power to abridge the freedom of speech or of the press, including news reporting, commentary, and editorial content, regardless of the speaker's form of organization. Congress and the States shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."*
   - **The functional press line (Int'l Lesson 8):** protecting press *activity* ("news reporting, commentary, and editorial content… regardless of… form of organization") rather than press *entities* adopts the line every peer regime draws — paid ad placement is regulable spending; journalism is not — and it protects the lone pamphleteer and the incorporated newspaper equally.
   - ⚠️ **The press-exemption problem:** any press carve-out invites arbitrage — a billionaire converts his super PAC into a "news channel"; a platform calls its paid promotions "commentary." Courts already police an analogous line under FECA's statutory media exemption, and the functional framing gives them the right question (is this bona fide journalism or purchased electioneering?) rather than the wrong one (is this speaker a Media Company?). No drafting fully dissolves this trap; it is managed, not solved — which is a reason to keep the exemption's detailed contours statutory.

**The package skeleton.** Read together, the five candidates form a complete amendment architecture: a named constitutional purpose that re-legitimizes what every peer democracy already permits (2), restored authority over both contributions and expenditures with reasonableness and neutrality built into the text (1), a people's right to disclosure of substantial election money (3), a narrowly scoped power to treat artificial entities differently in election spending (4), and a functional press-and-speech savings clause plus congressional enforcement power (5) — with all dollar figures, mechanisms, and institutional design deliberately pushed down to legislation. It is the Democracy For All chassis, re-armored against the three attacks that actually landed on S.J.Res. 19 (ACLU speech objection, incumbent-protection objection, press objection) and stripped of the corporate-personhood spillover.

**Inherently statutory (leave out of the amendment):** contribution and expenditure dollar amounts and indexing; disclosure thresholds, timing, and harassment exemptions; public financing and small-donor matching design (the ~50% weak remedy — the amendment need only permit it); regulated-period lengths; coordination and "independence" definitions; foreign-money enforcement mechanics; the enforcement agency's structure (FEC redesign vs. successor body); ballot-measure-specific rules; penalties and audit machinery.

### Extends: The doctrine an amendment must overturn or permit

- ***NRSC v. FEC* (June 30, 2026) — the newest link in the through-line, and it lands after this memo was compiled.** In a 6–3 opinion by Justice Kavanaugh the Court struck FECA's limits on *coordinated* party expenditures (52 U.S.C. § 30116(d)) and expressly overruled *FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee* (*Colorado II*, 2001). The majority reaffirmed that preventing *quid pro quo* corruption is the only permissible interest, said it had "squarely rejected undue influence as a permissible basis," and held that base contribution limits, earmarking rules, and disclosure already serve the anti-circumvention interest "without unduly restricting core political party speech." Base limits on party-to-candidate contributions survive; the narrow holding is expected to invite challenges to other contribution limits. For drafters: the sequence in the memo's "through-line" now reads *Buckley* → *Citizens United* → *SpeechNow* → *McCutcheon* → *ATP v. Bullock* → *Cruz* → *NRSC*; candidate 1's "raising and spending" language already reaches coordinated party spending, but the case is fresh evidence that the doctrine keeps moving and that only rationale-level text (candidate 2) stops the drift. ([FEC Record summary](https://www.fec.gov/updates/supreme-court-finds-limits-on-coordinated-party-expenditures-unconstitutional-in-nrsc-v-fec-609-us-____2026/); [National Law Review analysis](https://natlawreview.com/article/supreme-court-strikes-down-federal-limits-coordinated-party-spending-what-nrsc-v); [Skadden client note](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/07/nrsc-v-fec))
- ***FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate* (May 16, 2022)** — struck BCRA §304's $250,000 cap on using post-election contributions to repay a candidate's personal loans (6–3, Roberts). Notable for the memo because the Court restated that "prevention of 'quid pro quo' corruption (or its appearance) is the only permissible ground for restricting" electoral speech and rejected the government's evidence that post-election donations enriching the candidate personally raise a corruption risk — i.e., *McCutcheon*'s narrowing is now applied even where the money goes into the officeholder's pocket. ([FEC case page](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/court-cases/ted-cruz-for-senate-et-al-v-fec); [CRS LSB10796](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10796))
- ***Randall v. Sorrell* (June 26, 2006)** — the case that proves the memo's incumbent-protection trap is judicially recognized, not hypothetical. A 6–3 Court (Breyer plurality) struck Vermont's Act 64 expenditure limits under *Buckley* **and** its contribution limits ($200–$400) as disproportionately low, citing five factors: they would "significantly restrict the amount of funding available for challengers to run competitive campaigns"; they applied identically to parties, burdening "the right to associate in a political party"; they counted volunteer expenses; they were not inflation-indexed ("limits already suspiciously low will almost inevitably become too low over time"); and the state offered no special justification. This is the domestic analog to *Bowman* and *Working Families* cited in International Lesson 2 — courts here already strike self-dealing limits — and it is the strongest reason to keep "reasonable" in candidate 1's text and every dollar figure out of it. ([Cornell LII syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-1528.ZS.html))
- ***Arizona Free Enterprise Club's Freedom Club PAC v. Bennett* (June 27, 2011)** — 5–4 (Roberts), struck the "matching funds" trigger in Arizona's Clean Elections public-financing system, under which a publicly financed candidate received extra state money when a privately financed opponent or independent group outspent them. The Court repeated that it "has repeatedly rejected the argument that the government has a compelling state interest in 'leveling the playing field.'" Bears on two places in the memo: it is another expression of the *Buckley* rationale candidate 2 would overrule, and it means that even the "inherently statutory" public-financing designs are constrained by current doctrine — a purpose clause naming political equality is what would re-open matching-fund designs, not just spending limits. ([Cornell LII syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/10-238.ZS.html))
- ***Bluman v. FEC* (D.D.C. 2011, summarily aff'd Jan. 9, 2012) and the foreign-money gap.** A three-judge district court upheld the ban on foreign nationals contributing or spending in US elections: "It is fundamental to the definition of our national political community that foreign citizens do not have a constitutional right to participate in… activities of democratic self-government," and the government has "a compelling interest… preventing foreign influence over the U.S. political process." The gap the memo does not name: *Citizens United* expressly declined to decide the question — "We need not reach the question whether the Government has a compelling interest in preventing foreign individuals or associations from influencing our Nation's political process" — so US-incorporated subsidiaries and 501(c)(4)s with foreign owners or donors sit in unsettled territory, and *Bluman* by its terms does not reach foreign-controlled *domestic* corporations. See the foreign-money entries under State Models and Polling below; **this bears on the gate check** (discussed there). ([FEC case page for *Bluman*](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/court-cases/bluman-v-fec/); [*Citizens United* opinion, Part III.B.4, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/08-205.ZO.html))

### Extends: Prior Amendment Proposals (the real vote history)

- **Justice John Paul Stevens's proposed text (2014).** In *Six Amendments: How and Why We Should Change the Constitution*, the *Citizens United* dissenter offered a one-sentence amendment: "Neither the First Amendment nor any other provision of this Constitution shall be construed to prohibit the Congress or any state from imposing reasonable limits on the amount of money that candidates for public office, or their supporters, may spend in election campaigns." It is the leanest "restore authority" formulation on record — reasonableness built in, expenditure side reached ("may spend"), no artificial-entity or purpose clause — and a useful comparator for candidate 1: it shows what the enabling clause looks like stripped to the minimum, and by omission why the memo adds a purpose clause and a press clause. ([Josh Blackman, quoting the text](https://joshblackman.com/blog/2014/03/04/what-are-justice-stevenss-proposed-six-amendments/); [NPR on the book](https://www.npr.org/2014/04/26/306837618/justice-stevens-six-little-ways-to-change-the-constitution))

### Extends: Challenges in drafting and adoption

- **The "hydraulics" counterargument.** Samuel Issacharoff & Pamela S. Karlan, *The Hydraulics of Campaign Finance Reform*, 77 Tex. L. Rev. 1705 (1999), is the canonical scholarly statement of the strongest structural objection to the "restore authority" model: political money, dammed in one channel, flows to the next — from candidates and parties to less accountable outside groups, issue advocacy, and (after any press exemption) media-shaped vehicles. The memo's own history is partly a hydraulics story (BCRA's soft-money ban → 527s → super PACs → 501(c)(4)s), and the argument cuts in a specific direction for drafting: authority that reaches only some channels (e.g., corporate spending, or candidates but not "others") accelerates the flow rather than limiting it, which is the case for candidate 1's "candidates and others… in their support or opposition" language plus candidate 3's disclosure default. It is also the honest answer to "will limits work?" — the memo should present it as the good-faith skeptic's case, not dismiss it. ([Stanford Law School publication record](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/the-hydraulics-of-campaign-finance-reform/))

### Extends: The statutory graveyard (context, not targets)

- **The FEC has been without a quorum since April 2025.** Commissioner Cooksey resigned in January 2025; President Trump removed Commissioner Weintraub effective January 31, 2025; Commissioner Dickerson's departure at the end of April 2025 dropped the agency below the four votes needed to act. Through at least early 2026 the Commission had two sitting members (both Democrats), had canceled all public meetings through 2026, and could not open or resolve enforcement matters, issue advisory opinions, or adopt rules; its chair conceded "we are hobbled." Two Republican nominees (Stow and Woodson) were sent to the Senate on February 11, 2026. This is the fourth quorum loss in the agency's history and the sharpest illustration of International Lesson 7 (independent enforcement outperforms rule stringency): the disclosure and limits regime that survives *NRSC* still has no working enforcer. It reinforces the memo's choice to constitutionalize the *duty* of disclosure while leaving agency architecture to statute — a constitutional right-to-know clause (candidate 3) is enforceable by voters and courts even when the agency is dark. ([NOTUS, Sept. 26, 2025](https://www.notus.org/2026-election/federal-election-commission-fec-quorum-shut-down-trump-nominees); [NOTUS on the Feb. 11, 2026 nominations](https://www.notus.org/money/federal-election-commission-donald-trump-commissioner-nomination-quorum); [CRS R45160](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R45160))
- **A bipartisan statutory win on foreign money — H.R. 3535, the Stop Foreign Funds in Elections Act, passed the House by unanimous voice vote on July 13, 2026** (Fitzpatrick (R-PA), Golden (D-ME), Lee (D-NV)). It extends FECA's foreign-national ban — which today covers candidate elections only — to state and local ballot measures, referenda, and recalls; it now awaits the Senate. Ballotpedia counts 25 states with ballot-measure foreign-contribution bans, 30 with candidate-level prohibitions, two (New Jersey, New York) barring foreign independent expenditures, four (Alabama, Iowa, Nebraska, New Hampshire) enacting or expanding bans in 2026, and an Arizona constitutional amendment on the November 2026 ballot. Contrast with the DISCLOSE Act's three filibusters: the one slice of this category that moves through Congress on a voice vote is the foreign-money slice. ([Ballotpedia News, July 21, 2026](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/07/21/u-s-house-passes-bill-banning-foreign-contributions-to-state-and-local-ballot-measure-campaigns-2/))

### Extends: State Models

- **Maine Question 1 (Nov. 2024) — the live *SpeechNow* test case.** Voters imposed a $5,000 annual cap on contributions to independent-expenditure-only PACs (super PACs) with about **75%** support — a red-blue result on the order of Montana's I-166 and Colorado's Amendment 65. Two PACs (Dinner Table Action and For Our Future) sued in December 2024; in July 2025 a federal magistrate judge permanently enjoined the law as "unconstitutional on its face" under *SpeechNow*'s logic; Maine and Equal Citizens appealed, and the First Circuit heard argument on July 29, 2026 with the panel reportedly probing whether the cap can be squared with *Citizens United*. The measure was designed to force the Supreme Court to confront *SpeechNow* — the D.C. Circuit decision the memo identifies as the actual source of super PACs, which the Supreme Court has never reviewed. Whatever the outcome, it is the best current evidence that the "authority to limit contributions to independent-expenditure groups" piece of candidate 1 clears the full-spectrum bar at the ballot box. ([Brennan Center case page](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/dinner-table-action-v-schneider) — advocacy-adjacent; [WABI on the First Circuit argument](https://www.wabi.tv/2026/07/29/federal-appeals-court-hears-arguments-over-voter-approved-limit-maine-super-pacs/); [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Maine_Question_1,_Limit_Contributions_to_Super_PACs_Initiative_(2024)))
- **Maine Question 2 (Nov. 2023) — the highest ballot number in this category, and a cautionary tailoring lesson.** Voters barred election spending by foreign governments and by entities with ≥5% foreign-government ownership or control, in candidate *and* ballot-measure elections, by **86%–14%**. A federal district court enjoined it and the First Circuit affirmed in July 2025, holding the 5% threshold "overly broad, silencing U.S. corporations based on the mere possibility that foreign shareholders might try to influence its decisions on political speech." Two lessons for the memo: the *principle* (no foreign money in our elections) commands near-universal support; the *machinery* (ownership thresholds) is exactly what courts strike and exactly what belongs in statute — a clean example of "principles, not machinery." ([Ballotpedia News on the First Circuit ruling](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/07/21/federal-appeals-court-blocks-enforcement-of-maines-question-2-which-barred-election-spending-by-corporations-that-are-at-least-5-foreign%E2%80%91owned/))
- **Arizona Proposition 211, the Voters' Right to Know Act (Nov. 2022) — the disclosure model that has now survived state supreme court review.** Passed with **72%**, it requires anyone spending more than $50,000 on statewide (or $25,000 on local) campaign media to disclose the *original* sources of contributions above $5,000 — a "trace-back" rule aimed at pass-through 501(c)(4) money. On June 29, 2026 the Arizona Supreme Court upheld it in *Center for Arizona Policy v. Arizona Secretary of State*, holding that the state constitution's free-speech clause "permits requirements for robust disclosure of campaign spending," while leaving a narrow as-applied path for groups that can prove "actual, serious harm" from disclosure — the *Bonta* harassment carve-out the memo's candidate 3 already contemplates. Alongside Montana's 2015 Disclose Act, this is the second court-tested state template for a "right to know" formulation. ([Campaign Legal Center release on the ruling](https://campaignlegal.org/press-releases/victory-voters-arizona-supreme-court-upholds-proposition-211) — advocacy source, litigant; [CLC explainer with thresholds](https://campaignlegal.org/story/arizonas-proposition-211-and-fight-voters-right-know))
- **The Article V-convention route has been tried and has stalled — and partly reversed.** Wolf-PAC's campaign for state applications for an Article V convention limited to campaign finance won five states in 2014–2016 (Vermont, California, Illinois, New Jersey, Rhode Island) and none since; New Jersey (SCR 161, 2021) and Illinois (SJR 54, 2022) subsequently adopted blanket rescissions of all their prior Article V applications, driven by "runaway convention" concerns on both left and right. Relevant to the state-resolution section because it shows the resolution campaign's ceiling depends on *which* resolution is asked for: memorials urging Congress to propose an amendment (the American Promise model, 25 states) travel; convention applications do not, and can be clawed back. ([Wikipedia, Wolf-PAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf-PAC); [Wikipedia, list of Article V rescissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rescissions_of_Article_V_Convention_applications))
- **Public financing: Seattle's democracy-voucher renewal (Aug. 5, 2025) passed with about 57–59%.** Proposition 1 renewed the property-tax levy funding the nation's only voucher system for another decade ($4.5M/year); supporters cite that contributions of $400+ fell from nearly 60% of campaign dollars (2013) to 9% (2023). A high-50s result in one of the country's most progressive cities is consistent with the memo's decision to hold public financing back as the "~50% weak remedy" and to leave it in statute; it does not move it toward amendment text. ([Sightline Institute](https://sightline.org/2025/08/08/seattleites-keep-their-model-campaign-finance-reform-program/) — advocacy source; [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Seattle,_Washington,_Proposition_1,_Democracy_Voucher_Program_Property_Tax_Renewal_Measure_(August_2025)))

### Extends: International Models

- **Australia — the peer democracy whose court most often *strikes* campaign-finance limits, and why it does not undercut the memo's thesis.** Australia has no bill of rights but an implied constitutional freedom of political communication, and its High Court has used it repeatedly against money-in-politics laws: *Unions NSW v New South Wales* [2013] HCA 58 struck NSW's ban on donations from anyone not on the electoral roll (i.e., corporations and unions) and its aggregation of affiliated-union spending with party caps, finding no evidence non-voters posed greater corruption risk; *Unions NSW* [2019] HCA 1 struck a reduced third-party expenditure cap; and ***Hopper v Victoria* [2026] HCA 11 (April 15, 2026)** invalidated Victoria's entire donations-and-expenditure regime because a $4,970 individual cap coexisted with a "nominated entity" exception that in practice let only the three established parties receive uncapped funds — a textbook incumbent-protection strike, with the state conceding the cutoff date could not be justified. But the same court in ***McCloy v New South Wales* [2015] HCA 34** upheld general donation caps and a ban on property-developer donations as a proportionate response to corruption *and undue influence*, resting on eight ICAC corruption reports. Net for the memo: Australia's court polices *tailoring and self-dealing* (the *Randall*/*Bowman*/*Working Families* function), not the *legitimacy of the purpose* — it has never adopted *Buckley*'s "wholly foreign" rule — so the "US alone forbids the purpose" claim in the memo stands, but the phrase "every peer democracy" should be read with Australia's mixed record in mind. ([The Conversation FactCheck summarizing *Unions NSW* (2013) and *McCloy* (2015)](https://theconversation.com/factcheck-qanda-would-the-constitution-need-to-be-changed-to-ban-political-donations-from-unions-65463); [ABC News on *Hopper*](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-04-15/vic-political-donations-high-court-ruling/106566196); [High Court judgment, *Hopper v Victoria*](https://www.hcourt.gov.au/sites/default/files/eresources/2026-04-15/HCA/Hopper%20v%20Victoria%20(M10-2025)%20[2026]%20HCA%2011.pdf))
- **Canada — the *Working Families* backstory the memo should carry.** The 2025 SCC 5 decision (March 7, 2025) was **5–4** and rested on **s. 3 of the Charter (the right to vote / informed participation), not free expression** — because Ontario had already used the **notwithstanding clause** (s. 33) to override the courts once: after the Superior Court struck the 12-month third-party limit ($600,000, versus no party limit for six months and $1M for the next six) under s. 2(b) in June 2021, Ontario re-enacted it under s. 33; the challengers came back under s. 3, which s. 33 cannot override, and the majority found "absolute disproportionality" letting parties "drown out the voices of third parties… during an entire year." Two implications: (i) the "even fairness-friendly courts police overreach" lesson survives, but only because Canada's *democratic-rights* guarantee is override-proof — the US analog is that candidate 1's "reasonable" limits are policed by Article III courts with no legislative override; (ii) it is a live illustration of a *legislature* using a constitutional trump to protect an incumbent-favoring rule, i.e., the trap the memo warns about is not a US-only phenomenon. ### Extends: Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

- **Scholarly anchor for candidate 2's "dependence" language.** Lawrence Lessig, *Republic, Lost: How Money Corrupts Congress — and a Plan to Stop It* (2011), defines "dependence corruption": not bribery, but a Congress that has become dependent on funders when the Framers designed it to be "dependent on the *people alone*" (Federalist 52); an additional dependence "to attain office, beyond the dependence on the People, epitomizes corruption." This is the historical-originalist framing of exactly the interest *McCutcheon*, *Cruz*, and *NRSC* define away, and it lets the purpose clause be argued in the Framers' vocabulary rather than as a modern egalitarian import — useful for the full-spectrum case on the right. ([Harvard Magazine on Lessig's argument](https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2012/06/a-radical-fix-for-the-republic); [Harvard Law School bibliography entry](https://hls.harvard.edu/bibliography/republic-lost-how-money-corrupts-congress-and-a-plan-to-stop-it/))
- **Gate-check note — a possible sixth principle: authority to exclude foreign money.** The verified record above (the *Citizens United* reservation and *Bluman*; Maine's 86% in 2023; a unanimous House voice vote in July 2026; 25–30 states with bans; and the PPC poll below at 77 R / 84 D) shows a principle with a bipartisan floor higher than anything else in this category and a genuine constitutional gap (foreign-controlled domestic entities). Two things cut against adding it: current doctrine already *permits* Congress to ban foreign-national money (*Bluman*), so the constitutional work is narrower than for candidates 1–2; and Maine Q2 shows the failure mode is tailoring, which is statutory. A clean drafting option is a clause in candidate 4's register — *"Congress and the States may prohibit foreign nationals, foreign governments, and entities owned or controlled by them from contributing or spending money to influence elections"* — that settles the reserved question without constitutionalizing thresholds. This does not change the existing five candidates' gate status; it is flagged for the founder as a candidate that appears to clear the bar and was not evaluated in the original memo.
