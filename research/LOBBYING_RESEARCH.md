# Lobbying Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

This document supports **Target Category 5 (Lobbying)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md) and is a companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). It covers the constitutional doctrine around lobbying restrictions, current federal and state law, pending legislation, international models, and abstraction candidates for amendment text.

*Changelog: 2026-08-18 — figures re-verified; 14 corrections applied in place (git history has the diff).*

Lobbying is unusual among our target categories: it is the only one where the reform the public wants collides head-on with an *enumerated* constitutional right — the First Amendment's guarantee of the right "to petition the Government for a redress of grievances." Courts have recently begun striking down exactly the reforms voters keep passing. That makes this category simultaneously the strongest case *for* a constitutional amendment (a statute genuinely cannot do the job) and the most dangerous to draft (careless text tramples a right both MAGA Republicans and socialist Democrats actively use).

**The headline findings:**

1. **Cooling-off periods are the consensus; lifetime bans are not.** Extending the ex-member wait from 2→5 years is acceptable to 67% (66% R / 73% D) standalone, and 65% (65% R / 67% D) chose "at least 5 years" in the forced-choice item; a 5-year wait for senior executive officials polls at 71% (72% R / 72% D); but a lifetime ban on *all* lobbying is acceptable to only 50% (48% R / 54% D) standalone, and only 32% prefer it over five years in the forced-choice item ([UMD Program for Public Consultation, 2022](https://publicconsultation.org/united-states/large-bipartisan-majority-favors-increasing-lobbying-restrictions-on-former-members-of-congress-and-other-government-officials/)). The amendment should constitutionalize a long cooling-off, not a permanent ban.
2. **Foreign-government lobbying is the exception — and Republicans lead on it.** A *lifetime* ban on ex-officials lobbying for foreign governments polls at 71% (71% R / 74% D). This is one of the very few reform planks where Republican support edges the framing rightward, which is gold for full-spectrum credibility.
3. **The courts are actively dismantling statutory revolving-door bans.** In [Miller v. Ziegler (8th Cir. 2024)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca8/23-1902/23-1902-2024-07-29.html) a federal appeals court struck down Missouri's voter-approved 2-year ban under strict scrutiny, and in [Garcia v. Stillman](https://floridaethics.org/floridas-constitutional-lobbying-bans-remain-effective-for-the-moment-current-status-of-constitutional-lobbying-restrictions/) a federal judge struck Florida's in-office lobbying restriction. Only a *federal* constitutional amendment is immune to this line of attack.
4. **Florida proved the model: voters will constitutionalize this by landslide margins.** Florida's 2018 Amendment 12 — a six-year revolving-door ban written directly into the state constitution — passed with [nearly 79% of the vote](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_Amendment_12,_Lobbying_Restrictions_Amendment_(2018)), in a state Trump carried. It is the single best US precedent for what this foundation proposes.
5. **Executive-order ethics pledges are revocable theater.** Trump's [EO 13770](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13770) imposed a 5-year lobbying pledge and a lifetime foreign-lobbying ban — then [he revoked it in the final hours of his term](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2021/01/25/2021-01713/revocation-of-executive-order-13770), retroactively releasing everyone who signed it. Biden's [EO 13989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13989) was likewise rescinded within hours of the 2025 transition. The instability is itself the argument for constitutional entrenchment.
6. **Existing cooling-offs fail in practice because of the shadow-lobbying ("Daschle") loophole.** Roughly [10,000 unregistered "strategic advisors" work alongside 10,000 registered lobbyists](https://typeinvestigations.org/investigation/2014/02/20/shadow-lobbying-complex/); any amendment that bans "lobbying" without addressing paid influence work simply renames the profession.

## Amendment Framework

### Core provisions

Based on the polling consensus and the legal landscape, the strongest package is a three-part amendment:

1. **A five-year post-service ban on paid lobbying of the federal government** by former Members of Congress, former senior congressional staff, and former senior executive-branch officials (replacing the current 1-year House / 2-year Senate / 1–2-year executive scheme in [18 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42728.html)). Five years matches both the 65–71% polling consensus and the strongest international peer regime (Canada).
2. **A lifetime ban on former Members and senior officials acting as paid agents of foreign governments or foreign political parties** — the 71%/71R/74D winner, already embodied in the bipartisan Fighting Foreign Influence Act (Golden–Porter–Gosar, H.R. 8106, June 16, 2022), the foreign-lobbying title of the [PURE Executive Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr8544.xml) ([Golden–Hinson release, May 1, 2026](https://golden.house.gov/media/press-releases/golden-hinson-introduce-bipartisan-bill-to-strengthen-lobbying-ban-for-senior-government-officials)), and framed as extending 18 U.S.C. § 207(f)'s existing one-year foreign-representation bar rather than filling a void.
3. **An express carve-through of the Petition Clause objection** — text acknowledging that the restriction operates *notwithstanding* the First Amendment as to compensated representational lobbying by covered former officials, while explicitly preserving every citizen's uncompensated right to petition.

### Challenges

- **The Petition Clause collision (see full subsection below).** Any statute doing this is now presumptively vulnerable after *Miller v. Ziegler*; an amendment solves that, but the drafting must modify the First Amendment *narrowly* or the coalition fractures. No one on either flank wants to be seen "amending the First Amendment" — framing must be "closing the paid-influence exception," not "restricting petition."
- **The definition trap.** Ban "lobbying" (an LDA term of art) and every ex-senator becomes a "strategic consultant" by Monday. The amendment must reach *compensated influence activity*, not just registered lobbying contacts, or it constitutionalizes the Daschle loophole.
- **Overbreadth blowback.** Text that could plausibly touch a veteran writing his congressman, a union rep, a pastor, or a nonprofit's policy director will be demagogued to death from both directions — and would deserve to be.
- **Lifetime-ban temptation.** Advocates will push to constitutionalize the BLAST Act's lifetime ban on all lobbying. The polling says no: 50% acceptable (48% R / 54% D) standalone, and only 32% prefer it to a five-year wait. Hold the line at five years except for foreign governments.
- **Enforcement design.** Florida's amendment and Canada's Act both show that a ban without an empowered enforcer degrades into UK-style advisory theater.

### Strategic read

This category has the rare property that the *populist right* case ("drain the swamp," Trump's own 2017 EO) and the *progressive left* case (Warren's Anti-Corruption Act) converge on nearly identical text — Warren and Rick Scott are literally co-sponsoring [the same lifetime-ban bill](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-rick-scott-introduce-bipartisan-bill-to-ban-former-members-of-congress-from-lobbying/). 73% of Americans (78% R / 72% D) say lobbyists have too much influence. The court decisions striking down voter-approved bans supply the "why an amendment, not a law" answer that most categories lack. The main risk is not opposition but drafting error: the difference between a 79%-Florida-style landslide and a civil-libertarian revolt is entirely in how the text treats ordinary petitioners.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

### The Petition Clause problem

This is the load-bearing constitutional trap for the entire category.

**Is there a constitutional right to lobby?** The Supreme Court has never squarely held that *paid, professional* lobbying is fully protected — but the doctrine has been trending that way:

- **[United States v. Harriss (1954)](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/united-states-v-harriss/)** upheld the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act's registration and disclosure requirements against a First Amendment challenge, on the theory that Congress may know "who is being hired, who is putting up the money, and how much" ([full opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/347/612)). *Harriss* is why **disclosure and registration regimes are safe**; it says nothing blessing outright bans. The [Institute for Free Speech](https://www.ifs.org/news/institute-for-free-speech-urges-supreme-court-to-revisit-outdated-precedent-on-lobbying-disclosure/) *(advocacy source, free-speech side)* is actively urging the Court to narrow even *Harriss*.
- **[Autor v. Pritzker (D.C. Cir. 2014)](https://legalethicsopinions.justia.com/2016/12/16/autor-v-pritzker)** is the key modern case: registered lobbyists challenged Obama's ban on lobbyists serving on federal advisory committees, and the D.C. Circuit held they had **pled a viable First Amendment claim — treating paid lobbying as an exercise of the Petition Clause** and the committee ban as an unconstitutional condition. The administration narrowed the policy rather than defend it. The Congressional Research Service's [Constitution Annotated essay on lobbying](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt1-7-13-5/ALDE_00013494/) treats the right to petition as encompassing lobbying activity. Academic work like Maggie McKinley's [*Lobbying and the Petition Clause*](http://www.stanfordlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2016/06/68_McKinley_-_Stan._L._Rev._1131.pdf) (Stanford L. Rev. 2016) complicates this — arguing the historical petition right protected individual petitioning, not the modern access economy — but courts have not adopted that view.
- **[Nevada Commission on Ethics v. Carrigan (2011)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/117/)** cuts the other way and is the reformers' best precedent: a unanimous Court held that a legislator's *vote* is not his personal speech — "the legislative power … is not personal to the legislator but belongs to the people." *Carrigan* establishes that **restrictions attached to the office and its powers** (recusal, conflict rules) are not First Amendment problems. The doctrinal question is whether post-employment restrictions are more like *Carrigan* conditions-of-office or more like *Autor* burdens on private petitioning.
- **[Miller v. Ziegler (8th Cir. 2024)](https://www.jurist.org/news/2024/07/us-appeals-court-finds-missouri-law-banning-lobbying-2-years-after-office-unconstitutional/)** answered that question badly for reformers: the Eighth Circuit applied **strict scrutiny** to Missouri's voter-approved two-year revolving-door ban, held that it "burdens political speech," found Missouri had not proven actual or apparent quid-pro-quo corruption, and struck it down as not narrowly tailored. If *Miller* becomes the national rule, **every cooling-off statute — including 18 U.S.C. § 207 itself — is on borrowed time.**
- **[Garcia v. Stillman (S.D. Fla. 2023)](https://floridaethics.org/floridas-constitutional-lobbying-bans-remain-effective-for-the-moment-current-status-of-constitutional-lobbying-restrictions/)** struck Florida's *in-office* lobbying restriction (Art. II § 8(f)(2)) as an unconstitutional abridgment of speech, though the six-year *post-office* ban was not enjoined and remains in effect.

**The upshot:** courts uphold *disclosure* (Harriss) and *conditions on official power* (Carrigan), but increasingly treat *bans on compensated post-office lobbying* as core political speech triggering strict scrutiny (Autor, Miller). A federal statute imposing a 5-year or lifetime ban would face exactly the *Miller* attack — which is precisely why this belongs in the Constitution. **Any amendment must either expressly modify the Petition Clause as applied to compensated lobbying by former officials, or be drafted as a qualification/condition of federal office in the *Carrigan* mold.** Both routes are explored in the abstraction candidates below.

### Current federal law

- **[Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995](https://typeinvestigations.org/investigation/2014/02/20/shadow-lobbying-complex/)** — registration/disclosure regime. Its central flaw: registration is triggered only when lobbying exceeds **20% of time worked for a client**, so "strategic advisors" who plan campaigns and coach others (the **Daschle loophole**, named for the ex-Senate Majority Leader who became a "policy adviser") never register. An estimated [10,000+ unregistered influence professionals](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/shadow-lobbying-complex/) operate beside the registered corps, while reported lobbying spend stays above $3B/year.
- **[Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007](https://ethics.house.gov/outside-employment-income/post-employment-restrictions)** amended [18 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42728.html): **1-year cooling-off for House members, 2 years for Senators**, 1 year for senior staff, and 1–2 years for senior/very-senior executive officials. These are criminal provisions but narrowly cover "lobbying contacts" — behind-the-scenes strategy work is untouched.
- **[Executive Order 13770 (Trump, Jan. 2017)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13770)** — appointees pledged a 5-year ban on lobbying their agency and a **lifetime ban on FARA work for foreign governments**. Trump [revoked the entire order in the small hours of his last day in office](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2021/01/20/npr-trump-revokes-administration-ethics-rules-on-his-way-out-the-door), with no explanation, releasing all signatories retroactively ([Federal Register revocation](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2021/01/25/2021-01713/revocation-of-executive-order-13770)). **This is the instructive episode:** even a president elected on "drain the swamp" un-drained it by signature — pledges last only as long as the pledger's incentives.
- **[Executive Order 13989 (Biden, Jan. 2021)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13989)** — 2-year post-employment restrictions, a FARA restriction lasting the remainder of the administration or 2 years, and a golden-parachute ban; [rescinded by Trump within hours on Jan. 20, 2025](https://stateandfed.com/ethics/bidens-ethics-executive-order-for-appointees-revoked/). See CRS, [Ethics Pledges Since 1993](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44974).
- **[FARA (1938)](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/foreign-agents-registration-act-enforcement-in-the-twenty-first-century)** — foreign-agent registration, near-dormant for decades until the **post-2017 enforcement surge** driven by the Manafort and Flynn prosecutions; [first-time filings rose 50% in one year](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/mueller-effect-fara-filings-soar-shadow-manafort-flynn-probes-n838571) and supplemental filings doubled. FARA is disclosure-only — it does not *prohibit* an ex-Speaker from representing a foreign power, it merely requires a form (18 U.S.C. § 207(f) does impose a one-year bar on former Members and senior executive officials representing, aiding, or advising a foreign government or foreign political party, and a lifetime bar for the USTR/Deputy USTR — so the amendment extends § 207(f) rather than filling a void). [Responsible Statecraft](https://responsiblestatecraft.org/2022/06/28/the-revolving-door-from-congress-to-foreign-interest-lobbyist-is-well-traveled/) *(advocacy-adjacent, Quincy Institute)* documents the well-traveled road from Congress to foreign agent.

### Current bills (the reform demand signal)

- **[Public Service Integrity Act (H.R. 414, Rep. Posey, R-FL)](https://vop.org/americans-on-lobbying-restrictions/)** — extends ex-member cooling-off to 5 years; the Republican-sponsored bill tested in the UMD survey.
- **[Cleaning Up Washington's Act (S. 751 / S. 1158, Sen. Tester, D-MT)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/senate-bill/1158)** — the Democratic companion concept, similar 5-year extension.
- **[BLAST Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/4524/text)** — lifetime ban on ex-member lobbying; introduced by [Warren (D) and Rick Scott (R) in May 2026](https://www.rickscott.senate.gov/2026/5/sens-rick-scott-elizabeth-warren-introduce-bill-to-permanently-ban-members-of-congress-from-lobbying), with a House version from [Vindman (D-VA) and Barry Moore (R-AL) in June 2026](https://vindman.house.gov/2026/06/04/vindman-introduces-bipartisan-bill-to-tackle-corruption-ban-members-of-congress-from-lobbying/) ([H.R. 9130 text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9130/text/ih)). Its § 2(a) amends [18 U.S.C. § 207(e)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/207) to reach a former Member who either **(A)** registers as an LDA lobbyist **or (B)** knowingly makes an influencing communication to Congress on another's behalf — **registration is one of two independent triggers, not an irrelevance**, which is how the bill reaches the Daschle loophole. The bill specifies **no penalty schedule of its own**: a violator "shall be punished as provided in section 216," so the up-to-five-years and $50,000-civil-fine figures come from [18 U.S.C. § 216](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/216), not from this bill. Scope note: S. 4524 covers former Members and elected officers lobbying *Congress* — not senior executive officials, and not agency lobbying — so it is narrower than the clause the corpus drafts from it; its § 2(d) applies only to those leaving office after enactment.
- **[PURE Executive Act (H.R. 8544)](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr8544.xml)** — bipartisan; 5-year ban on senior executive officials lobbying their former agency plus a **lifetime ban on lobbying for foreign governments**.
- **[Close the Revolving Door Act](https://ocasio-cortez.house.gov/media/press-releases/ocasio-cortez-neguse-introduce-legislation-impose-lifetime-ban-members)** (Bennet lineage since 2010; AOC/Neguse [H.R. 3554 in 2025](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3554/text)) — lifetime member ban + 6-year staff cooling-off. Note that even sympathetic analysts [predict it would be struck down](https://ace-usa.org/blog/research/research-democratic-governance/can-congress-regulate-its-own-influence-unpacking-the-2025-close-the-revolving-door-act/) under the Petition Clause — again, the amendment argument.
- **Fighting Foreign Influence Act (Golden D-ME / Porter D-CA / Gosar R-AZ; H.R. 8106, June 16, 2022)** — lifetime ban on former members, flag officers, and senior officials registering as foreign agents; the [Golden–Hinson release](https://golden.house.gov/media/press-releases/golden-hinson-introduce-bipartisan-bill-to-strengthen-lobbying-ban-for-senior-government-officials) is the May 1, 2026 PURE Executive Act reintroduction (5-year agency ban plus lifetime foreign-government ban), same substance, different bill and sponsors.

The pattern: **every one of these has genuine cross-party sponsorship**, and every serious analysis concedes the statutory versions face First Amendment peril. The demand is bipartisan; the vehicle is inadequate. That is the amendment's opening.

### Prior Amendment Proposals

Unlike campaign finance (where dozens of amendment texts exist post-*Citizens United*), there is **no significant history of proposed federal constitutional amendments specifically restricting the revolving door** — reformers have pursued statutes ([Bennet's 2012 STOCK Act amendment](https://bennet.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/2016/3/bennet-files-amendment-to-ban-members-of-congress-from-becoming-lobbyists-close-revolving-door) was a bill amendment, not Article V). Some omnibus anti-corruption amendment proposals (e.g., American Promise's campaign-finance-focused "For Our Freedom" amendment) touch influence generally, and Warren's [Anti-Corruption and Public Integrity Act](https://www.warren.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/media/doc/Master%20Summary%20of%20Anti%20Corruption%20Act%20-%20FINAL.pdf) is the most comprehensive statutory blueprint. This is essentially **open ground at the federal constitutional level** — the only true precedent is at the state level, in Florida.

## State Models

**Florida — the keystone precedent.** In November 2018, Florida voters approved **[Amendment 12](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_Amendment_12,_Lobbying_Restrictions_Amendment_(2018))** with **~79% of the vote** — the highest share of any measure on that year's ballot — writing into **Article II, § 8(f) of the Florida Constitution** a ban on public officers lobbying "for compensation on issues of policy, appropriations, or procurement" for **six years** after leaving office, covering state legislators, statewide officers, judges, and local officials. It was placed on the ballot by the Constitution Revision Commission, sponsored by Republican Don Gaetz, explicitly "to nail shut the revolving door" ([Florida Bar Journal analysis](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/amendment-12-lobbying-and-abuse-of-office-by-public-office-holders/); [Florida Commission on Ethics text](https://ethics.state.fl.us/Documents/Ethics/ArticleIISec8.html)). Three lessons:

1. **A 6-year ban — triple the federal Senate period — passed by a landslide in a red-trending state.** The full-spectrum test was passed in the field, not just in polls.
2. **Constitutionalizing it was deliberate**: proponents understood a statute could be repealed by the next legislature of ex-legislators.
3. **But state constitutions can't beat the First Amendment**: in [Garcia v. Stillman](https://www.floridabulldog.org/2022/02/five-florida-local-politicians-sue-state-to-block-new-get-tough-lobbying-ban/), a federal judge permanently enjoined the *in-office* restriction on First Amendment grounds (Aug. 2023) — but the Eleventh Circuit stayed the injunction on Dec. 1, 2023 as to everyone except the named plaintiff, so Florida enforces the in-office restriction against all other officers pending appeal (argued Feb. 26, 2025, panel signaling it would uphold the ban), while [the six-year post-office ban survives for now](https://floridaethics.org/floridas-constitutional-lobbying-bans-remain-effective-for-the-moment-current-status-of-constitutional-lobbying-restrictions/). Only a *federal* amendment is beyond this reach.

**Missouri — the cautionary tale.** [Clean Missouri (Amendment 1, 2018)](https://ballotpedia.org/Missouri_Amendment_1,_Lobbying,_Campaign_Finance,_and_Redistricting_Initiative_(2018)) passed with 62%, imposing a 2-year revolving-door ban and a $5 lobbyist gift cap. In practice, [lobbyists adapted quickly](https://www.stltoday.com/news/local/government-politics/it-forces-you-to-be-more-creative-how-lobbyists-kept-their-grip-on-missouri-politics/article_c64bfa0b-daab-560a-b023-ede49f81ba1c.html) ("it forces you to be more creative"), and in July 2024 the Eighth Circuit [struck the revolving-door ban entirely](https://missouriindependent.com/briefs/federal-court-strikes-down-missouris-revolving-door-lobbying-ban/) in *Miller v. Ziegler*. A state constitutional provision, approved by voters, was erased by the federal First Amendment. **Missouri is the proof that this category cannot be finished at the state level.**

**The broader map.** Per [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/revolving-door-restrictions-by-state-2019/) *(advocacy source, pro-reform)* and [MultiState's 50-state survey](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2024/10/1/revolving-door-laws-by-state): ~33 states have cooling-offs of one year or less; more than a dozen impose two years; several (California, New York, New Mexico, Mississippi, Texas) add permanent bans on working the *specific matters* an official personally handled; Florida's six years is the national maximum; and a handful of states (until [Michigan](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/07/17/michigan-poised-to-close-revolving-door-for-lawmaker-lobbyists)'s ban, signed July 27, 2026 and effective Jan. 1, 2027) allow same-day lawmaker-to-lobbyist conversion. The "particular-matter" permanent bans are doctrinally safest (closest to *Carrigan*-style conflict rules) and worth echoing in amendment enforcement language.

## International Models

### Canada — the strongest peer regime

Canada's [Lobbying Act](https://lobbycanada.gc.ca/en/rules/the-lobbying-act/5-year-post-employment-prohibition-on-lobbying/) imposes a **five-year post-employment lobbying prohibition** on "designated public office holders" (ministers, ministerial staff, senior officials, MPs' senior staff), enforced by an independent **Commissioner of Lobbying** with investigation powers and a public registry; the Commissioner may grant narrow exemptions only where "not contrary to the purposes of the Act" ([Library of Parliament overview](https://lop.parl.ca/sites/PublicWebsite/default/en_CA/ResearchPublications/201173E)). Canada is the direct proof-of-concept that a 5-year ban is administrable in a G7 democracy — it is exactly the number the US polling supports.

**Effectiveness critiques:** [Democracy Watch](https://democracywatch.ca/secret-lobbying-loopholes-in-canadas-federal-lobbying-law/) *(advocacy source, pro-reform)* catalogs a "dirty dozen" loopholes: the five-year ban effectively binds only *registrable* lobbying, so ex-officials can begin unregistered "strategic advising" immediately; in-house corporate lobbying is restricted only where it is a "significant part" of duties; and enforcement is criticized as opaque — a [2026 report found the Commissioner declining to name rule-breakers](https://www.nationalobserver.com/2026/03/16/news/federal-lobbying-regulations-politics-criticism), and critics call the regime ["a black hole"](https://www.canadianaffairs.news/2026/04/22/canadas-lobbying-commissioner-rules-draw-criticism/). **Lesson: the 5-year number works; the definition ("registrable lobbying") and the enforcement culture are where Canada leaks.**

### European Union — scandal-driven, still weak

Former Commissioners face notification obligations for **2 years** (3 for the President) and an **18-month cooling-off** under the Code of Conduct — a regime exposed as inadequate when **Commission President José Manuel Barroso joined Goldman Sachs** 20 months after leaving office, formally breaking no rule ([Corporate Europe Observatory](https://corporateeurope.org/en/revolving-doors/2016/07/barroso-and-goldman-sachs-dangerous-liaison) *(advocacy source)*; Green MEP Sven Giegold: ["a miserly 18 months … in contrast to the five-year ban that applies in Canada"](https://sven-giegold.de/en/goldman-sachs-hires-barroso-longer-cooling-off-period-for-eu-commissioners-urgently-needed/)). The **Qatargate** cash-for-influence scandal (2022, MEPs allegedly bribed by Qatar and Morocco) forced Parliament to adopt reforms — but the result was only a **six-month cooling-off for ex-MEPs**, which [Transparency International EU](https://transparency.eu/qatargate-reforms-european-parliament-fails-its-first-big-test/) *(advocacy source)* dismissed as "window dressing," and the EU Ombudsman [flagged continuing gaps a year later](https://eno.ombudsman.europa.eu/home/news/archives-2023/maincontent/enonews-1215.html). The [Transparency Register](https://transparency-register.europa.eu/find-out-more/news/important-reminder-about-european-parliament-conditionality-measures-2024-09-18-0_en) conditions access on registration but remains transparency-first, prohibition-light. **Lesson: half-measure cooling-offs (6–18 months) fail visibly and repeatedly; scandals produce reform theater unless the number is real.**

### France — criminal teeth

France's [HATVP](https://publicmatters.nl/en/actueel/over-draaideuren-pantouflage-franse-regels-voor-lobbytransparantie/) (High Authority for Transparency in Public Life, created after the 2013 Cahuzac scandal) pre-clears post-government moves: officials are barred for **three years** from taking private roles related to their former responsibilities ("pantouflage" is so entrenched [the French have a word for it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantouflage)), with violations punishable by up to **3 years' imprisonment and a €200,000 fine**. France is the model for *criminalized, pre-cleared* revolving-door control — though [research on French debt-management officials](https://academic.oup.com/policyandsociety/article/44/2/165/8242001) finds sophisticated actors still time their exits around the rules. **Lesson: mandatory pre-clearance by an independent authority plus criminal penalties is the enforcement ceiling among peers.**

### United Kingdom — the anti-model

The UK's [Advisory Committee on Business Appointments (ACOBA)](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn03745/) reviews ex-ministers' and civil servants' jobs under the Business Appointment Rules — but it is purely advisory: it [cannot veto any appointment, and its strongest sanction is recommending a delay](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/SN03745/SN03745.pdf). Parliament's own committee called it a **"toothless regulator"**; the David Cameron/Greensill lobbying scandal showed the system [is "absurdly easy to sidestep"](https://theconversation.com/david-cameron-and-greensill-this-toothless-regulator-is-absurdly-easy-to-sidestep-158914) because ex-PMs face only a two-year advisory window and no penalty at all for ignoring advice. **Lesson: rules without an enforcer and without penalties are functionally decorative — the enforcement trap made flesh.**

### Australia — foreign-influence disclosure without teeth

Australia's [Foreign Influence Transparency Scheme (2018)](https://www.ag.gov.au/integrity/foreign-influence-transparency-scheme) was pitched as an ["improved version" of FARA](https://www.unswlawjournal.unsw.edu.au/article/foreign-agent-registration-schemes-in-australia-and-the-united-states-the-scope-risk-and-limitations-of-transparency), and notably imposes **lifetime enhanced registration obligations on former Cabinet ministers** acting for foreign principals. But it has produced [no prosecutions and minimal registrations](https://www.lowyinstitute.org/the-interpreter/fits-starts), with narrower definitions and thinner disclosure than FARA. **Lesson: the US's post-2017 FARA enforcement surge — not Australia's newer statute — is the effective foreign-influence model when prosecuted, and prosecution priorities are themselves revocable (the Feb. 2025 Bondi memo narrowed criminal FARA enforcement to espionage-like conduct and disbanded the Foreign Influence Task Force); and the "lifetime obligation for former ministers" concept validates the amendment's foreign-lobbying plank.**

### OECD baseline

The OECD's [Post-Public Employment: Good Practices for Preventing Conflict of Interest (2010)](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/post-public-employment_9789264056701-en.html) and its lobbying-transparency principles recommend cooling-off periods calibrated to seniority, restrictions on "switching sides" in matters an official personally handled, and — the near-universal finding across 30 member states — that **standards exist everywhere but "enforcing standards and imposing suitable sanctions remains a challenge for many countries."** The international consensus problem is not the rule; it is the enforcement.

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Five years is the proven ceiling of the politically achievable and the floor of the effective.** Canada administers 5 years without incident; the EU's 6–18 months failed publicly (Barroso, Qatargate); US polling lands on 5 (65–71%). The number writes itself.
2. **Define the conduct, not the job title.** Canada's ban binds only "registrable lobbying"; the US LDA's 20% threshold created shadow lobbying; both regimes leak through "strategic advisor" relabeling. Effective text must reach *compensated influence activity on behalf of another*, however styled.
3. **An independent enforcer with real sanctions is the whole game.** France (pre-clearance + prison) works better than Canada (commissioner + registry), which works better than the UK (advice + nothing). ACOBA is the controlled experiment in what a rule without an enforcer becomes.
4. **Transparency regimes and prohibition regimes are different tools; neither substitutes for the other.** Registries (EU, FARA, FITS) illuminate; only prohibitions with penalties deter. FARA's post-2017 lesson: even a disclosure statute is transformative *when actually prosecuted* — and prosecution priorities are themselves revocable (Feb. 2025 Bondi memo).
5. **Revocable instruments revert.** Trump's EO 13770 (self-revoked at 11:59), Biden's EO 13989 (revoked within hours by his successor), and ACOBA's voluntary compliance all show that anything less durable than entrenched law tracks the incumbent's incentives.
6. **Foreign-government representation is treated as categorically different everywhere.** Trump's EO imposed a *lifetime* foreign ban alongside a 5-year domestic one; Australia imposes lifetime obligations on ex-ministers; the PURE and Fighting Foreign Influence Acts do the same. No peer democracy treats representing a foreign state as ordinary petitioning — and neither does US public opinion (71% for a lifetime foreign ban vs. 50% acceptable / 32% preferred over five years for a general one).
7. **Scandal is the reform engine — build the vehicle before the scandal.** Qatargate, Greensill, Barroso, and Manafort each produced a reform window measured in months. An amendment text sitting on the shelf when the next scandal breaks is worth ten introduced after.
8. **Seniority-tiering is universal and legitimate.** Every peer regime (OECD-recommended) scales restrictions to the power of the office — cabinet/members get the longest bans, junior staff shorter ones. This also helps the amendment survive overbreadth objections.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. All four below clear it — the five-year door polls 65–72% with identical R/D support, the foreign-agent bar is the rare plank Republicans lead on (71R/74D), Florida ratified the model with 79%, and the firewall and enforcement clauses are structural protections for the polled package. The lifetime ban on all lobbying (50% acceptable, 47.5% R; 32% preferred over five years) was excluded from candidacy at the outset; a standalone favor/oppose re-test of the lifetime ban is the specific evidence that could reopen it.*

**1. The five-year door.** No senior federal official profits from selling access to the government they just left, for five years.

- Proposed formulation: *"No person who has served as a Senator, Representative, or officer of the United States in a senior position defined by law shall, within five years after leaving such office, receive compensation for advocating before, or advising others in advocating before, the Congress or the executive agencies of the United States on behalf of any other person or entity. This section shall not be construed to abridge the right of any person to petition the Government on their own behalf, or without compensation on behalf of others."*
- Constitutionalizes the 65–71% consensus (UMD 2022; Posey's H.R. 414; Tester's S. 751; Canada's Lobbying Act), and — because it *is* the First Amendment's peer — extinguishes the *Miller v. Ziegler* strict-scrutiny attack that no statute can survive.
- ⚠️ **The Petition Clause collision.** This text restricts what *Autor v. Pritzker* treated as protected petitioning. It works only because a later amendment controls an earlier one — but the drafting must say so implicitly through specificity, and the campaign must be ready for the "they're amending the First Amendment" attack. The savings clause (own-behalf and uncompensated petitioning) is not decoration; it is the answer to that attack.
- ⚠️ **The shadow-lobbying definition trap.** Ban "lobbying" and everyone becomes a "strategic advisor" (Daschle loophole; Canada's identical leak). Hence "advocating before, *or advising others in advocating before*" — the phrase must reach the coaching-and-strategy business model, with Congress empowered to define the perimeter.

**2. The lifetime foreign-agent bar.** Former officials never work for foreign governments. Period.

- Proposed formulation: *"No person who has served as President, Vice President, Senator, Representative, judge of the United States, officer of the United States in a senior position defined by law, or commissioned officer of the armed forces in a general or flag rank shall at any time receive compensation from, or act as the agent of, a foreign government or foreign political party for the purpose of influencing any policy or action of the United States."*
- This is the 71% / 71R / 74D plank where Republicans lead — mirroring EO 13770's lifetime pledge, the PURE Executive Act, and the Golden–Porter–Gosar Fighting Foreign Influence Act (2022), and echoing the Framers' own Foreign Emoluments Clause logic (Art. I § 9 already bans present-taking from foreign states; this extends the principle past the exit door).
- ⚠️ **The definition trap, foreign edition.** FARA practice shows foreign states hire through cutouts — state-owned enterprises, sovereign wealth funds, "foundations." Text keyed to "foreign government" alone invites the Qatar-via-NGO structure that produced Qatargate; the enforcement statute must be authorized to reach entities directed or principally funded by foreign states.
- ⚠️ **The overbreadth trap.** Must not catch academics at foreign universities, employees of ordinary foreign private companies, or dual nationals' civic life — the limiting phrase "for the purpose of influencing any policy or action of the United States" is load-bearing.

**3. The citizen-petitioner firewall.** The amendment touches the access industry, never the citizen.

- Proposed formulation: *"Nothing in this article shall be construed to limit the right of any citizen, or of any association of citizens including religious, labor, charitable, or membership organizations, to petition the Government for a redress of grievances, nor to require the registration of any person petitioning without compensation."*
- A belt-and-suspenders construction rule making the full-spectrum coalition possible: gun-rights groups, unions, churches, and civil-liberties organizations all lobby, and all will (rightly) kill any text that could touch them. *Harriss* already limits regulation to paid direct lobbying; this locks that limit in.
- ⚠️ **The citizen-petitioner overbreadth trap is the campaign-killer.** Every opposing ad will feature a veteran, a nurse, or a pastor "banned from talking to their congressman." The firewall must be *in the constitutional text itself*, quotable in thirty seconds — not left to implementing legislation.

**4. Enforcement that cannot be waived by the enforced.** Real penalties, an independent enforcer, no self-pardon-by-EO.

- Proposed formulation: *"The Congress shall enforce this article by appropriate legislation, which shall provide civil and criminal penalties and shall vest investigative authority in an officer whose independence is protected by law; and no provision of this article may be waived, exempted, or suspended by executive order or by rule of either House."*
- Learns from the whole comparative record: ACOBA (no enforcer → dead letter), Canada (weak enforcement culture → "black hole"), France (pre-clearance + criminal penalties → strongest deterrence), and EO 13770's midnight self-revocation (waivability → nullity).
- ⚠️ **The enforcement trap.** A self-executing ban with no enforcer becomes Florida's — standing but untested; an enforcement clause that merely says "Congress may enforce" lets a Congress of future lobbyists enforce nothing. The non-waiver sentence exists because both Trump and Biden pledges evaporated at the stroke of a successor's (or their own) pen.
- ⚠️ **Counter-trap: over-specification.** Penalty amounts, agency names, and registration thresholds age badly in constitutional text (the LDA's 20% rule is the fossil warning). The Constitution sets the floor and the independence guarantee; Congress sets the machinery.

**The package skeleton.** A single "Public Integrity" lobbying article: **Section 1** — five-year post-service compensated-advocacy ban for Members of Congress and senior officials (Principle 1). **Section 2** — lifetime foreign-government agency bar (Principle 2). **Section 3** — the citizen-petitioner firewall (Principle 3). **Section 4** — enforcement, independence, and non-waiver (Principle 4), with seniority tiers and definitions delegated to legislation within constitutional floors. Lead the public case with Section 2 (the Republican-leading 71% plank) and Florida's 79% precedent; hold the perimeter with Section 3.

**Inherently statutory:** (leave out of the amendment; belongs in implementing legislation or ordinary law)

- Registration mechanics, thresholds, and reporting cadence (LDA/FARA modernization; abolishing the 20% test).
- The precise roster of "senior positions" covered, and seniority tiers for staff.
- Penalty schedules, fine amounts, and the design of the independent enforcement office (a FARA-unit/HATVP hybrid).
- Lobbyist gift bans, contingency-fee bans, and bundling rules (Clean Missouri-style).
- In-office lobbying restrictions for sitting local/state officials (the part of Florida's scheme that lost in *Garcia v. Stillman* — and a state-law matter regardless).
- Golden-parachute and reverse-revolving-door rules for people *entering* government (Biden EO 13989 territory).
- FARA exemption-tightening for adversary states and cutout-entity definitions.
- Transparency infrastructure: public lobbying databases, meeting-disclosure ("legislative footprint") rules.

### Extends: The headline findings / Amendment Framework — Challenges (the polling that drives the gate check)

- **The "32% for a lifetime ban" figure is a forced-choice preference share, not a support level — read the questionnaire.** In the [UMD Program for Public Consultation questionnaire (fielded June 13–29, 2022; n=2,606 RV; ±1.9%)](https://vop.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Lobbying_Quaire_0622.pdf), Q38 asked respondents to pick the *one* option they "would recommend the most": no waiting period 4.4%, keep current law 17.6%, **extend to 5 years 43.3%**, **prohibit for life 31.6%** (R 32.5% / D 29.4%). So the memo's "65% (65 R / 67 D) for five years" is really the *"at least 5 years"* total (43% five-year + 32% lifetime = 65.3%; R 64.9 / D 66.8), and the "32%" is the share who *prefer* lifetime over five years — not the share who would accept it. Asked standalone (Q37, 0–10 acceptability scale), a lifetime ban on ex-Members lobbying was rated **acceptable (6–10) by 50.3% (R 47.5% / D 54.4%)** and **"at least tolerable" (5–10) by 64.7% (R 62.1% / D 68.4%)**; the 2017 wave was nearly identical (48.5% acceptable; R 51.5 / D 45.1). The 5-year extension alone (Q36) was acceptable to 67.1% (R 65.7 / D 73.3) and at least tolerable to 80.1% (R 77.5 / D 85.9). **Gate-check bearing:** the five-year door still clearly out-polls the lifetime ban in the less-supportive party (R 65.7% vs 47.5% "acceptable"), so the held-back conclusion stands — but its stated evidentiary basis ("32%") should be corrected, and the lifetime ban should be re-tested as a standalone favor/oppose item before being described as failing the bar. See *Flagged corrections* below.
- **The strongest counterarguments were tested in the same survey — and lost, but not by landslides.** The First Amendment/petition argument against longer bans (Q31) was found convincing by 38.0% (R 42.8% / D 31.5%); the "chilling effect on talented people serving" argument (Q33) by 44.0% (R 45.3 / D 41.4); the "lobbying as a fallback career for risky public service" argument (Q35) by 32.3% (R 34.1 / D 27.9). Against a lifetime *foreign*-government ban, the "discriminatory / violates free speech / they'll just hire another lobbyist" argument (Q44) was found convincing by only 29.0% (R 30.9 / D 26.0), and the ban itself was favored 71.3% (R 70.5 / D 73.7) in a plain favor/oppose item (Q46) — 74.9% (R 81.2 / D 69.7) in 2017 ([questionnaire](https://vop.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Lobbying_Quaire_0622.pdf)). The recruitment/"brain drain" objection is the good-faith case the memo should carry alongside the access-industry and free-speech objections; the seniority-tiering in Principle 4 is the standard answer to it.
- **The empirical literature says the revolving door sells access, not expertise — which is the anti-corruption rationale courts (Miller) demanded evidence for.** [Blanes i Vidal, Draca & Fons-Rosen, "Revolving Door Lobbyists," *American Economic Review* 102(7): 3731–48 (2012)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.102.7.3731): "Lobbyists with experience in the office of a US Senator suffer a 24 percent drop in generated revenue when that Senator leaves office," an effect that is immediate and grows with the departing senator's seniority. [Bertrand, Bombardini & Trebbi, "Is It Whom You Know or What You Know?," *AER* 104(12): 3885–3920 (2014)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.12.3885) find lobbyists follow the politicians they are connected to when those politicians change committees, and that connections carry "a more consistent monetary premium" than expertise. Both are the peer-reviewed record a litigator or a ratification campaign would cite for *why* a cooling-off is tailored to an appearance-of-corruption interest.
- **How many actually walk through the door.** Public Citizen's [*Revolving Congress* (May 30, 2019)](https://www.citizen.org/article/revolving-congress/) *(advocacy source, pro-reform)* found that 59% (26 of 44) of members of the 115th Congress who took jobs outside government and politics went into federal-influence work — 22 to lobbying/consulting firms, 4 to trade or business groups — up from 43% (86 of 198) of members who left between 1998 and 2004 in its 2005 study. Not party-broken; the report also flags that many operate as unregistered "strategic consultants" (the Daschle loophole).

### Extends: The Petition Clause problem

- ***United States v. Rumely* (1953) is the doctrinal origin of the "direct lobbying only" reading that Harriss then locked in.** In [*United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41 (1953)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/345/41), the Court read a House committee's mandate over "lobbying activities" to reach only "representations made directly to the Congress," not efforts to influence public opinion, expressly invoking the "cardinal principle" of construing statutes to avoid a "serious doubt of constitutional validity." The narrow-construction reflex that produced the LDA's direct-contact focus (and hence the shadow-lobbying gap) is thus judge-made, not merely a 1995 drafting choice — supporting the memo's argument that reaching *indirect* compensated influence safely requires Article V text.
- ***Garcia v. Stillman* — appellate status.** The district court's August 2023 judgment against Florida's *in-office* restriction was narrowed on appeal: on Dec. 1, 2023 the Eleventh Circuit partially stayed the injunction, [limiting it to the named plaintiff (Miami-Dade Commissioner René Garcia) and allowing Florida to enforce the in-office restriction against every other public officer](https://www.wusf.org/courts-law/2023-12-01/federal-appeals-court-gives-go-ahead-florida-lobbying-restrictions) pending appeal ("Injunctive relief should be no broader than 'necessary to protect the interests of the parties'"). At oral argument on Feb. 26, 2025 the panel (Chief Judge Pryor, Judges Grant and Luck) was openly skeptical of the challenge — Pryor: "He can lobby them, he can talk to them, he just can't be paid on the side if he wants to be an elected official … That's the price of being a county commissioner"; Grant: "This is a prophylactic rule based on the people's concerns about corruption in the government. Why isn't that a valid concern?" ([Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/florida-lobbying-ban-likely-to-survive-11th-circuit-scrutiny/)). No merits opinion was located as of this review. If the Eleventh Circuit upholds Florida's in-office ban, the circuit picture becomes *split* rather than uniformly hostile (Miller in the Eighth vs. Garcia in the Eleventh) — which strengthens, not weakens, the "only a federal amendment settles it" argument.

### Extends: Current federal law

- **Federal law already imposes a short *prohibition* — not just disclosure — on ex-officials working for foreign governments, and a *lifetime* one for one office.** [18 U.S.C. § 207(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/207) bars anyone covered by § 207(c), (d), or (e) — senior and very-senior executive officials *and* former Members of Congress — for **one year** from representing "a foreign entity before any officer or employee of any department or agency" or from "aid[ing] or advis[ing] a foreign entity with the intent to influence a decision" of U.S. officials; "foreign entity" is defined by reference to FARA's "government of a foreign country" and "foreign political party." Under § 207(f)(2), the U.S. Trade Representative and Deputy USTR are barred "at any time after the termination of that person's service" — i.e., **a lifetime foreign-representation ban already exists in Title 18 for the trade office.** Principle 2 therefore extends an existing federal principle from one office to all senior offices, and lengthens a one-year rule to life; it does not invent a new category. (The memo's statement that FARA is disclosure-only is correct as to FARA itself; the prohibition lives in § 207(f).)
- **The FARA→LDA exemption is the statutory cutout the "definition trap, foreign edition" is worried about.** Under [22 U.S.C. § 613(h)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/613), an agent of a foreign *commercial* principal who registers under the LDA is exempt from FARA — but the exemption is unavailable where the principal is a foreign government or foreign political party, and DOJ's longstanding position (restated in its Jan. 2, 2025 proposed FARA rule) is that it is also unavailable "where the principal beneficiary is a foreign government or foreign political party," with the proposed rule adding that activities for a state-owned enterprise that "promote the political or public interests of that foreign government" require FARA registration ([Morgan Lewis summary of the NPRM](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/01/dojs-latest-attempt-at-modernizing-fara-signals-greater-focus-on-compliance-and-enforcement)). The "principal beneficiary" test is the ready-made language for the implementing statute's cutout-entity definition (SOEs, sovereign funds, foundations) called for under Principle 2.
- **The post-2017 FARA enforcement surge the memo relies on was formally reversed in 2025.** On Feb. 5, 2025 Attorney General Bondi disbanded the FBI's Foreign Influence Task Force and directed that criminal FARA (and 18 U.S.C. § 951) charges be "limited to instances of alleged conduct similar to more traditional espionage by foreign government actors," with the FARA Unit to "focus on civil enforcement, regulatory initiatives, and public guidance"; in July 2025 prosecutors moved to dismiss the FARA counts against Rep. Henry Cuellar citing that memo, even as other prosecutions (Pras Michel, sentenced Nov. 2025; Linda Sun; David Rivera) continued, and a Sept. 2025 presidential memorandum (NSPM-7) then redirected FARA toward domestic-terrorism-linked NGOs ([Mayer Brown, *FARA Enforcement in 2025*](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/01/fara-enforcement-in-2025-from-the-bondi-memo-and-nspm-7-to-case-resolutions-and-quieter-administrative-developments)). Bears on International Abstraction Lesson 4: a disclosure statute is "transformative when actually prosecuted" — and prosecutorial priorities are themselves a revocable instrument. This is a second, cross-administration data point for Principle 4's non-waiver clause.
- **Executive-order ethics pledges have been self-revoked by presidents of both parties — the "revocable theater" episode is bipartisan.** Per CRS, [*Ethics Pledges and Other Executive Branch Appointee Restrictions Since 1993* (R44974, updated Apr. 28, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R44974/R44974.7.pdf): President Clinton's [E.O. 12834 (1993)](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R44974/R44974.7.pdf) imposed a **five-year** ban on lobbying one's former agency, a five-year ban on lobbying the Executive Office of the President, and a **lifetime ban on representing a foreign principal under FARA** — and Clinton revoked it himself by E.O. 13184 on **Dec. 28, 2000**, weeks before leaving office; George W. Bush issued no pledge; Obama's E.O. 13490 imposed a ban on lobbying the administration for its remainder; Trump revoked his own E.O. 13770 on Jan. 19, 2021; and "On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued an executive order to revoke E.O. 13989" (Biden's). The CRS update records no replacement pledge for the second Trump administration. For the memo's pairing discipline: the Clinton 2000 self-revocation should be cited alongside the Trump 2021 self-revocation wherever the latter appears — the 5-year-plus-lifetime-foreign template the memo proposes was *Clinton's own 1993 pledge*, which makes it hard for either flank to disown.

- **Michigan closed its door — bipartisan, and signed.** Gov. Whitmer (D) signed the Republican-sponsored package (Rep. ### 1. Federal law today

**Statutes the article would sit on top of (none of them repealed by it):**

- **[18 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/207) — the post-employment criminal statute.** Subsection (e): a former Senator may not, for **two years**, and a former Representative or elected House officer may not, for **one year**, knowingly make "any communication to or appearance before" a Member, officer, or employee of the relevant House with intent to influence official action on behalf of another; senior congressional staff (paid at 75% of Member pay for at least 60 days in the prior year) wait one year. Subsection (c): "senior" executive personnel — defined by pay level (basic pay at or above 86.5% of Executive Schedule Level II) or by specified presidential appointment authorities — wait one year before contacting their former agency; subsection (d): "very senior" personnel (Level I/II, the Vice President, and senior Executive Office staff) wait two years before communicating with any executive-branch official. Subsection (a): a **permanent** bar on former executive-branch officers and employees representing anyone on a "particular matter" involving specific parties in which they "participated personally and substantially." Subsection (f): the same senior/very-senior officials and former Members may not, for **one year**, represent, aid, or advise "a foreign entity" (a foreign government or foreign political party, by cross-reference to FARA) with intent to influence U.S. officials; the U.S. Trade Representative and Deputy USTR are barred "at any time after" leaving office. Subsection (j) lists the statutory exceptions: acting in official duties for the United States; representing a state or local government or (with a State Department certification) an international organization; uncompensated statements based on special knowledge; furnishing scientific or technological information; testimony under oath; and specified political-party and campaign activity. Violations are punished under § 216. *(This is the memo's existing citation, re-read for the subsection-level detail.)*
- **[18 U.S.C. § 216](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/216) — penalties for §§ 203–209.** Imprisonment up to one year (up to five for willful violations) or a fine or both; a civil penalty, sued for by the Attorney General, of up to $50,000 per violation "or the amount of compensation which the person received or offered for the prohibited conduct, whichever amount is greater"; and injunctive relief. This is the penalty template an enforcing statute would most plausibly extend to the article.
- **[5 C.F.R. Part 2641](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/5/part-2641) — the Office of Government Ethics regulations implementing § 207**, effective June 25, 2008: § 2641.201 (permanent bar), § 2641.204 (one-year senior), § 2641.205 (two-year very senior), § 2641.206 (foreign entities), Subpart C exceptions and waivers, and Appendix A listing positions the OGE Director has *waived* from § 207(c) and (f) coverage. That waiver mechanism is exactly the kind of executive-branch carve-out Section 4's non-waiver sentence would place beyond reach for the article's own prohibitions.
- **[Lobbying Disclosure Act, 2 U.S.C. § 1602](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/2/1602) — registration and disclosure.** "Lobbyist" means an individual employed or retained for compensation whose services include more than one lobbying contact, *except* one whose lobbying activities are "less than 20 percent of the time" spent for that client over three months (¶ 10); "lobbying contact" is a communication to a covered official on behalf of a client (¶ 8); "lobbying activities" includes "preparation and planning activities, research and other background work" intended for use in contacts (¶ 7). The LDA is disclosure-only and is untouched by the article; *Harriss* is its constitutional foundation.
- **[Foreign Agents Registration Act, 22 U.S.C. § 611](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/611) — definitions.** "Foreign principal" includes "a government of a foreign country and a foreign political party," persons outside the United States, and entities organized under foreign law or with a principal place of business abroad (subsec. (b)); "agent of a foreign principal" is anyone who acts at the order, request, or direction of a foreign principal in political activities, public-relations or political consulting, fundraising, or representation before U.S. agencies or officials, or who "agrees, consents, assumes or purports to act as" one (subsec. (c)); "government of a foreign country" reaches de facto and de jure sovereigns and their subdivisions (subsec. (e)). The FARA→LDA exemption in [22 U.S.C. § 613(h)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/613) is discussed in the 2026-08-17 review additions above.
- **[18 U.S.C. § 219](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/219) — sitting officials as foreign agents.** A Member of Congress, Delegate, Resident Commissioner, or officer or employee of the United States who "is or acts as an agent of a foreign principal required to register" under FARA, or who is a registered LDA lobbyist representing a foreign entity, is punishable by a fine, imprisonment up to two years, or both; the sole exception is a special Government employee whose agency head certifies the employment is "required in the national interest." Section 2 of the draft extends this in-office rule past the end of service, for life, for the offices it names.
- **Ethics pledges by executive order** (E.O. 12834, 13490, 13770, 13989 — see the memo's Current federal law and 2026-08-17 additions, and CRS [R44974](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44974)): each bound only the signing administration's appointees, and each was revoked or expired. As of the April 2025 CRS update no pledge is in force.

**Controlling court doctrine (as of this writing):**

- *[United States v. Harriss](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/347/612)* (1954): registration and disclosure of paid direct lobbying is constitutional. Unaffected by the article; Section 3 states expressly that the article does not require registration of anyone petitioning without compensation.
- *[United States v. Rumely](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/345/41)* (1953): "lobbying" construed to reach direct representations to Congress, not efforts to influence public opinion — the constitutional-avoidance reading that Section 1's phrase "advising others in advocating before" is drafted to move past.
- *[Nevada Commission on Ethics v. Carrigan](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/117/)* (2011): restrictions attached to the exercise of official power are not abridgments of the officeholder's speech.
- *[Autor v. Pritzker](https://legalethicsopinions.justia.com/2016/12/16/autor-v-pritzker)* (D.C. Cir. 2014) and *[Miller v. Ziegler](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca8/23-1902/23-1902-2024-07-29.html)* (8th Cir. 2024): paid lobbying treated as protected petitioning; strict scrutiny applied to a voter-approved two-year cooling-off, which fell for want of proof of a compelling anti-corruption interest and narrow tailoring. This is the line the article overrides — but only to the extent of its own text: a later amendment controls an earlier one where they conflict, and the First Amendment continues to govern everything the article does not itself prohibit (see § 3(f) below).
- *[City of Boerne v. Flores](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-2074.ZO.html)* (1997): enforcement legislation under an amendment's enforcement clause must be "congruen[t] and proportiona[l]" to the violations the amendment itself defines; "Congress does not enforce a constitutional right by changing what the right is." Governs how far the Section 4 statute may go in either direction (see § 3(d), (f)).
- *[Seila Law LLC v. CFPB](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/19-7)* (2020): the President may remove a single agency head at will under Article II; for-cause protection survives only for multimember expert bodies (*Humphrey's Executor*) and inferior officers with limited duties (*Morrison*). Bears directly on Section 4's "officer whose independence is protected by law" (see § 3(f)).

**Constitutional text the article extends:** the Foreign Emoluments Clause (Art. I § 9 cl. 8: no officer may accept a present, emolument, office, or title from a foreign state without the consent of Congress) governs officers *while in office*; Section 2 carries the principle past the exit door for the offices it names.

### 2. State law today

- **Counts.** NCSL's 50-state table of legislator revolving-door provisions ([*Legislator Revolving Door Prohibitions*, updated Jan. 15, 2025](https://www.ncsl.org/ethics/legislator-revolving-door-prohibitions)) states that "most states set mandatory waiting periods before a legislator may register as a lobbyist or engage in lobbying activities," that "the length of these waiting periods generally varies between six months and two years," and that "Florida's six-year waiting period is the longest and was enacted through a ballot measure." The table lists two-year waits in Alabama, Colorado, Iowa, Kentucky, Missouri (annotated as held unconstitutional in *Miller v. Ziegler*), New York, North Dakota, and South Dakota (and Guam); one year in most other states; six months in Illinois and North Carolina; and "no statute located" for twelve states (Arkansas, Idaho, Kansas, Louisiana, Minnesota, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Hampshire, Oklahoma, Texas, Washington, Wyoming). *Caveat: the NCSL table has no row for Montana or Nevada, and Michigan's row predates the July 2026 statute — so its "no statute" list is not a clean count.* Public Citizen's older tally ([*Revolving Door Restrictions by State*, Aug. 9, 2019](https://www.citizen.org/article/revolving-door-restrictions-by-state-2019/); advocacy source, pro-reform) put it at 33 states with a policy of one year or less, "more than a dozen" with at least two years for some or all officials, and 7 with none. Michigan added a two-year wait for former legislators, governors, lieutenant governors, and department heads, effective Jan. 1, 2027 ([Detroit Free Press via Yahoo News, July 28, 2026](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/lobbying-ban-tackles-one-gap-141934152.html)).
- **State constitutional provisions.** At least four states carry the rule in the constitution itself: Florida (Art. II § 8(f), six years, [Amendment 12 of 2018](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_Amendment_12,_Lobbying_Restrictions_Amendment_(2018))); Missouri (Art. III § 2, two years, [Amendment 1 of 2018](https://ballotpedia.org/Missouri_Amendment_1,_Lobbying,_Campaign_Finance,_and_Redistricting_Initiative_(2018)) — struck under the First Amendment in *Miller*); Colorado (Const. Art. XXIX § 4, two years); and North Dakota (Const. art. XIV, two years) — the latter two per the NCSL table.
- **Conflicting state law: none identified.** The draft regulates former *federal* officers and compensated advocacy before *federal* institutions, and Section 2 concerns influence on "any policy or action of the United States." No state cooling-off, registration, or in-office rule regulates that conduct, so no state law becomes unenforceable on ratification. State revolving-door provisions remain what they are today: state law regulating state officials, subject to the same First Amendment challenge that succeeded in *Miller* — the article does not itself alter the constitutional analysis of state bans (see § 3(f)). Section 3's registration sentence is a rule of construction *for this article* ("Nothing in this article shall be construed … to require the registration of any person petitioning without compensation"); it does not repeal or preempt any state statute that requires unpaid lobbyists to register under state authority.

### 3. The day after ratification

**(a) What becomes unenforceable or moot.** Nothing on the federal statute books directly conflicts with the article; the existing rules are shorter and narrower, not opposite. 18 U.S.C. § 207 therefore stays enforceable as a statutory floor beneath the constitutional rule; the LDA and FARA registration regimes, § 207(a)'s permanent particular-matter bar, and § 219's in-office foreign-agent ban all stand. Two categories of *executive-branch action* become inoperative as against the article on certification, without further legislation, by force of Section 4's last clause ("no provision of this article may be waived, exempted, or suspended by executive order or by rule of either House"): any executive order purporting to waive or exempt a covered person from Sections 1–2 (the ethics-pledge waiver practice), and any House or Senate rule doing the same. OGE's authority to waive positions from § 207(c)/(f) (5 C.F.R. Part 2641, App. A) continues to operate on the *statute*, but cannot narrow the article's own coverage of an office that Congress has defined as "senior" by law. Whether § 207(j)'s statutory exceptions can be carried into the enforcing statute is a litigation question — Section 1 states only two exceptions (own-behalf petitioning and uncompensated petitioning), and *Boerne* forbids enforcement legislation from "changing what the right is."

**(b) What stands.** Everything in § 1 above except as just described. In particular *Harriss* disclosure law, FARA, and the LDA's 20% registration trigger are untouched — the memo's "inherently statutory" list already assigns LDA/FARA modernization to ordinary legislation.

**(c) The draft's own transition, enforcement, and effective-date clauses.** The draft has **no effective-date clause and no grandfather clause**; it takes effect on certification. **Section 4** is the enforcement clause, and it is phrased as a duty rather than a power: "The Congress *shall* enforce this article by appropriate legislation, which *shall* provide civil and criminal penalties and *shall* vest investigative authority in an officer whose independence is protected by law." The same section supplies the self-executing non-waiver rule quoted in (a). Sections 1 and 2 are flat prohibitions as to the offices they name (Senators and Representatives in § 1; President, Vice President, Senators, Representatives, judges of the United States, and general and flag officers in § 2) and apply on certification to those persons; as to "officer[s] of the United States in a senior position *defined by law*," both sections depend on a statutory definition. Section 3 is a rule of construction and applies at once. Because the draft is silent on persons who left office before ratification, the general rule from the ratification memo governs: silence is resolved by the courts (compare *Hollingsworth*'s application of the Eleventh Amendment to pending cases with the Twenty-second Amendment's express exemption of the sitting President). The open questions are stated in (f).

**(d) Enabling legislation Congress would need, and what existing bills look like.** Section 4 obliges Congress to pass a statute doing at least four things: (1) define the "senior position[s]" covered by Sections 1 and 2 — the ready-made candidates are § 207(c)'s pay-level test (86.5% of Executive Schedule Level II) and § 207(d)'s "very senior" tier, and the LDA's "covered executive branch official" list; (2) provide civil and criminal penalties — § 216's schedule (one year / five years willful; civil penalty of $50,000 or the compensation received) is the existing template; (3) create or designate an investigative officer "whose independence is protected by law"; and (4) define the perimeter of "advocating before, or advising others in advocating before" and of "agent of a foreign government or foreign political party" — FARA § 611(b)–(e) supplies the foreign-principal vocabulary. Until (1) is enacted, the executive-officer tier of Sections 1–2 has no defined membership (see (f)). Bills already on file that resemble pieces of this statute: the [BLAST Act (S. 4524 / H.R. 9130, 2026)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/4524/text), whose § 2(a) triggers on either LDA registration or a knowing influencing communication and whose penalties are § 216's by cross-reference rather than a schedule of its own — a lifetime rule, limited to lobbying Congress, that would need to be re-scaled to five years and broadened to the executive branch; the [PURE Executive Act (H.R. 8544)](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr8544.xml) (five-year agency ban plus lifetime foreign-government ban for senior executive officials); the [Close the Revolving Door Act (H.R. 3554, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3554/text); and the Public Service Integrity Act (H.R. 414) and Cleaning Up Washington's Act (S. 1158) five-year extensions listed under Current bills above. None of them creates an independent investigator of the kind Section 4 requires; that piece would be new drafting. Congress could enact the enforcing statute in advance with a clause making it effective on certification (the contingent-statute mechanism in the ratification memo § 4); no such bill was located.

**(e) State trigger or conforming legislation.** Because the article regulates federal officers before federal institutions, states have nothing they must conform. A state legislature *could* enact a statute now that by its terms takes effect on certification (the ordinary contingent-effective-date mechanism); the plausible subjects would be housekeeping — cross-references to the new federal definitions in state lobbyist-registration codes — rather than substance. No state trigger bill on this subject was located, and none is cited.

**(f) Litigation questions the wording leaves to the courts.** Stated candidly, in rough order of consequence:

1. **Retroactivity.** With no effective-date or grandfather clause, courts must decide whether Section 1's five-year clock reaches a person who left office *before* ratification (e.g., three years out, two to run), and whether Section 2's "shall at any time" bars a former Member or general officer already registered under FARA as an agent of a foreign government on the day of certification. The text says "has served," which points toward coverage; existing-contract and fair-notice arguments point the other way.
2. **"Senior position defined by law."** Whether Sections 1–2 lie dormant for executive officers until Congress enacts a definition, or whether courts may borrow § 207(c)–(d)'s existing pay-level definitions as the "law" that defines seniority. Members of Congress and the offices Section 2 names by title are covered either way.
3. **"Advising others in advocating before."** Whether it reaches in-house government-relations executives (paid by an employer that is "any other person or entity"), lawyers advising clients on legislative strategy, paid expert testimony, and paid public commentary. *Rumely*'s direct-representation reading and Section 3's petition savings clause will be argued on both sides.
4. **"The Congress or the executive agencies of the United States."** Whether "executive agencies" tracks [5 U.S.C. § 105](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/105) ("an Executive department, a Government corporation, and an independent establishment") — and so includes independent regulatory commissions — and whether the White House and Executive Office of the President are "executive agencies." Advocacy before courts is not named and so is not covered; former officials may practice law before them.
5. **The § 207(j) exceptions.** Whether the enforcing statute may exempt representation of a state or local government, a university, or an international organization within the five years, given that Section 1 names only own-behalf and uncompensated petitioning as exceptions and *Boerne* limits enforcement legislation to what the amendment defines. A former Representative hired as a state's Washington liaison, or a university president lobbying for federal grants, is inside Section 1's literal terms.
6. **Section 2's perimeter.** Whether "foreign government or foreign political party" reaches state-owned enterprises, sovereign wealth funds, and government-funded foundations (FARA's "principal beneficiary" practice, discussed in the 2026-08-17 additions, is the closest existing test), and whether "receive compensation from … for the purpose of influencing" covers indirect payment structures. The limiting phrase "for the purpose of influencing any policy or action of the United States" is the text a court would apply to academics, dual nationals, and employees of foreign private companies.
7. **"An officer whose independence is protected by law."** Whether Section 4 authorizes for-cause removal protection for a single investigating officer notwithstanding *Seila Law*'s reading of Article II — the amendment being later in time and specific — or whether Congress must satisfy the requirement through a multimember body or an inferior officer with limited duties (*Humphrey's Executor* / *Morrison* territory), and what "protected by law" minimally requires.
8. **Enforcement absent legislation.** Whether Sections 1–2 can be applied by courts (e.g., in a suit to void a contract, or as a defense) before Congress passes the Section 4 statute, whether anyone has standing to compel Congress to act on its "shall enforce" duty, and whether § 216's penalties can be borrowed by implication (the general rule is that criminal penalties require a statute).
9. **The First Amendment residue.** The article displaces *Miller*-style strict scrutiny only for what the article itself prohibits. Any statutory reach beyond its terms — a longer wait, a broader class of offices than "senior," restrictions on uncompensated advocacy — remains fully exposed to the Petition Clause. Relatedly, state cooling-offs get no direct shelter: whether ratification changes how courts weigh the anti-corruption interest in cases like *Miller* is an argument by analogy, not a rule.
10. **"Rule of either House."** Whether the non-waiver clause bars an Ethics Committee advisory opinion or informal practice, as opposed to a formal House or Senate rule, from exempting a former Member.
