# Pardon Reform Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

This document supports **Target Category 8 (Limited Presidential Pardon Reform)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md) and is a companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). The framing decision made at the category level governs everything below: this is **transparency-and-public-benefit-led reform**, not a self-pardon-ban-led amendment. A self-pardon ban polls as an anti-Trump measure and collapses inside the incumbent's party; a "no president of either party may use mercy as personal property" frame does not. The abuse record here is deliberately balanced — Clinton, Trump (both terms), and Biden — because the amendment's only viable pitch is that it binds **all future presidents**, including ones the reader likes.

**The headline findings:**

1. **The durable bipartisan core is narrow but real: family/self-benefit bans plus transparency.** Banning pardons that benefit the president's family polls at 77% (64R/88D) and pardons of staff/associates at 69% (50R/84D) in [Protect Democracy's 2024 polling](https://protectdemocracy.org/) *(advocacy source — pro-reform organization; numbers should be independently replicated before use in materials)*, while a generic "limit the pardon power" amendment gets only 56% (34R/76D, Dec 2025) because it reads as aimed at the sitting president. See [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md).
2. **Both parties' recent pardons were unpopular with the public — including each party's own voters.** The Hunter Biden pardon drew ~32% overall approval; Trump's associate pardons ~29%; pardons of violent January 6 offenders drew 83% disapproval ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). The public is already where the amendment needs it to be; elites are the lagging indicator.
3. **A public-justification requirement has never been directly polled.** This is the single most important polling gap for the foundation to fill — it is the least "aimed at anyone" provision in the package and plausibly the highest-consensus one.
4. **The US is the comparative outlier.** No peer democracy gives one person unreviewable, uncounseled, unexplained clemency power. Germany and Italy require ministerial countersignature; India and South Africa subject clemency to judicial review for bad faith; the UK routes mercy through a cabinet minister answerable to Parliament; France abolished collective pardons in 2008. State governors are even more constrained: Georgia's governor has **no** pardon power at all.
5. **The strongest evidence base is the states.** Roughly half of US states condition or remove the governor's clemency power — board-exclusive (Georgia, Idaho, Utah, Nebraska), board-recommendation-gated (Pennsylvania, Texas, Delaware, Florida), or transparency-mandated (New York, California require every act reported to the legislature with reasons). The board-gated states are conspicuously absent from the pardon-scandal record; the scandal states (Tennessee 1979, Kentucky 2019) are governor-alone states.
6. **Two traps can kill this amendment: incumbent-coding and mercy-destruction.** Any draft that reads as targeting the current president loses ~40 points of his party (34R on the generic amendment). And any draft that proceduralizes clemency into paralysis worsens the existing clemency *drought* for ordinary applicants — only 11% of first-term Trump grants went through the Pardon Attorney, but the deeper scandal is the [thousands of ordinary petitions that sit unanswered for years](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-statistics).

## Amendment Framework

### Core provisions

The research below supports a package built in this order (most consensual first):

1. **Transparency / public justification.** Every act of clemency must be published — recipient, offense, and the president's written justification — and transmitted to Congress. Modeled directly on [California Const. art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V) and [New York Const. art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution), which have required exactly this of governors for over a century without impairing mercy.
2. **Categorical self-benefit exclusions.** No pardon of the president by himself or herself; no pardon of the president's spouse or close relatives; no pardon granted in exchange for anything of value (with express confirmation that the bribery laws reach the pardon power — codifying what [DOJ already treated as prosecutable in its 2020 pardon-bribery investigation](https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/01/politics/pardon-investigation-doj/index.html)).
3. **Individual-naming requirement.** Clemency must name the person and specify the offenses covered — no blanket categorical pardons of unnamed persons, and no open-ended preemptive pardons for "any offenses" over a decade. This targets the Hunter Biden 2014–2024 blanket, the Biden preemptive pardons, and the January 6 mass grant symmetrically.
4. **(Harder tier) Coordination exclusion.** No pardon for offenses committed at the direction of, or in coordination with, the president — the Cohen-amendment core ([H.J.Res.13, 119th Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13)).
5. **(Optional, historically grounded) Congressional disapproval.** Mondale's 1974 mechanism: a supermajority of both houses may void a pardon within a fixed window ([S.J.Res.241, 93rd Cong.](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241)).

### Challenges

- **The doctrine is maximalist.** [*Ex parte Garland* (1866)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/71/333/) calls the power "unlimited," reaching "every offence known to the law," and holds it "cannot be fettered by any legislative restrictions"; [*Schick v. Reed* (1974)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/419/256/) confirms it "cannot be modified, abridged, or diminished by the Congress." Statutory reform of the power's *scope* is therefore constitutionally dead on arrival — which is precisely the argument for an amendment, but also means there is no incremental statutory proof-of-concept for the exclusions (transparency and bribery statutes are the exceptions; see **Inherently statutory**).
- **Every federal proposal since 1974 has died without a floor vote** (Mondale's included), and the one bill that did pass a chamber — the [Protecting Our Democracy Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/5314) — passed the House 220-208 with a single Republican, proving how easily pardon reform gets absorbed into a partisan omnibus.
- **Line-drawing on "family" and "associates"** is genuinely hard (degrees of kinship; who counts as a campaign employee), and the "coordination" and "corrupt purpose" provisions raise justiciability problems (who decides a pardon was corrupt, and when?).
- **The mercy function is already broken in the other direction.** Clemency for ordinary applicants is in a decades-long drought; reform that adds friction without fixing supply will be attacked — fairly — by criminal-justice reformers on the left and right (the [Barkow/Osler critique](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2479595)).

### Strategic read

Lead with **transparency and the public-benefit principle**, framed as *"the pardon belongs to the public, not the president's family and donors."* Every messaging artifact should pair one Democratic and one Republican abuse in the same sentence (Marc Rich + Charles Kushner; Hunter Biden + January 6). The self-pardon ban rides *inside* the self-benefit provision rather than leading the package — it is the same principle (no one may be the judge and the beneficiary of his own mercy, per the [1974 OLC memo](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president)) but framed as anti-corruption rather than anti-Trump. The state models are the persuasion centerpiece for Republican audiences: red-state constitutions (Georgia, Texas, Utah, Idaho, Nebraska) are the *most* restrictive in the country, so conditioning clemency is a conservative governance tradition, not a progressive innovation. Commission the missing poll on public-justification first.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

### Constitutional doctrine — what the power is, and what it already is not

Article II, §2, cl. 1: the President "shall have Power to grant Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment."

- **Scope near-plenary.** [*Ex parte Garland* (1866)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/71/333/): the power "extends to every offence known to the law, and may be exercised at any time after its commission, either before legal proceedings are taken or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment" and "is not subject to legislative control." [*United States v. Klein* (1871)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/80/128/): Congress cannot direct courts to treat pardons as inoperative or as proof of guilt.
- **Conditions are permitted.** [*Schick v. Reed* (1974)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/419/256/): the president may attach conditions (there, commuting a death sentence to life without parole); the power "flows from the Constitution alone." Conditional clemency is thus already constitutional practice — relevant precedent for an amendment that itself conditions the power.
- **Acceptance implies guilt.** [*Burdick v. United States* (1915)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/236/79/): a pardon may be rejected, and it "carries an imputation of guilt; acceptance a confession of it." (Useful framing: preemptive pardons brand their recipients.)
- **Existing hard limits.** Federal offenses only — pardons cannot reach **state crimes** (Bannon and Manafort both faced New York prosecution after federal pardons) — and cannot be used "in Cases of Impeachment."
- **The open self-pardon question.** Never tested. The Office of Legal Counsel's [August 5, 1974 memorandum](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president) (Mary Lawton, days before Nixon's resignation): "Under the fundamental rule that no one may be a judge in his own case, the President cannot pardon himself." An OLC memo binds no court; an amendment would settle it.
- **Bribery-for-pardon is already prosecutable.** DOJ has treated selling a pardon as bribery of an official act — a [2020 unsealed court filing revealed an active DOJ investigation into a pardon-for-money scheme](https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/01/politics/pardon-investigation-doj/index.html), and the Clinton-era Rich pardon drew a federal criminal investigation. The amendment's quid-pro-quo provision constitutionalizes and clarifies this rather than inventing it — important for answering "isn't this already illegal?"

### The Abuse Record (both parties — this balance is the argument)

**Clinton (2001) — the paradigmatic sale-adjacent pardon.** On his final morning, Clinton pardoned fugitive financier **Marc Rich**, whose ex-wife Denise Rich had given [over $1 million to Democratic causes and $450,000 to the Clinton presidential library](https://www.propublica.org/article/questions-for-potential-ag-holder-on-clinton-pardon-1202). Clinton took the extraordinary step of defending himself in a [New York Times op-ed, "My Reasons for the Pardons" (Feb. 18, 2001)](https://abcnews.go.com/Politics/story?id=121773&page=1), insisting "there was no quid pro quo"; federal prosecutors in New York nonetheless [opened a criminal investigation](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/clinton-pardon-under-fire/) (closed without charges). The Rich pardon bypassed the Justice Department entirely and remains the template for why *process transparency* — not just outcome bans — matters.

**Trump, first term (2017–21) — associates and family-adjacent grants.** Commutation and then pardon of **Roger Stone** (convicted of lying to Congress in the Russia investigation), pardons of **Paul Manafort** and **Michael Flynn** (both convicted in matters touching the president's own campaign and administration), **Steve Bannon** (pardoned mid-prosecution for defrauding Trump's own donors in "We Build the Wall"), and **Charles Kushner** (his son-in-law's father, later appointed ambassador to France). Per [Lawfare's analysis of DOJ records](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/trump-and-pardon-attorney), only **25 of 238 first-term grants (11%) came through the Pardon Attorney** — a historic low that turned the end-run into the norm.

**Biden (2024–25) — the family precedent crosses party lines.** On December 1, 2024, after repeated public pledges not to, Biden granted **Hunter Biden** a "full and unconditional" pardon covering *any* federal offenses committed **2014–2024** — a decade-wide blanket, approved by only ~32% of the public ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). In his final hours (January 2025), Biden issued **preemptive pardons** to five family members (his brothers, sister, and their spouses) plus Anthony Fauci, Gen. Mark Milley, and January 6 committee members — pardons for people charged with nothing, criticized across the spectrum, including by Democratic senators, as normalizing exactly the tool a future president could abuse. These grants are what make a family-pardon ban a *both-parties* provision rather than an anti-Trump one.

**Trump, second term (2025–) — mass, transactional, and personal-benefit-adjacent.** On January 20, 2025, blanket clemency for **~1,500 January 6 defendants**, including those convicted of assaulting police — the 83%-disapproval grant ([POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md)). October 23, 2025: pardon of Binance founder **Changpeng Zhao ("CZ")**, whose exchange [closely supported the Trump family's World Liberty Financial crypto venture](https://www.cnn.com/2025/10/23/politics/binance-founder-trump-pardon); Trump said he acted "at the request of a lot of very good people," and Senate Banking Democrats [demanded a DOJ/Treasury accounting](https://www.banking.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/letter_to_doj_treasury_re_binance_pardon.pdf). December 1, 2025: pardon of former Honduran president **Juan Orlando Hernández**, 18 months into a 45-year US cocaine-trafficking sentence ([CRS analysis](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12621)), while the administration waged a military campaign against Latin American traffickers. December 3, 2025: pardon of sitting Democratic Rep. **Henry Cuellar** and his wife on the eve of their bribery trial — followed by Trump's public complaint of Cuellar's ["lack of LOYALTY"](https://www.cnn.com/2025/12/03/politics/henry-cuellar-trump-pardon) when Cuellar filed for reelection as a Democrat, as candid a statement of the transactional theory of clemency as exists in the record. See also the [Christian Science Monitor's synthesis of how 2025 clemency "broke the mold"](https://www.csmonitor.com/USA/Politics/2025/1209/trump-pardons-cuellar-hernandez).

The record's shape across four presidencies: **the abuse is structural, not partisan.** Every messaging document should use it in balanced pairs.

### Prior Amendment Proposals (with history)

- **Post-Watergate (93rd Congress, 1974).** The Ford–Nixon pardon triggered the first serious wave. **Sen. Walter Mondale's [S.J.Res.241](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241)** would have voided any pardon disapproved by **two-thirds of both Houses within 180 days** of issuance — clemency subject to a legislative check, mirroring the veto-override structure. It received hearings but never reached a floor vote; the Senate's only recorded action in the episode was a nonbinding sense-of-the-Senate resolution opposing further Watergate pardons before trial. A parallel House proposal, [H.J.Res.1145](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/house-joint-resolution/1145), would have limited pardons to post-conviction absent congressional concurrence. Lesson: even at peak public fury over a pardon, an amendment perceived as aimed at one act stalled.
- **Rep. Steve Cohen's serial amendments (2017–present)** — the modern standard text. Introduced in every Congress since October 2017: [H.J.Res.120 (115th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/house-joint-resolution/120), [H.J.Res.8 (116th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/house-joint-resolution/8/text), [H.J.Res.4 (117th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-joint-resolution/4/text), [H.J.Res.77 (118th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-joint-resolution/77), [H.J.Res.13 (119th, Jan. 9, 2025)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13). The text prohibits self-pardons and pardons of the president's **family members, administration officials, and paid campaign employees**; bars pardons for offenses motivated by a "direct and significant personal interest" of the president or those close to him, or committed **at the direction of or in coordination with** the president; and voids pardons issued for a **corrupt purpose** ([Cohen's summary](https://cohen.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/congressman-cohen-will-reintroduce-constitutional-amendment-limit)). History: never a hearing, never a markup, no Republican cosponsors of note — a Democratic-caucus artifact, which is exactly the packaging failure the foundation exists to avoid.
- **[Protecting Our Democracy Act, H.R. 5314 (117th)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/5314)** — the statutory high-water mark. Passed the House **220-208 on Dec. 9, 2021** with one Republican (Kinzinger); died in the Senate. Pardon provisions: required DOJ and the White House to **submit pardon-related materials to Congress** for self-, family-, and obstruction-adjacent pardons; declared self-pardons invalid; and clarified that the **federal bribery statute covers pardons and commutations as "official acts."** Why it read partisan despite popular components: it was an omnibus of Trump-era grievances (emoluments, Hatch Act, subpoena enforcement, IG protections) passed near-party-line eleven months after Trump left office — a case study in how good provisions inherit the coding of their vehicle. Sen. Blumenthal's standalone [Pardon Transparency and Accountability Act](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/video-blumenthal-introduces-pardon-transparency-and-accountability-act) carries the disclosure piece forward but faces the *Garland* problem for anything beyond disclosure.
- **The process-reform track (statutory, complementary).** The [Office of the Pardon Attorney](https://www.justice.gov/pardon) — the regularized application pipeline inside DOJ — has been systematically bypassed: 11% usage in Trump's first term ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/trump-and-pardon-attorney)), with recent grants overwhelmingly originating from White House and political channels. Meanwhile [thousands of ordinary petitions languish](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-statistics). The leading academic reformer is **NYU's Rachel Barkow** (with Mark Osler): their ["Restructuring Clemency"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2479595) proposes an **independent, bipartisan clemency board outside DOJ** reporting directly to the president — removing the institutional conflict of prosecutors reviewing mercy for their own convictions; Barkow's [2022 House testimony](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/JU/JU08/20220519/114782/HHRG-117-JU08-Wstate-BarkowR-20220519.pdf) documented that 63% of pending FY2020 petitions were stalled at the Deputy AG or White House Counsel. This is the answer to the "mercy drought" objection — but it is statutory/executive-order territory, not amendment text.

## State Models

State constitutions are the richest source of tested designs. Taxonomy, from most to least constrained:

**Model 1 — Board-exclusive (governor has little or no clemency power).** About nine states. **Georgia** is the pure case: [Const. art. IV, §II](https://law.justia.com/constitution/georgia/conart4.html) vests the **State Board of Pardons and Paroles** — not the governor — with the full clemency power ("the powers to grant reprieves, pardons, and paroles; to commute penalties; [and] to remove disabilities imposed by law"). Georgia's governor cannot pardon anyone, even in death cases. The design dates to a **1943 constitutional amendment passed after a governor's pardon-selling scandal** — ratified by the voters of a deep-red-tradition state ([Georgia Board history](https://pap.georgia.gov/about)). **Idaho** (Commission of Pardons and Parole), **Utah** (Board of Pardons and Parole, Const. art. VII, §12), and **Nebraska** ([Const. art. IV, §13](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/articles.php?article=IV-13): clemency vested in a **Board of Pardons** consisting of the Governor, Attorney General, and Secretary of State, acting by majority — the governor holds one vote of three) follow the same logic. Connecticut and South Carolina likewise vest pardons in boards.

**Model 2 — Board-recommendation-gated (governor acts only on a board's affirmative recommendation).** The exact constitutional texts are the foundation's best drafting precedents:

- **Pennsylvania**, [Const. art. IV, §9](https://codes.findlaw.com/pa/constitution-of-the-commonwealth-of-pennsylvania/pa-const-art-4-sect-9.html): *"No pardon shall be granted, nor sentence commuted, except on the recommendation in writing of a majority of the Board of Pardons, and in the case of a sentence of death or life imprisonment, on the unanimous recommendation in writing of the Board of Pardons, after full hearing in open session, upon due public notice. The recommendation, with the reasons therefor at length, shall be delivered to the Governor and a copy thereof shall be kept on file in the office of the Lieutenant Governor in a docket kept for that purpose."* — gating, open hearings, public notice, and written reasons in one clause.
- **Texas**, [Const. art. IV, §11](https://law.justia.com/constitution/texas/sections/cn000400-001100.html): *"In all criminal cases, except treason and impeachment, the Governor shall have power, after conviction, on the written signed recommendation and advice of the Board of Pardons and Paroles, or a majority thereof, to grant reprieves and commutations of punishment and pardons…"* The governor of **Texas** — the state most rhetorically committed to executive strength — cannot pardon without the board, and only **after conviction** (no preemptive pardons at all).
- **Delaware**, [Const. art. VII](https://delcode.delaware.gov/constitution/constitution-08.shtml): pardons only "upon the recommendation in writing of a majority of the Board of Pardons," with the recommendation *"with the reasons therefor at length"* filed with the Secretary of State, and the governor required to *"fully set forth in writing the grounds of all reprieves, pardons and remissions, to be entered in a register... and laid before the General Assembly."*
- **Florida** requires the governor plus two cabinet members; **Oklahoma** and **Louisiana** also gate on board recommendation.

**Model 3 — Transparency/reporting-mandated (governor free to act, but must publicly account).** The direct precedent for the amendment's lead provision:

- **California**, [Const. art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V): *"The Governor shall report to the Legislature each reprieve, pardon, and commutation granted, stating the pertinent facts and the reasons for granting it."* Section 8(b) adds a striking check: no pardon of a twice-convicted felon *"except on recommendation of the Supreme Court, 4 judges concurring."*
- **New York**, [Const. art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution): the governor *"shall annually communicate to the legislature each case of reprieve, commutation or pardon granted, stating the name of the convict, the crime of which he or she was convicted, the sentence and its date, and the date of the commutation, pardon or reprieve."*
- New Jersey and other states impose similar annual reporting with reasons; see the [Collateral Consequences Resource Center's 50-state comparison](https://ccresourcecenter.org/state-restoration-profiles/50-state-comparisoncharacteristics-of-pardon-authorities-2/) for the full map.

**Model 4 — Unconditioned governor-alone.** A minority of states — and the home of essentially every modern state pardon scandal: Tennessee's Gov. Ray Blanton **cash-for-clemency scandal (1979)**, which forced his successor's early swearing-in; Kentucky's Gov. Matt Bevin **lame-duck pardon spree (2019)**, including the brother of a fundraiser (recipient later convicted federally of the same murder), which prompted Kentucky legislative proposals to bar election-to-inauguration pardons. **The correlation is the argument: board-gated and reporting states are conspicuously absent from the scandal record; the scandals cluster where one person decides alone, in secret, on the way out the door.** The federal pardon power is a Model 4 power — the least constrained clemency design in American law, state or federal.

## International Models

### Germany — countersignature and delegation

[Basic Law art. 60(2)](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/englisch_gg/englisch_gg.html): the Federal President "shall exercise the power to pardon offenders on behalf of the Federation **in individual cases**." Under the dominant reading of art. 58 — *"Orders and directions of the Federal President shall require for their validity the countersignature of the Federal Chancellor or of the competent Federal Minister"* — pardons, not being among art. 58's enumerated exceptions, require **ministerial countersignature**; in practice the President has delegated most federal clemency to the Federal Ministry of Justice, and most crimes fall under *Land* (state) clemency anyway. German presidential pardons are individual, counseled, rare, and have never functioned as political self-protection.

### Italy — the countersignature conflict, resolved

[Const. arts. 87 and 89](https://www.senato.it/documenti/repository/istituzione/costituzione_inglese.pdf): the President "may grant pardons and commute punishments," but no presidential act is valid unless countersigned by the proposing minister. When a justice minister refused to process a pardon, the Constitutional Court's [judgment no. 200/2006](https://www.cortecostituzionale.it/) resolved the standoff: the pardon is a **substantially presidential act** with an exclusively **humanitarian purpose** — the minister cannot block it on political grounds and is obliged to countersign, but the process still runs through the ministry's file, ensuring a documented, counseled decision. Two institutions must touch every pardon; neither can use it politically.

### India — clemency on advice, reviewable for bad faith

[Const. art. 72](https://www.india.gov.in/my-government/constitution-india) vests pardon power in the President, exercised — like all presidential powers under art. 74 — **on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**. The Supreme Court in [*Epuru Sudhakar v. Govt. of Andhra Pradesh* (2006)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/758562/) held clemency **judicially reviewable** — not on the merits, but where the order shows *"non-application of mind,"* is *"mala fide,"* rests on *"extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations,"* or is arbitrary — and quashed a remission granted on political/party grounds. A pardon granted as a political favor is, in the world's largest democracy, simply void.

### United Kingdom — mercy as a ministerial, accountable act

The royal prerogative of mercy is exercised **on the advice of the Justice Secretary** (in practice, decided by the minister, answerable to Parliament) — the sovereign personally decides nothing. After the Birmingham Six and related miscarriages, the Criminal Appeal Act 1995 created the [Criminal Cases Review Commission](https://ccrc.gov.uk/) (1997), an independent body that refers doubtful convictions back to the courts — institutionalizing the pardon's core legitimate function (correcting injustice) and shrinking free pardons to near-extinction. The UK's lesson: build the *machinery* of mercy and the discretionary act withers honestly.

### France — the 2008 narrowing

[Const. art. 17](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr/en/constitution-of-4-october-1958), as amended by the [constitutional law of 23 July 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_constitutional_law_of_23_July_2008): the President "has the right to grant **individual** pardons" — the reform **abolished collective pardons** (the traditional Bastille Day mass grants). Under art. 19 the act is countersigned by the Prime Minister and Justice Minister. A modern peer democracy looked at *mass, categorical clemency* and constitutionally banned it — direct precedent for an individual-naming requirement.

### South Africa — pardons under, not above, the constitution

Pardon power sits in [Const. §84(2)(j)](https://www.justice.gov.za/constitution/SAConstitution-web-eng.pdf). In [*President v. Hugo* (1997)](http://www.saflii.org/za/cases/ZACC/1997/4.html), the Constitutional Court held the pardon power is **subject to the Constitution and judicially reviewable**; in *Albutt v. Centre for the Study of Violence and Reconciliation* (2010) it went further — a special pardon process for political offenders was invalid because **victims had a right to be heard** first. Clemency as a public, procedurally fair act rather than a private favor.

**The comparative norm:** across every peer democracy surveyed, clemency is at least one of — counseled (ministerial advice/countersignature), individual, explained, or reviewable for bad faith. The United States federal pardon is **none of the four**. The US is not preserving an ancient norm; it is the outlier that dropped every safeguard the tradition evolved.

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Two-key systems don't kill mercy; they kill sale.** Germany and Italy have had countersignature for 75 years with no clemency paralysis and no pardon-selling scandal. The second signature's function is evidentiary and deliberative, not obstructive (Italy's Court even *ordered* the minister to sign).
2. **"Individual cases only" is a tested constitutional phrase.** France 2008 and Germany art. 60(2) both use it. It surgically removes blanket/categorical clemency while leaving every act of individual mercy intact.
3. **Review for bad faith is separable from review on the merits.** India and South Africa police only *mala fides*, non-application of mind, and irrelevant considerations — courts never reweigh whether mercy was deserved. That line has held for decades and answers the "courts will run clemency" objection.
4. **Institutionalized mercy outperforms discretionary mercy at mercy's own job.** The UK's CCRC corrects more miscarriages than free pardons ever did; Georgia's board grants far more pardons per capita than recent presidents. Constraining the *political* pardon and building *administrative* clemency are complements, not trade-offs.
5. **Humanitarian-purpose language does real work.** Italy's Court confined the pardon to humanitarian ends, which is what stripped the minister's political veto *and* the president's political use in one move. Purpose clauses are enforceable.
6. **Advice requirements convert clemency from a personal act into a governmental act.** UK/India-style "on advice" models make some minister own every pardon on the record — accountability without any ban at all. The weakest intervention with a track record.
7. **Victims and the public are recognized stakeholders in clemency elsewhere.** South Africa's *Albutt* (victims must be heard) and Pennsylvania's "full hearing in open session, upon due public notice" converge from opposite hemispheres on the same principle: mercy done in secret is presumptively suspect.
8. **No democracy trusts the pardon to protect the pardoner.** Self-protective clemency (self, family, co-conspirators) is either structurally impossible (board systems, ministerial systems) or voidable (mala fides review) everywhere surveyed. The US debate over whether a president may pardon his own co-conspirators would be unintelligible in any peer system.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. The four below clear it — public justification (never directly polled, but the least incumbent-coded provision, backed by a century of California/New York constitutional practice — commission the poll), the family ban (77%, 64R/88D), the sold-pardon ban (codifies existing bribery law; corruption is a "big problem" for 92%, 90R/93D), and individual naming (both parties' blanket pardons drew majority disapproval from both parties' voters). Three candidates are held back at the end.*

1. **Public justification — no secret mercy.**
   Proposed formulation: *"Every reprieve or pardon granted shall be published, together with the name of the recipient, the offenses covered, and a statement of the reasons for the grant, and shall be reported to Congress. No reprieve or pardon shall take effect until so published."*
   - Direct lineage: [California art. V, §8(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=V) ("stating the pertinent facts and the reasons for granting it") and [New York art. IV, §4](https://www.nysenate.gov/new-york-state-constitution); over a century of state practice with zero evidence of chilling legitimate clemency. Also the never-polled provision — commission the poll.
   - ⚠️ **Mercy-destruction trap:** the requirement must be a *disclosure* condition, not an *approval* condition — no official may sit in judgment of the reasons' adequacy, or the clause becomes a heckler's veto and deepens the clemency drought. Draft so a boilerplate reason satisfies the clause; its power is publicity, not review.

2. **No family clemency.**
   Proposed formulation: *"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to his or her spouse, or to any relative of the President or the President's spouse within the third degree of kinship, or to the spouse of any such relative."*
   - The strongest-polling provision in the package — 77% (64R/88D) — with fresh precedents from **both** parties (Hunter Biden and the January 2025 Biden family pardons; Charles Kushner). Degree-of-kinship language is standard in state ethics and judicial-recusal law.
   - ⚠️ **Line-drawing trap:** every boundary excludes a sympathetic hypothetical (a president's child railroaded by a hostile prosecutor). Answer in the record: the family member retains every other remedy — courts, the clemency board, and a *successor* president; the ban is only on the conflicted grantor, not on mercy itself.

3. **No sold or self-benefiting pardons (the public-benefit principle).**
   Proposed formulation: *"A reprieve or pardon granted in exchange for anything of value, or for the purpose of shielding the President from legal liability or advancing the President's private financial interest, is void; and the acceptance of anything of value in return for the grant of a reprieve or pardon may be punished as bribery notwithstanding any grant."*
   - Codifies what DOJ already treats as chargeable (the [2020 pardon-bribery investigation](https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/01/politics/pardon-investigation-doj/index.html); PODA's bribery clarification) and reaches the Rich and CZ fact patterns; the "void" language means a corrupt pardon fails even if the bribery prosecution never comes.
   - ⚠️ **Review-standard trap:** any motive test invites case-by-case judicial excavation of presidential intent — exactly what *Garland*-minded critics will call a destruction of the power. Mitigate by drafting the *bribery* half as self-executing criminal law (a transaction test, not a motive test) and treating the "purpose" half as the fallback severable clause; the categorical exclusions (family, naming) should carry the load precisely because they require no inquiry into motive.

4. **Individual, specified clemency only.**
   Proposed formulation: *"Every reprieve or pardon shall name the individual to whom it is granted and specify the offenses to which it applies; no reprieve or pardon shall extend to offenses not yet committed at the time of the grant."*
   - France's 2008 "individual basis" reform and Germany's "individual cases" are the tested models; this provision symmetrically addresses the January 6 blanket (~1,500 unnamed-by-deliberation grants), the Hunter Biden 2014–2024 decade blanket, and open-ended preemptive pardons — while leaving genuinely individual preemptive mercy (Ford–Nixon-style, for *specified past* conduct) available.
   - ⚠️ **Mass-mercy trap:** a strict naming rule would have barred Carter's 1977 draft-evasion amnesty and Washington's Whiskey Rebellion pardons — categorical clemency has legitimate uses. Consider a safety valve: categorical grants permitted only for classes defined by offense (not by relationship to the president) with each beneficiary individually certified and published. If the safety valve gets complicated, drop the categorical ban and rely on naming-plus-publication alone.

**Held back — does not yet clear the full-spectrum bar:**

- **No self-directed clemency** (*"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to himself or herself."*) — consistent with the rejected standalone category in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md): Republican support is regime-dependent (59% in Protect Democracy's 2024 battleground polling, 43% by Dec 2025). The principle partially survives inside the sold/self-benefiting clause ("shielding the President from legal liability"); the explicit ban returns if polling stabilizes across an administration change. Pedigree when it does: the [1974 OLC memo](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/presidential-or-legislative-pardon-president) ("no one may be a judge in his own case").
- **No clemency for the president's own conspirators** (*"The President may not grant a reprieve or pardon for an offense committed at the direction of, or in knowing coordination with, the President, or an offense of which the President was an intended beneficiary."*) — the associates ban sits at exactly 50% with Republicans, on advocacy-commissioned polling only ([H.J.Res.13](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-joint-resolution/13) lineage), and it carries the acute review-standard trap ("at the direction of" requires a factfinder). Path back: independent polling above the bar; until then it is the first provision to trade away.
- **Congressional disapproval — the Mondale check** (*"A pardon shall be void if, within ninety days of its publication, two-thirds of each House of Congress votes to disapprove it."*) — genuine bipartisan-era pedigree ([S.J.Res.241, 1974](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-joint-resolution/241)) but never polled and procedurally complex; if revived, draft the 90 days to run from *publication* so end-of-term pardons are reviewed by the next Congress, and do not substitute a lame-duck blackout (which would also bar legitimate end-of-term mercy).

**The package skeleton.** A single amendment article: **Section 1** — publication, naming, and reasons requirement (principles 1 + 4); **Section 2** — categorical exclusions: family and quid pro quo (principles 2 + 3); **Section 3** — savings clause: *"Nothing in this article shall be construed to diminish the power to grant reprieves, pardons, or commutations to any person not described herein"* — the drafted answer to the mercy-destruction objection, and the sentence every spokesperson should memorize. Held-back provisions rejoin as polling clears them. Lead all public materials with Sections 1–2; the pitch is *"every president, both parties, forever: mercy in public, never for sale, never for family."*

**Inherently statutory** (belongs in companion legislation, not the amendment):

- **An independent clemency board outside DOJ** (the [Barkow–Osler model](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2479595)) with a duty to process every petition within a fixed period — the supply-side fix for the clemency drought that makes the amendment's constraints defensible.
- **Pardon-lobbying disclosure** — registration and fee disclosure for paid pardon brokers.
- **Bribery-statute clarification** that clemency is an "official act" (the PODA provision) — worth enacting even before ratification.
- **Document-preservation and congressional-transmission rules** for clemency files (PODA's transparency title), including Burdick-notice and acceptance records.
- **A CCRC-style miscarriage-of-justice review body** for federal convictions — institutionalizing the pardon's legitimate core, on the UK model.
- **Machine-readable publication** of the clemency database (recipients, offenses, process channel, OPA recommendation status) so the bypass rate is permanently public.
- **Victim-notification requirements** before grants of clemency in crimes with identifiable victims (the *Albutt* principle, already partial DOJ practice).
