# Lobbying Research — US Landscape, International Models, Amendment Framework

This document supports **Target Category 5 (Lobbying)** in [CATEGORIES.md](CATEGORIES.md) and is a companion to [POLLING_RESEARCH.md](POLLING_RESEARCH.md). It covers the constitutional doctrine around lobbying restrictions, current federal and state law, pending legislation, international models, and abstraction candidates for amendment text.

Lobbying is unusual among our target categories: it is the only one where the reform the public wants collides head-on with an *enumerated* constitutional right — the First Amendment's guarantee of the right "to petition the Government for a redress of grievances." Courts have recently begun striking down exactly the reforms voters keep passing. That makes this category simultaneously the strongest case *for* a constitutional amendment (a statute genuinely cannot do the job) and the most dangerous to draft (careless text tramples a right both MAGA Republicans and socialist Democrats actively use).

**The headline findings:**

1. **Cooling-off periods are the consensus; lifetime bans are not.** Extending the ex-member wait from 2→5 years polls at 65% (65% R / 67% D); a 5-year wait for senior executive officials polls at 71% (72% R / 72% D); but a lifetime ban on *all* lobbying gets only 32% ([UMD Program for Public Consultation, 2022](https://publicconsultation.org/united-states/large-bipartisan-majority-favors-increasing-lobbying-restrictions-on-former-members-of-congress-and-other-government-officials/)). The amendment should constitutionalize a long cooling-off, not a permanent ban.
2. **Foreign-government lobbying is the exception — and Republicans lead on it.** A *lifetime* ban on ex-officials lobbying for foreign governments polls at 71% (71% R / 74% D). This is one of the very few reform planks where Republican support edges the framing rightward, which is gold for full-spectrum credibility.
3. **The courts are actively dismantling statutory revolving-door bans.** In [Miller v. Ziegler (8th Cir. 2024)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca8/23-1902/23-1902-2024-07-29.html) a federal appeals court struck down Missouri's voter-approved 2-year ban under strict scrutiny, and in [Garcia v. Stillman](https://floridaethics.org/floridas-constitutional-lobbying-bans-remain-effective-for-the-moment-current-status-of-constitutional-lobbying-restrictions/) a federal judge struck Florida's in-office lobbying restriction. Only a *federal* constitutional amendment is immune to this line of attack.
4. **Florida proved the model: voters will constitutionalize this by landslide margins.** Florida's 2018 Amendment 12 — a six-year revolving-door ban written directly into the state constitution — passed with [nearly 79% of the vote](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_Amendment_12,_Lobbying_Restrictions_Amendment_(2018)), in a state Trump carried. It is the single best US precedent for what this foundation proposes.
5. **Executive-order ethics pledges are revocable theater.** Trump's [EO 13770](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13770) imposed a 5-year lobbying pledge and a lifetime foreign-lobbying ban — then [he revoked it in the final hours of his term](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2021/01/25/2021-01713/revocation-of-executive-order-13770), retroactively releasing everyone who signed it. Biden's [EO 13989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13989) was likewise rescinded within hours of the 2025 transition. The instability is itself the argument for constitutional entrenchment.
6. **Existing cooling-offs fail in practice because of the shadow-lobbying ("Daschle") loophole.** Roughly [10,000 unregistered "strategic advisors" work alongside 10,000 registered lobbyists](https://typeinvestigations.org/investigation/2014/02/20/shadow-lobbying-complex/); any amendment that bans "lobbying" without addressing paid influence work simply renames the profession.

## Amendment Framework

### Core provisions

Based on the polling consensus and the legal landscape, the strongest package is a three-part amendment:

1. **A five-year post-service ban on paid lobbying of the federal government** by former Members of Congress, former senior congressional staff, and former senior executive-branch officials (replacing the current 1-year House / 2-year Senate / 1–2-year executive scheme in [18 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42728.html)). Five years matches both the 65–71% polling consensus and the strongest international peer regime (Canada).
2. **A lifetime ban on former Members and senior officials acting as paid agents of foreign governments or foreign political parties** — the 71%/71R/74D winner, already embodied in the bipartisan [Fighting Foreign Influence Act](https://golden.house.gov/media/press-releases/golden-hinson-introduce-bipartisan-bill-to-strengthen-lobbying-ban-for-senior-government-officials) and the foreign-lobbying title of the [PURE Executive Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr8544.xml).
3. **An express carve-through of the Petition Clause objection** — text acknowledging that the restriction operates *notwithstanding* the First Amendment as to compensated representational lobbying by covered former officials, while explicitly preserving every citizen's uncompensated right to petition.

### Challenges

- **The Petition Clause collision (see full subsection below).** Any statute doing this is now presumptively vulnerable after *Miller v. Ziegler*; an amendment solves that, but the drafting must modify the First Amendment *narrowly* or the coalition fractures. No one on either flank wants to be seen "amending the First Amendment" — framing must be "closing the paid-influence exception," not "restricting petition."
- **The definition trap.** Ban "lobbying" (an LDA term of art) and every ex-senator becomes a "strategic consultant" by Monday. The amendment must reach *compensated influence activity*, not just registered lobbying contacts, or it constitutionalizes the Daschle loophole.
- **Overbreadth blowback.** Text that could plausibly touch a veteran writing his congressman, a union rep, a pastor, or a nonprofit's policy director will be demagogued to death from both directions — and would deserve to be.
- **Lifetime-ban temptation.** Advocates will push to constitutionalize the BLAST Act's lifetime ban on all lobbying. The polling says no: 32% support. Hold the line at five years except for foreign governments.
- **Enforcement design.** Florida's amendment and Canada's Act both show that a ban without an empowered enforcer degrades into UK-style advisory theater.

### Strategic read

This category has the rare property that the *populist right* case ("drain the swamp," Trump's own 2017 EO) and the *progressive left* case (Warren's Anti-Corruption Act) converge on nearly identical text — Warren and Rick Scott are literally co-sponsoring [the same lifetime-ban bill](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-rick-scott-introduce-bipartisan-bill-to-ban-former-members-of-congress-from-lobbying/). 73% of Americans (78% R / 72% D) say lobbyists have too much influence. The court decisions striking down voter-approved bans supply the "why an amendment, not a law" answer that most categories lack. The main risk is not opposition but drafting error: the difference between a 79%-Florida-style landslide and a civil-libertarian revolt is entirely in how the text treats ordinary petitioners.

## US Legal & Legislative Landscape

### The Petition Clause problem

This is the load-bearing constitutional trap for the entire category.

**Is there a constitutional right to lobby?** The Supreme Court has never squarely held that *paid, professional* lobbying is fully protected — but the doctrine has been trending that way:

- **[United States v. Harriss (1954)](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/united-states-v-harriss/)** upheld the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act's registration and disclosure requirements against a First Amendment challenge, on the theory that Congress may know "who is being hired, who is putting up the money, and how much" ([full opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/347/612)). *Harriss* is why **disclosure and registration regimes are safe**; it says nothing blessing outright bans. The [Institute for Free Speech](https://www.ifs.org/news/institute-for-free-speech-urges-supreme-court-to-revisit-outdated-precedent-on-lobbying-disclosure/) *(advocacy source, free-speech side)* is actively urging the Court to narrow even *Harriss*.
- **[Autor v. Pritzker (D.C. Cir. 2014)](https://legalethicsopinions.justia.com/2016/12/16/autor-v-pritzker)** is the key modern case: registered lobbyists challenged Obama's ban on lobbyists serving on federal advisory committees, and the D.C. Circuit held they had **pled a viable First Amendment claim — treating paid lobbying as an exercise of the Petition Clause** and the committee ban as an unconstitutional condition. The administration narrowed the policy rather than defend it. The Congressional Research Service's [Constitution Annotated essay on lobbying](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt1-7-13-5/ALDE_00013494/) treats the right to petition as encompassing lobbying activity. Academic work like Maggie McKinley's [*Lobbying and the Petition Clause*](http://www.stanfordlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2016/06/68_McKinley_-_Stan._L._Rev._1131.pdf) (Stanford L. Rev. 2016) complicates this — arguing the historical petition right protected individual petitioning, not the modern access economy — but courts have not adopted that view.
- **[Nevada Commission on Ethics v. Carrigan (2011)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/117/)** cuts the other way and is the reformers' best precedent: a unanimous Court held that a legislator's *vote* is not his personal speech — "the legislative power … is not personal to the legislator but belongs to the people." *Carrigan* establishes that **restrictions attached to the office and its powers** (recusal, conflict rules) are not First Amendment problems. The doctrinal question is whether post-employment restrictions are more like *Carrigan* conditions-of-office or more like *Autor* burdens on private petitioning.
- **[Miller v. Ziegler (8th Cir. 2024)](https://www.jurist.org/news/2024/07/us-appeals-court-finds-missouri-law-banning-lobbying-2-years-after-office-unconstitutional/)** answered that question badly for reformers: the Eighth Circuit applied **strict scrutiny** to Missouri's voter-approved two-year revolving-door ban, held that it "burdens political speech," found Missouri had not proven actual or apparent quid-pro-quo corruption, and struck it down as not narrowly tailored. If *Miller* becomes the national rule, **every cooling-off statute — including 18 U.S.C. § 207 itself — is on borrowed time.**
- **[Garcia v. Stillman (S.D. Fla. 2023)](https://floridaethics.org/floridas-constitutional-lobbying-bans-remain-effective-for-the-moment-current-status-of-constitutional-lobbying-restrictions/)** struck Florida's *in-office* lobbying restriction (Art. II § 8(f)(2)) as an unconstitutional abridgment of speech, though the six-year *post-office* ban was not enjoined and remains in effect.

**The upshot:** courts uphold *disclosure* (Harriss) and *conditions on official power* (Carrigan), but increasingly treat *bans on compensated post-office lobbying* as core political speech triggering strict scrutiny (Autor, Miller). A federal statute imposing a 5-year or lifetime ban would face exactly the *Miller* attack — which is precisely why this belongs in the Constitution. **Any amendment must either expressly modify the Petition Clause as applied to compensated lobbying by former officials, or be drafted as a qualification/condition of federal office in the *Carrigan* mold.** Both routes are explored in the abstraction candidates below.

### Current federal law

- **[Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995](https://typeinvestigations.org/investigation/2014/02/20/shadow-lobbying-complex/)** — registration/disclosure regime. Its central flaw: registration is triggered only when lobbying exceeds **20% of time worked for a client**, so "strategic advisors" who plan campaigns and coach others (the **Daschle loophole**, named for the ex-Senate Majority Leader who became a "policy adviser") never register. An estimated [10,000+ unregistered influence professionals](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/shadow-lobbying-complex/) operate beside the registered corps, while reported lobbying spend stays above $3B/year.
- **[Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007](https://ethics.house.gov/outside-employment-income/post-employment-restrictions)** amended [18 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42728.html): **1-year cooling-off for House members, 2 years for Senators**, 1 year for senior staff, and 1–2 years for senior/very-senior executive officials. These are criminal provisions but narrowly cover "lobbying contacts" — behind-the-scenes strategy work is untouched.
- **[Executive Order 13770 (Trump, Jan. 2017)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13770)** — appointees pledged a 5-year ban on lobbying their agency and a **lifetime ban on FARA work for foreign governments**. Trump [revoked the entire order in the small hours of his last day in office](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2021/01/20/npr-trump-revokes-administration-ethics-rules-on-his-way-out-the-door), with no explanation, releasing all signatories retroactively ([Federal Register revocation](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2021/01/25/2021-01713/revocation-of-executive-order-13770)). **This is the instructive episode:** even a president elected on "drain the swamp" un-drained it by signature — pledges last only as long as the pledger's incentives.
- **[Executive Order 13989 (Biden, Jan. 2021)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13989)** — 2-year post-employment restrictions, a FARA restriction lasting the remainder of the administration or 2 years, and a golden-parachute ban; [rescinded by Trump within hours on Jan. 20, 2025](https://stateandfed.com/ethics/bidens-ethics-executive-order-for-appointees-revoked/). See CRS, [Ethics Pledges Since 1993](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44974).
- **[FARA (1938)](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/foreign-agents-registration-act-enforcement-in-the-twenty-first-century)** — foreign-agent registration, near-dormant for decades until the **post-2017 enforcement surge** driven by the Manafort and Flynn prosecutions; [first-time filings rose 50% in one year](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/mueller-effect-fara-filings-soar-shadow-manafort-flynn-probes-n838571) and supplemental filings doubled. FARA is disclosure-only — it does not *prohibit* an ex-Speaker from representing a foreign power, it merely requires a form. [Responsible Statecraft](https://responsiblestatecraft.org/2022/06/28/the-revolving-door-from-congress-to-foreign-interest-lobbyist-is-well-traveled/) *(advocacy-adjacent, Quincy Institute)* documents the well-traveled road from Congress to foreign agent.

### Current bills (the reform demand signal)

- **[Public Service Integrity Act (H.R. 414, Rep. Posey, R-FL)](https://vop.org/americans-on-lobbying-restrictions/)** — extends ex-member cooling-off to 5 years; the Republican-sponsored bill tested in the UMD survey.
- **[Cleaning Up Washington's Act (S. 751 / S. 1158, Sen. Tester, D-MT)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/senate-bill/1158)** — the Democratic companion concept, similar 5-year extension.
- **[BLAST Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/4524/text)** — lifetime ban on ex-member lobbying; introduced by [Warren (D) and Rick Scott (R) in May 2026](https://www.rickscott.senate.gov/2026/5/sens-rick-scott-elizabeth-warren-introduce-bill-to-permanently-ban-members-of-congress-from-lobbying), with a House version from [Vindman (D-VA) and Barry Moore (R-AL) in June 2026](https://vindman.house.gov/2026/06/04/vindman-introduces-bipartisan-bill-to-tackle-corruption-ban-members-of-congress-from-lobbying/) ([H.R. 9130 text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9130/text/ih)). Crucially, it bans **"lobbying contacts regardless of registration status"** — a direct legislative attack on the Daschle loophole — with penalties up to 5 years' imprisonment and $50,000 civil fines per violation.
- **[PURE Executive Act (H.R. 8544)](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr8544.xml)** — bipartisan; 5-year ban on senior executive officials lobbying their former agency plus a **lifetime ban on lobbying for foreign governments**.
- **[Close the Revolving Door Act](https://ocasio-cortez.house.gov/media/press-releases/ocasio-cortez-neguse-introduce-legislation-impose-lifetime-ban-members)** (Bennet lineage since 2010; AOC/Neguse [H.R. 3554 in 2025](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3554/text)) — lifetime member ban + 6-year staff cooling-off. Note that even sympathetic analysts [predict it would be struck down](https://ace-usa.org/blog/research/research-democratic-governance/can-congress-regulate-its-own-influence-unpacking-the-2025-close-the-revolving-door-act/) under the Petition Clause — again, the amendment argument.
- **[Fighting Foreign Influence Act (Golden D-ME / Gooden R-TX)](https://golden.house.gov/media/press-releases/golden-hinson-introduce-bipartisan-bill-to-strengthen-lobbying-ban-for-senior-government-officials)** — lifetime ban on former members, flag officers, and senior officials registering as foreign agents.

The pattern: **every one of these has genuine cross-party sponsorship**, and every serious analysis concedes the statutory versions face First Amendment peril. The demand is bipartisan; the vehicle is inadequate. That is the amendment's opening.

### Prior Amendment Proposals

Unlike campaign finance (where dozens of amendment texts exist post-*Citizens United*), there is **no significant history of proposed federal constitutional amendments specifically restricting the revolving door** — reformers have pursued statutes ([Bennet's 2012 STOCK Act amendment](https://bennet.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/2016/3/bennet-files-amendment-to-ban-members-of-congress-from-becoming-lobbyists-close-revolving-door) was a bill amendment, not Article V). Some omnibus anti-corruption amendment proposals (e.g., American Promise's campaign-finance-focused "For Our Freedom" amendment) touch influence generally, and Warren's [Anti-Corruption and Public Integrity Act](https://www.warren.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/media/doc/Master%20Summary%20of%20Anti%20Corruption%20Act%20-%20FINAL.pdf) is the most comprehensive statutory blueprint. This is essentially **open ground at the federal constitutional level** — the only true precedent is at the state level, in Florida.

## State Models

**Florida — the keystone precedent.** In November 2018, Florida voters approved **[Amendment 12](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_Amendment_12,_Lobbying_Restrictions_Amendment_(2018))** with **~79% of the vote** — the highest share of any measure on that year's ballot — writing into **Article II, § 8(f) of the Florida Constitution** a ban on public officers lobbying "for compensation on issues of policy, appropriations, or procurement" for **six years** after leaving office, covering state legislators, statewide officers, judges, and local officials. It was placed on the ballot by the Constitution Revision Commission, sponsored by Republican Don Gaetz, explicitly "to nail shut the revolving door" ([Florida Bar Journal analysis](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/amendment-12-lobbying-and-abuse-of-office-by-public-office-holders/); [Florida Commission on Ethics text](https://ethics.state.fl.us/Documents/Ethics/ArticleIISec8.html)). Three lessons:

1. **A 6-year ban — triple the federal Senate period — passed by a landslide in a red-trending state.** The full-spectrum test was passed in the field, not just in polls.
2. **Constitutionalizing it was deliberate**: proponents understood a statute could be repealed by the next legislature of ex-legislators.
3. **But state constitutions can't beat the First Amendment**: in [Garcia v. Stillman](https://www.floridabulldog.org/2022/02/five-florida-local-politicians-sue-state-to-block-new-get-tough-lobbying-ban/), a federal judge permanently enjoined the *in-office* restriction on First Amendment grounds, while [the six-year post-office ban survives for now](https://floridaethics.org/floridas-constitutional-lobbying-bans-remain-effective-for-the-moment-current-status-of-constitutional-lobbying-restrictions/). Only a *federal* amendment is beyond this reach.

**Missouri — the cautionary tale.** [Clean Missouri (Amendment 1, 2018)](https://ballotpedia.org/Missouri_Amendment_1,_Lobbying,_Campaign_Finance,_and_Redistricting_Initiative_(2018)) passed with 62%, imposing a 2-year revolving-door ban and a $5 lobbyist gift cap. In practice, [lobbyists adapted quickly](https://www.stltoday.com/news/local/government-politics/it-forces-you-to-be-more-creative-how-lobbyists-kept-their-grip-on-missouri-politics/article_c64bfa0b-daab-560a-b023-ede49f81ba1c.html) ("it forces you to be more creative"), and in July 2024 the Eighth Circuit [struck the revolving-door ban entirely](https://missouriindependent.com/briefs/federal-court-strikes-down-missouris-revolving-door-lobbying-ban/) in *Miller v. Ziegler*. A state constitutional provision, approved by voters, was erased by the federal First Amendment. **Missouri is the proof that this category cannot be finished at the state level.**

**The broader map.** Per [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/revolving-door-restrictions-by-state-2019/) *(advocacy source, pro-reform)* and [MultiState's 50-state survey](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2024/10/1/revolving-door-laws-by-state): ~33 states have cooling-offs of one year or less; more than a dozen impose two years; several (California, New York, New Mexico, Mississippi, Texas) add permanent bans on working the *specific matters* an official personally handled; Florida's six years is the national maximum; and a handful of states (until recently [Michigan](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/07/17/michigan-poised-to-close-revolving-door-for-lawmaker-lobbyists)) allow same-day lawmaker-to-lobbyist conversion. The "particular-matter" permanent bans are doctrinally safest (closest to *Carrigan*-style conflict rules) and worth echoing in amendment enforcement language.

## International Models

### Canada — the strongest peer regime

Canada's [Lobbying Act](https://lobbycanada.gc.ca/en/rules/the-lobbying-act/5-year-post-employment-prohibition-on-lobbying/) imposes a **five-year post-employment lobbying prohibition** on "designated public office holders" (ministers, ministerial staff, senior officials, MPs' senior staff), enforced by an independent **Commissioner of Lobbying** with investigation powers and a public registry; the Commissioner may grant narrow exemptions only where "not contrary to the purposes of the Act" ([Library of Parliament overview](https://lop.parl.ca/sites/PublicWebsite/default/en_CA/ResearchPublications/201173E)). Canada is the direct proof-of-concept that a 5-year ban is administrable in a G7 democracy — it is exactly the number the US polling supports.

**Effectiveness critiques:** [Democracy Watch](https://democracywatch.ca/secret-lobbying-loopholes-in-canadas-federal-lobbying-law/) *(advocacy source, pro-reform)* catalogs a "dirty dozen" loopholes: the five-year ban effectively binds only *registrable* lobbying, so ex-officials can begin unregistered "strategic advising" immediately; in-house corporate lobbying is restricted only where it is a "significant part" of duties; and enforcement is criticized as opaque — a [2026 report found the Commissioner declining to name rule-breakers](https://www.nationalobserver.com/2026/03/16/news/federal-lobbying-regulations-politics-criticism), and critics call the regime ["a black hole"](https://www.canadianaffairs.news/2026/04/22/canadas-lobbying-commissioner-rules-draw-criticism/). **Lesson: the 5-year number works; the definition ("registrable lobbying") and the enforcement culture are where Canada leaks.**

### European Union — scandal-driven, still weak

Former Commissioners face notification obligations for **2 years** (3 for the President) and an **18-month cooling-off** under the Code of Conduct — a regime exposed as inadequate when **Commission President José Manuel Barroso joined Goldman Sachs** 20 months after leaving office, formally breaking no rule ([Corporate Europe Observatory](https://corporateeurope.org/en/revolving-doors/2016/07/barroso-and-goldman-sachs-dangerous-liaison) *(advocacy source)*; Green MEP Sven Giegold: ["a miserly 18 months … in contrast to the five-year ban that applies in Canada"](https://sven-giegold.de/en/goldman-sachs-hires-barroso-longer-cooling-off-period-for-eu-commissioners-urgently-needed/)). The **Qatargate** cash-for-influence scandal (2022, MEPs allegedly bribed by Qatar and Morocco) forced Parliament to adopt reforms — but the result was only a **six-month cooling-off for ex-MEPs**, which [Transparency International EU](https://transparency.eu/qatargate-reforms-european-parliament-fails-its-first-big-test/) *(advocacy source)* dismissed as "window dressing," and the EU Ombudsman [flagged continuing gaps a year later](https://eno.ombudsman.europa.eu/home/news/archives-2023/maincontent/enonews-1215.html). The [Transparency Register](https://transparency-register.europa.eu/find-out-more/news/important-reminder-about-european-parliament-conditionality-measures-2024-09-18-0_en) conditions access on registration but remains transparency-first, prohibition-light. **Lesson: half-measure cooling-offs (6–18 months) fail visibly and repeatedly; scandals produce reform theater unless the number is real.**

### France — criminal teeth

France's [HATVP](https://publicmatters.nl/en/actueel/over-draaideuren-pantouflage-franse-regels-voor-lobbytransparantie/) (High Authority for Transparency in Public Life, created after the 2013 Cahuzac scandal) pre-clears post-government moves: officials are barred for **three years** from taking private roles related to their former responsibilities ("pantouflage" is so entrenched [the French have a word for it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantouflage)), with violations punishable by up to **3 years' imprisonment and a €200,000 fine**. France is the model for *criminalized, pre-cleared* revolving-door control — though [research on French debt-management officials](https://academic.oup.com/policyandsociety/article/44/2/165/8242001) finds sophisticated actors still time their exits around the rules. **Lesson: mandatory pre-clearance by an independent authority plus criminal penalties is the enforcement ceiling among peers.**

### United Kingdom — the anti-model

The UK's [Advisory Committee on Business Appointments (ACOBA)](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn03745/) reviews ex-ministers' and civil servants' jobs under the Business Appointment Rules — but it is purely advisory: it [cannot veto any appointment, and its strongest sanction is recommending a delay](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/SN03745/SN03745.pdf). Parliament's own committee called it a **"toothless regulator"**; the David Cameron/Greensill lobbying scandal showed the system [is "absurdly easy to sidestep"](https://theconversation.com/david-cameron-and-greensill-this-toothless-regulator-is-absurdly-easy-to-sidestep-158914) because ex-PMs face only a two-year advisory window and no penalty at all for ignoring advice. **Lesson: rules without an enforcer and without penalties are functionally decorative — the enforcement trap made flesh.**

### Australia — foreign-influence disclosure without teeth

Australia's [Foreign Influence Transparency Scheme (2018)](https://www.ag.gov.au/integrity/foreign-influence-transparency-scheme) was pitched as an ["improved version" of FARA](https://www.unswlawjournal.unsw.edu.au/article/foreign-agent-registration-schemes-in-australia-and-the-united-states-the-scope-risk-and-limitations-of-transparency), and notably imposes **lifetime enhanced registration obligations on former Cabinet ministers** acting for foreign principals. But it has produced [no prosecutions and minimal registrations](https://www.lowyinstitute.org/the-interpreter/fits-starts), with narrower definitions and thinner disclosure than FARA. **Lesson: the US's post-2017 FARA enforcement surge — not Australia's newer statute — is the effective foreign-influence model; and the "lifetime obligation for former ministers" concept validates the amendment's foreign-lobbying plank.**

### OECD baseline

The OECD's [Post-Public Employment: Good Practices for Preventing Conflict of Interest (2010)](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/post-public-employment_9789264056701-en.html) and its lobbying-transparency principles recommend cooling-off periods calibrated to seniority, restrictions on "switching sides" in matters an official personally handled, and — the near-universal finding across 30 member states — that **standards exist everywhere but "enforcing standards and imposing suitable sanctions remains a challenge for many countries."** The international consensus problem is not the rule; it is the enforcement.

## International Abstraction Lessons

1. **Five years is the proven ceiling of the politically achievable and the floor of the effective.** Canada administers 5 years without incident; the EU's 6–18 months failed publicly (Barroso, Qatargate); US polling lands on 5 (65–71%). The number writes itself.
2. **Define the conduct, not the job title.** Canada's ban binds only "registrable lobbying"; the US LDA's 20% threshold created shadow lobbying; both regimes leak through "strategic advisor" relabeling. Effective text must reach *compensated influence activity on behalf of another*, however styled.
3. **An independent enforcer with real sanctions is the whole game.** France (pre-clearance + prison) works better than Canada (commissioner + registry), which works better than the UK (advice + nothing). ACOBA is the controlled experiment in what a rule without an enforcer becomes.
4. **Transparency regimes and prohibition regimes are different tools; neither substitutes for the other.** Registries (EU, FARA, FITS) illuminate; only prohibitions with penalties deter. FARA's post-2017 lesson: even a disclosure statute is transformative *when actually prosecuted*.
5. **Revocable instruments revert.** Trump's EO 13770 (self-revoked at 11:59), Biden's EO 13989 (revoked within hours by his successor), and ACOBA's voluntary compliance all show that anything less durable than entrenched law tracks the incumbent's incentives.
6. **Foreign-government representation is treated as categorically different everywhere.** Trump's EO imposed a *lifetime* foreign ban alongside a 5-year domestic one; Australia imposes lifetime obligations on ex-ministers; the PURE and Fighting Foreign Influence Acts do the same. No peer democracy treats representing a foreign state as ordinary petitioning — and neither does US public opinion (71% for a lifetime ban vs. 32% for a general one).
7. **Scandal is the reform engine — build the vehicle before the scandal.** Qatargate, Greensill, Barroso, and Manafort each produced a reform window measured in months. An amendment text sitting on the shelf when the next scandal breaks is worth ten introduced after.
8. **Seniority-tiering is universal and legitimate.** Every peer regime (OECD-recommended) scales restrictions to the power of the office — cabinet/members get the longest bans, junior staff shorter ones. This also helps the amendment survive overbreadth objections.

## Constitutional Abstraction Candidates

**Amendment-grade principles** (general, cross-ideological, court-testable). *Gate check: only principles that clear the foundation's full-spectrum bar are listed. All four below clear it — the five-year door polls 65–72% with identical R/D support, the foreign-agent bar is the rare plank Republicans lead on (71R/74D), Florida ratified the model with 79%, and the firewall and enforcement clauses are structural protections for the polled package. The lifetime ban on all lobbying (32%) was excluded from candidacy at the outset.*

**1. The five-year door.** No senior federal official profits from selling access to the government they just left, for five years.

- Proposed formulation: *"No person who has served as a Senator, Representative, or officer of the United States in a senior position defined by law shall, within five years after leaving such office, receive compensation for advocating before, or advising others in advocating before, the Congress or the executive agencies of the United States on behalf of any other person or entity. This section shall not be construed to abridge the right of any person to petition the Government on their own behalf, or without compensation on behalf of others."*
- Constitutionalizes the 65–71% consensus (UMD 2022; Posey's H.R. 414; Tester's S. 751; Canada's Lobbying Act), and — because it *is* the First Amendment's peer — extinguishes the *Miller v. Ziegler* strict-scrutiny attack that no statute can survive.
- ⚠️ **The Petition Clause collision.** This text restricts what *Autor v. Pritzker* treated as protected petitioning. It works only because a later amendment controls an earlier one — but the drafting must say so implicitly through specificity, and the campaign must be ready for the "they're amending the First Amendment" attack. The savings clause (own-behalf and uncompensated petitioning) is not decoration; it is the answer to that attack.
- ⚠️ **The shadow-lobbying definition trap.** Ban "lobbying" and everyone becomes a "strategic advisor" (Daschle loophole; Canada's identical leak). Hence "advocating before, *or advising others in advocating before*" — the phrase must reach the coaching-and-strategy business model, with Congress empowered to define the perimeter.

**2. The lifetime foreign-agent bar.** Former officials never work for foreign governments. Period.

- Proposed formulation: *"No person who has served as President, Vice President, Senator, Representative, judge of the United States, officer of the United States in a senior position defined by law, or commissioned officer of the armed forces in a general or flag rank shall at any time receive compensation from, or act as the agent of, a foreign government or foreign political party for the purpose of influencing any policy or action of the United States."*
- This is the 71% / 71R / 74D plank where Republicans lead — mirroring EO 13770's lifetime pledge, the PURE Executive Act, and the Golden–Gooden Fighting Foreign Influence Act, and echoing the Framers' own Foreign Emoluments Clause logic (Art. I § 9 already bans present-taking from foreign states; this extends the principle past the exit door).
- ⚠️ **The definition trap, foreign edition.** FARA practice shows foreign states hire through cutouts — state-owned enterprises, sovereign wealth funds, "foundations." Text keyed to "foreign government" alone invites the Qatar-via-NGO structure that produced Qatargate; the enforcement statute must be authorized to reach entities directed or principally funded by foreign states.
- ⚠️ **The overbreadth trap.** Must not catch academics at foreign universities, employees of ordinary foreign private companies, or dual nationals' civic life — the limiting phrase "for the purpose of influencing any policy or action of the United States" is load-bearing.

**3. The citizen-petitioner firewall.** The amendment touches the access industry, never the citizen.

- Proposed formulation: *"Nothing in this article shall be construed to limit the right of any citizen, or of any association of citizens including religious, labor, charitable, or membership organizations, to petition the Government for a redress of grievances, nor to require the registration of any person petitioning without compensation."*
- A belt-and-suspenders construction rule making the full-spectrum coalition possible: gun-rights groups, unions, churches, and civil-liberties organizations all lobby, and all will (rightly) kill any text that could touch them. *Harriss* already limits regulation to paid direct lobbying; this locks that limit in.
- ⚠️ **The citizen-petitioner overbreadth trap is the campaign-killer.** Every opposing ad will feature a veteran, a nurse, or a pastor "banned from talking to their congressman." The firewall must be *in the constitutional text itself*, quotable in thirty seconds — not left to implementing legislation.

**4. Enforcement that cannot be waived by the enforced.** Real penalties, an independent enforcer, no self-pardon-by-EO.

- Proposed formulation: *"The Congress shall enforce this article by appropriate legislation, which shall provide civil and criminal penalties and shall vest investigative authority in an officer whose independence is protected by law; and no provision of this article may be waived, exempted, or suspended by executive order or by rule of either House."*
- Learns from the whole comparative record: ACOBA (no enforcer → dead letter), Canada (weak enforcement culture → "black hole"), France (pre-clearance + criminal penalties → strongest deterrence), and EO 13770's midnight self-revocation (waivability → nullity).
- ⚠️ **The enforcement trap.** A self-executing ban with no enforcer becomes Florida's — standing but untested; an enforcement clause that merely says "Congress may enforce" lets a Congress of future lobbyists enforce nothing. The non-waiver sentence exists because both Trump and Biden pledges evaporated at the stroke of a successor's (or their own) pen.
- ⚠️ **Counter-trap: over-specification.** Penalty amounts, agency names, and registration thresholds age badly in constitutional text (the LDA's 20% rule is the fossil warning). The Constitution sets the floor and the independence guarantee; Congress sets the machinery.

**The package skeleton.** A single "Public Integrity" lobbying article: **Section 1** — five-year post-service compensated-advocacy ban for Members of Congress and senior officials (Principle 1). **Section 2** — lifetime foreign-government agency bar (Principle 2). **Section 3** — the citizen-petitioner firewall (Principle 3). **Section 4** — enforcement, independence, and non-waiver (Principle 4), with seniority tiers and definitions delegated to legislation within constitutional floors. Lead the public case with Section 2 (the Republican-leading 71% plank) and Florida's 79% precedent; hold the perimeter with Section 3.

**Inherently statutory:** (leave out of the amendment; belongs in implementing legislation or ordinary law)

- Registration mechanics, thresholds, and reporting cadence (LDA/FARA modernization; abolishing the 20% test).
- The precise roster of "senior positions" covered, and seniority tiers for staff.
- Penalty schedules, fine amounts, and the design of the independent enforcement office (a FARA-unit/HATVP hybrid).
- Lobbyist gift bans, contingency-fee bans, and bundling rules (Clean Missouri-style).
- In-office lobbying restrictions for sitting local/state officials (the part of Florida's scheme that lost in *Garcia v. Stillman* — and a state-law matter regardless).
- Golden-parachute and reverse-revolving-door rules for people *entering* government (Biden EO 13989 territory).
- FARA exemption-tightening for adversary states and cutout-entity definitions.
- Transparency infrastructure: public lobbying databases, meeting-disclosure ("legislative footprint") rules.
