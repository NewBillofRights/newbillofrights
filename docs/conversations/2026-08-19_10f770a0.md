That clip is the April 2018 Cambridge Analytica hearing — Durbin's point being that Zuckerberg wouldn't disclose his hotel or his DMs, yet Facebook collects exactly that from everyone else "to connect people." Measured against our Privacy article (Article 4, `research/PROPOSED_AMENDMENTS.md`), the honest answer is: it fixes half of Durbin's problem outright, and only *enables* a fix for the other half.

**Where it directly helps — the government half:**

- **§2 closes the data-broker loophole.** Government can't acquire "by compulsion, purchase, or exchange" what it couldn't get through a lawful search. Today, agencies buy location and social-graph data from brokers fed by apps like Meta's — precisely the "where did you sleep last night, who did you message" data. That purchase route dies, self-executing, no legislation needed.
- **§1 kills the third-party doctrine.** "This right is not diminished because information has been disclosed to another or is insignificant in isolation." Under current doctrine, handing your data to Facebook strips it of Fourth Amendment protection. Under §1 it doesn't — and the "insignificant in isolation" clause constitutionalizes the aggregation insight, which is exactly the mosaic Durbin was gesturing at: each message trivial, the whole picture intimate.
- **§4 puts the Durbin examples in the top tier** — private communications and precise movements get the strongest protection, and §2 separately bans generalized suspicionless collection or retention.

**Where it doesn't help — the corporate half:**

- **The article binds government, not Meta.** Nothing in it makes Zuckerberg's actual conduct — harvesting, profiling, monetizing under terms-of-service "consent" — unlawful. That's the state-action line, and it was a deliberate design choice: the memo (`PRIVACY_RESEARCH.md`) notes only California's constitution has ever been applied against private actors (*Hill v. NCAA*), and we resolved toward the empowerment model rather than binding corporations directly.
- **§3's last sentence is the corporate hook, not the fix:** "Congress and the States may by law secure rights of access, correction, and deletion of personal information held by others." It removes any constitutional doubt about Congress's power to pass a GDPR-style law against Meta — but until a legislature acts, the "premise of connecting people" business model is untouched.

So the one-sentence version: **the amendment guarantees the government can never exploit what Zuckerberg collects, and hands Congress unambiguous power — but not an obligation — to constrain the collection itself.**

## Assistant (2026-08-19 23:52)

Good question — let me ground it in what the memo already gathered on models that bind private actors.

This is a real design fork, and the memo already contains the three live models. Here's how I'd frame the expansion options, each with draft language in the article's voice:

## The threshold fact: horizontal application is rare but not unprecedented

Constitutional rights almost always bind government only. The two proofs it *can* be done otherwise: the **Thirteenth Amendment** — the only federal amendment that binds private actors directly (and our own Article 9 builds on it) — and **California Art. I §1**, applied to private parties in *Hill v. NCAA* via a balancing test. So "the Constitution can't reach Meta" is false; the question is which mechanism.

## Option A — The Charter Article 8 "rulebook" section (my recommendation)

The EU's insight (memo §"twin rights"): privacy is a *shield* against intrusion; data protection is a *rulebook* for all processing, public or private. Our Article 4 is the shield. The expansion is a new section — or cleaner, a §3 rewrite — that constitutionalizes the five principles that have survived 45 years unchanged (Convention 108 → Charter → GDPR Art. 5), and nothing else:

> **Section 9.** Every person has the right to the protection of personal information concerning them, against whomever holds it. Personal information may be collected and processed only fairly, for specified purposes, and to the extent those purposes require; it shall be kept accurate and secure. Every person may know what personal information others hold about them in the course of commerce, obtain correction of material inaccuracies, and obtain deletion of what is held without consent or other lawful basis. Congress and the States shall have power to enforce and further define this section by appropriate legislation.

- **Helps the Durbin problem directly:** purpose limitation kills "collected to connect people, monetized for everything else." Deletion-absent-lawful-basis attacks hoarding.
- **Follows the memo's Lesson 4:** principles, not machinery. No consent mechanics, no thresholds, no agency org chart — that's where ADPPA/APRA/GDPR all bled.
- **Cost:** "fairly, for specified purposes" against private actors invites decades of case-by-case litigation (Canada's 30-year *Plant*→*Spencer* crawl is the cautionary tale).

## Option B — The fiduciary section (the sleeper)

The Schatz Data Care Act model: instead of rights-in-the-data, duties on the holder.

> **Section 9.** Whoever collects personal information of others as a condition of providing a service owes those persons duties of care, loyalty, and confidentiality with respect to that information, and may not use it against their interests. Congress and the States may define and enforce these duties by law.

- **Elegance:** it's the exact answer to Zuckerberg's asymmetry — you trusted him with your DMs the way you trust a doctor or lawyer, and the law already knows what that trust demands. No consent theater at all.
- **Cost:** "fiduciary" is novel constitutional language with no state-constitutional track record; "against their interests" would be litigated forever. Polls untested.

## Option C — The California move (strike "governmental")

Make §1's shield itself horizontal, as *Hill* did. **I'd reject this one:** it constitutionalizes privacy tort law wholesale, collides hardest with the First Amendment (*Sorrell v. IMS Health* held data flows are speech — a horizontal shield vs. §7's speech savings clause becomes a self-eating watermelon), and the memo notes we deliberately scoped §1 to "governmental intrusion" partly to leave *Hill* undisturbed as a state floor.

## The two flags any option must survive

1. ***Sorrell*:** any private-sector rule will be attacked as a speech restriction. §7's savings clause protects the article from killing journalism, but it also gives Meta its litigation position. The draft has to thread that — which is an argument for A's "rights-in-the-data" framing (access/correction/deletion are conduct remedies, not speech bans) over broad processing bans.
2. **Ratification politics:** 24 states now have consumer-privacy statutes, red and blue — but on the *Virginia* model (AG-enforced, no private right of action). A floor of access/correction/deletion probably polls like motherhood; a self-executing right to sue Meta probably doesn't. That argues for A's enforcement sentence pointing to legislatures, matching how §3 and §7 already work.

**Bottom line:** the natural expansion is Option A — a tenth-section data-protection rulebook, horizontal, principles-only, legislatively enforced — with Option B's loyalty duty as the split-sample poll arm to see which framing Americans actually prefer.
