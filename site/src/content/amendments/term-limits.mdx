## The problem

The Constitution puts no limit on how long anyone can hold federal power. The
result is a government of careers: members of Congress who serve for decades,
and Supreme Court justices who now average about twenty-eight years on the
bench — more than double the tenure of high-court judges in comparable
democracies. No other major democracy gives lifetime, unbounded seats to the
judges of its constitutional court. Within the United States itself, 339 of
the 344 justices on state supreme courts serve limited terms, face a
retirement age, or both.

Unbounded tenure has costs beyond stagnation. When a Supreme Court seat can
last forty years, every vacancy carries generational stakes — and
confirmation fights, retirement timing, and even presidential elections warp
around them. Voters cannot fix this at the state level: twenty-three states
tried to term-limit their own members of Congress, and the Supreme Court
ruled in *U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton* (1995) that only a constitutional
amendment can do it. This is not a problem legislation can reach — by the
Court's own ruling, only an amendment can.

## The principle

Three parts, each doing a specific job:

**Twelve years per chamber of Congress.** Two Senate terms, six House terms —
counting only terms that begin after ratification. Twelve years is a
deliberate choice, not a compromise: the states that tried short six-year
limits (California, Michigan) found they empowered lobbyists and staff at
legislators' expense, and both states independently reformed to twelve-year
caps. When Americans deliberate on the numbers, majorities in both parties
land at about twelve years per chamber. Very short limits drain expertise;
twelve years ends indefinite incumbency without amateurizing Congress.

**Eighteen-year fixed terms on the Supreme Court.** Justices keep the office
and the independence of life tenure, but exercise the Supreme Court's judicial
power for a single, nonrenewable eighteen-year term, with one term beginning
every two years. Staggered seats turn each vacancy from a generational event
into a routine one: every president gets two appointments per term, no more,
regardless of retirement timing or health. This is the design used, in
essence, by nearly every constitutional court in the democratic world.

**An anti-evasion rule.** Around the world, term limits die by legal-looking
evasion — a friendly amendment here, a court reinterpretation there — far more
often than by repeal. So no change to these limits can ever benefit the people
in office when it passes. Loosening the rules for your successors is
legitimate constitutional politics; loosening them for yourself is not.

## The draft text

<div class="article-text">

**Section 1.** No person shall be elected to the Senate more than twice, nor to the House of Representatives more than six times; and no term to which a person was elected before the ratification of this article shall be counted.

**Section 2.** Justices of the Supreme Court shall be appointed as heretofore, and shall hold their offices during good behaviour, but shall exercise the judicial power of the Supreme Court for a single term of eighteen years, one such term commencing in each odd-numbered year; thereafter they may continue in judicial office as Congress shall by law provide.

**Section 3.** No amendment to, or law affecting, the limitations of this article shall apply to any person holding office at the time of its adoption, nor to any person elected or appointed within four years thereafter.

</div>

## Why it can pass

Term limits are the strongest full-spectrum case we have measured — the
highest support and the smallest partisan gap of any structural reform:

The support survives argument. In the University of Maryland's deliberative
survey (2023), respondents heard the strongest cases both for and against
before deciding — and 83% still backed an amendment (86% of Republicans, 80%
of Democrats, 84% of independents).

The country has done this before, and nearly done this exact thing:

- **The 22nd Amendment** (ratified 1951) term-limited the presidency —
  evidence that a term-limits amendment can be proposed and ratified in the
  modern era.
- **The House has voted for it twice**: 227–204 in 1995 and 217–211 in 1997 —
  majorities both times, short of two-thirds, the closest any modern
  structural amendment has come.
- **Sixteen states** limit their own legislators today; **thirty-three** set
  retirement ages for judges; and fourteen states have formally called for a
  term-limits amendment convention — the same pressure that historically moves
  Congress to act first.

## What we left out

What we considered and set aside, and why:

- **Six-year caps.** The tighter version many advocates prefer. Two decades of
  evidence from the states shows very short limits shift power to lobbyists,
  staff, and the executive — the states that lived under them reformed to
  twelve years, and this proposal follows that evidence.
- **Applying limits to sitting members and justices.** Every version that
  nearly passed Congress was prospective-only, and the 22nd Amendment
  exempted the sitting president. Counting current officeholders turns a
  neutral rule into an attack on particular incumbents and forecloses the
  two-thirds vote. Prospective-only limits will be criticized for sparing the
  people who vote on them; we make that trade knowingly, because it is the
  trade every version that came close to passing has made.
- **A uniformity clause** (declaring these the sole term limits and preserving
  state election powers) — held back because it is unpolled and its language
  risks being read against redistricting reform. It can return if formal
  drafting requires it.
- **Statutory Supreme Court term limits.** Bills in Congress would impose
  18-year terms by ordinary statute. Any such statute would be reviewed by the
  very justices it limits, under a constitutional clause most commentators
  read to forbid it. An amendment resolves the question outright — which is
  the reason to do this by amendment.
- **Details that belong in statute**: the senior-service regime for
  term-expired justices, confirmation scheduling, and retirement rules for
  lower federal courts.
