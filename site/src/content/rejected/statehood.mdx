## The proposal

Two jurisdictions share one condition — residents subject to federal taxes and the draft who send no voting member to either chamber of Congress — and differ in law. Puerto Rico's admission would be by ordinary statute: Article IV, Section 3 provides that "New states may be admitted by the Congress into this union," and thirty-seven states have entered under it.[^art4] For the District, the enclave clause gives Congress "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" over the seat of government,[^art1] and the Twenty-third Amendment grants electors to "the District constituting the seat of government"[^amend23] — which admission bills, by shrinking the district to a capital core, would leave in force for a residual district of very small population. The amendment as commonly framed would repeal the Twenty-third Amendment and admit the District's residential territory, or give its residents congressional representation without statehood.

## The evidence

We gated the two jurisdictions separately. For the District, the floor of record: asked about "making Washington, D.C., a separate state" in March 2021, 13% of Republicans supported and 64% opposed, against 64% of Democrats in support (Yahoo News/YouGov, 2021).[^yahoo]

Gallup asked the same question in 2019 and found 29% favor overall — 15% of Republicans, 30% of independents, and 39% of Democrats.[^gallup-dc] Washington Examiner/YouGov put the Republican figure at 13% again in 2020, with 71% of Democrats.[^examiner]

For Puerto Rico, the newest independent reading is conditional: "If the residents of Puerto Rico voted in favor of it, would you support or oppose making Puerto Rico a U.S. state?" drew 59% support — 79% of Democrats, 56% of independents, and 41% of Republicans (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov2024]

<FullSpectrumChart proposal="Making Puerto Rico a U.S. state if its residents vote for it — YouGov, 2024" support={{ dem: 79, rep: 41, ind: 56 }} />

Gallup's unconditional ask in 2019 drew 66% favor — 45% of Republicans and 83% of Democrats, the highest Republican reading on record.[^gallup-pr] The newest national reading covers both: in February 2026, DC statehood drew 32% support and 36% oppose — the only item in the pollster's five-reform battery where opposition exceeded support — and Puerto Rico statehood 51% and 24%, with party figures published as net rather than raw support, so they set no floor (Strength In Numbers/Verasight, 2026).[^sin] Residents of both have voted for statehood — 58.61% of valid votes in Puerto Rico's 2024 plebiscite, about 49% of all ballots cast,[^pr2024] and 85.69% in the District's 2016 advisory referendum[^dc2016] — but those measure local demand, not national consent. The roll calls diverge: the Puerto Rico Status Act passed the House in 2022 with 16 Republican yeas;[^roll529] the DC admission bill passed twice with none.[^roll132][^roll122]

## The test it failed

**District of Columbia — the bipartisan floor, and the symmetry test.** The floor is 13% of Republicans (Yahoo News/YouGov, 2021): Weak, and the lowest floor in our corpus, replicated at 13% in 2020 and bracketed at 15% in 2019 — the survey in which only 39% of Democrats favored it, so the proposal does not command a majority in the party that sponsors it.[^gallup-dc] Support also tracks who holds power: from July 2020 to March 2021, across a change in Senate control and a House vote, Democratic support rose from 49% to 64% while Republican support fell from 23% to 13%.[^yahoo] The symmetry test fails for the same reason. Admission is a one-time act with a known partisan result, not a rule binding future actors equally, so the devices that make a rule neutral — prospective effective dates, grandfathering — have no purchase on it. The legislative record shows the same shape: two House passages with no Republican yeas,[^roll132][^roll122] and a current bill whose 208 cosponsors all come from one party.[^hr51]

**Puerto Rico — the why-an-amendment test.** Admission is a statute: Article IV, Section 3 supplies the mechanism and thirty-seven states have used it,[^art4] so no court has struck a statute here, the text is not silent, and Congress is not exempting itself. An idea that is genuinely statutory is recorded and parked rather than drafted as an amendment, and Puerto Rico's statutory route stays on our record. The polling would not have carried it in any event: 41% of Republicans in 2024[^yougov2024] and 45% at the best independent reading ever taken[^gallup-pr] — one Weak, one at the bottom edge of the Moderate band — and six independent readings run from 21% to 45% of Republicans against 63% to 86% of Democrats.[^economist][^examiner]

## Framings we tested

No framing clears the bar for either jurisdiction. For the District, the strongest reading on record is an argument frame — "taxation without representation" — which reached 42% of Republicans and 73% of Democrats in an advocacy-commissioned poll whose plain topline drew 34% of Republicans against 56% opposed (Data for Progress, 2021).[^dfp] An advocacy pollster testing its own best argument, in the issue's highest-salience week, landing eighteen points below the Strong threshold is a ceiling, not a floor.

For Puerto Rico, the conditional wording gives 41% of Republicans and Gallup's unconditional ask 45%. When a question named the federal-power consequence — Puerto Rico voting for President — Republican support fell from 43% to 38% within a single survey while Democratic support held at 77% (YouGov, 2019).[^yougov2019] Two framings have never been tested by any independent pollster: congressional representation for the District without statehood, and a binding federal process for Puerto Rico's status.

## The path back

For the District, the category reopens on an independent national poll with 2026-or-later fieldwork asking whether the roughly 700,000 U.S. citizens who live there should have voting representation in Congress, then naming the mechanism — statehood, representation by amendment without statehood, or retrocession to Maryland — with the remedy, not the grievance alone, clearing 60% among Republicans. A cross-party roll call on any DC representation measure would count on the same question. The route is narrow but specific: 74% of opponents objected that "D.C. was never meant to be a state," against 17% citing partisan concerns (Morning Consult/Politico, 2021; no party breakdown published for that item)[^mc] — an objection to statehood's form rather than to representation itself, never polled. For Puerto Rico, no poll reopens the category as an amendment: the failing test is the mechanism, not the numbers.
