## The proposal

Three different amendments travel under this heading, pointing in opposite directions. The first would write a right to decide whether to continue a pregnancy into the Constitution, usually with a viability line and a health exception; Congress has voted only on its statutory version.[^whpa-2022] The second would declare that constitutional protection of life or personhood begins at conception, or return authority over abortion to Congress and the states; the last Senate floor vote on any abortion amendment, in 1983, was of this kind and split both caucuses.[^senate-1983] The third, "leave it to the states," is the law after *Dobbs*, not an amendment, and the framing most often offered as the middle ground.

## The evidence

One independent national poll has asked the amendment question directly with a party breakdown. Asked about "a federal constitutional amendment that protects a woman's right to choose to have an abortion," 56% of Americans supported it: 87% of Democrats, 50% of independents, and 25% of Republicans (UMass Amherst/YouGov, 2024).[^umass-amendment] The same survey asked about a national abortion ban: 15% overall — 7% of Democrats, 10% of independents, and 28% of Republicans (UMass Amherst/YouGov, 2024).[^umass-ban] Each direction fails the other party's floor.

The underlying question of legality shows the same gap under every wording: across the 2026 waves of Pew, Gallup, and Economist/YouGov, Democratic support for legality runs 75% to 85% and Republican support 15% to 36%.[^pew-2026][^gallup-2026][^econ-yougov-2026] On the principle behind the second amendment, "human life begins at conception, so a fetus is a person with rights" described the views of 56% of Republicans and 23% of Democrats extremely or very well (Pew Research Center, 2022).[^pew-2022]

State ballots since 2022 decided state text, not federal text, and the results split. Measures protecting a right to abortion or reproductive freedom passed in Michigan, Ohio, Montana, and Missouri (51.6% to 57.8%) and failed in Florida (57.2%, short of the 60% required), Nebraska, and South Dakota.[^ballots-rights] Measures removing or denying a state right failed in Kansas and Kentucky; Nebraska approved a ban after the first trimester.[^ballots-restrict] Since 1970, six personhood amendments have reached state ballots and none was approved.[^ballotpedia-table]

## The test it failed

The bipartisan floor is 25%, among Republicans, on the only direct amendment question: Weak. The opposite direction has no direct national poll; its nearest proxies, a national ban and the personhood principle, are Weak as well.

**High topline, low floor, in both directions.** A rights amendment carries a 56% topline over a 25% Republican floor, a 62-point party gap; a ban carries a 28% Republican ceiling over a 7% Democratic floor (UMass Amherst/YouGov, 2024).[^umass-amendment][^umass-ban]

**Framing dependency.** State reproductive-freedom text wins state ballots at 51% to 58% in states that voted Republican for president, but it is not federal text, and three of the five such 2024 measures failed.[^ballots-rights] The framing that wins Republicans, leaving the question to the states, is not an amendment.

**The symmetry test.** Every amendment we advance binds both parties' future majorities equally: an abuse both sides condemn, a remedy both sides accept. An abortion amendment in either direction is a substantive rule on a contested moral question, with no abuse both sides name and no remedy that binds each side the same way. The logic that carried the pardon reform target does not apply.

The split does not move with who holds power. On legality in all or most cases, the gap between Democrats (84%) and Republicans (36%) is 48 points, up from 24 in 2007 (Pew Research Center, 2026);[^pew-2026] a re-poll under a different administration is unlikely to change the result.

## Framings we tested

- **A right-to-abortion amendment.** 87% of Democrats, 25% of Republicans (UMass Amherst/YouGov, 2024).[^umass-amendment] Weak. A federal law guaranteeing access to legal abortion nationwide: 84% of Democrats, 32% of Republicans (AP-NORC, 2023).[^apnorc-2023] Weak.
- **A right-to-life or personhood amendment.** No direct national poll; the proxies are 7% of Democrats for a national ban (UMass Amherst/YouGov, 2024)[^umass-ban] and 23% of Democrats on the personhood principle (Pew Research Center, 2022),[^pew-2022] both Weak. The Republican Party's 2024 platform dropped its four-decade call for such an amendment.[^platform-2024]
- **"Leave it to the states."** This is current law. As an amendment it would constitutionalize the status quo, and neither side's amendment proposals take that form; it is a different proposal wearing the name.
- **Narrow, high-consensus slices.** A federal law requiring emergency rooms to provide an abortion to save the life or health of a pregnant woman drew 89% of Democrats, 68% of independents, and 61% of Republicans (UMass Amherst/YouGov, 2024).[^umass-er] That clears the bar, but it is a statutory health-care rule, not a constitutional principle.

None clears the bar as an amendment either side would draft. One consequence: the foundation's privacy target is written in informational and electronic-data terms only, because an autonomy-framed privacy amendment would be heard as the first framing above and inherit its 25% floor.

## The path back

None identified. Reopening would require an independent national poll showing at least 45% support in both parties — a Moderate floor — for one specific amendment text, sustained across two waves under different administrations. None has appeared from 2022 through mid-2026, and the party gap is widening (Pew Research Center, 2026).[^pew-2026]
