## The proposal

The idea is a single sentence added to Article II: the President may not grant a reprieve or pardon to himself or herself. It is the narrowest pardon-limiting proposal. A 1974 Office of Legal Counsel memorandum concluded that "under the fundamental rule that no one may be a judge in his own case, the President cannot pardon himself."[^olc-1974] No court has ruled, and the opinion binds none. Two variants circulate: a bundled version, introduced in every Congress since 2017, that also bars pardons of relatives, administration members, and campaign staff,[^hjres13] and a consequence rule treating a self-pardon as grounds for removal. The polls test the standalone ban.

## The evidence

Only one independent national poll has asked about prohibiting self-pardons with a party breakdown. Asked whether they would support or oppose "prohibiting a president from pardoning themself," 64% of Americans supported it: 81% of Democrats, 67% of independents, and 43% of Republicans, with 29% of Republicans opposed and 27% not sure (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov-2024-ban]

The same survey measured the norm rather than the rule: 77% called it inappropriate for a president to pardon themself — 88% of Democrats, 77% of independents, and 63% of Republicans (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov-2024-norm]

Earlier polling measured the norm and the consequence, not a ban. In June 2018, 85% called it unacceptable for presidents to pardon themselves if charged with a crime; the pollster's topline published no party breakdown, but the AP's write-up reported about 75% of Republicans and more than 90% of Democrats (AP-NORC, 2018).[^apnorc-2018] An advocacy-commissioned survey of battleground House districts found 78% disagreeing that "presidents should pardon themselves": 96% of Democrats, 76% of independents, 59% of Republicans (YouGov for Protect Democracy, 2024).[^protect-democracy-2024] Polls about a self-pardon by a named president measure a person, not a rule, and are excluded (no party breakdown published).[^named-polls] No self-pardon amendment has received a hearing or markup in any Congress.[^hjres13]

## The test it failed

The bipartisan floor is 43%, among Republicans, on the only independent ban question: Weak. Three failure modes apply.

**High topline, low floor.** The 64% overall conceals a 38-point gap between the parties: 81% of Democrats and 43% of Republicans (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov-2024-ban]

**Beneficiary coding.** The evidence is that the Republican number tracks the plausible beneficiary. In June 2018, with no charge pending against anyone, about 75% of Republicans called a self-pardon unacceptable (AP-NORC, 2018).[^apnorc-2018] In August 2024, when a self-pardon was a live question for one party's presidential nominee, 63% called it inappropriate and 43% supported a ban (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov-2024-norm] A floor that moves with the likely beneficiary is not stable.

**The abuse-vs-remedy gap.** In the same survey, 63% of Republicans called a self-pardon inappropriate and 43% backed the prohibition (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov-2024-ban] Both parties oppose the act; one supports the fix. This is also framing dependency: the norm clears, the remedy does not, and the remedy is the text we would draft.

The category passes the three non-polling tests: no official as judge in his own case is the core of the mission's rule-of-law pillar; the text binds every future president of either party identically; and the constitutional text does not expressly address a self-pardon, the 1974 opinion binds no court, and Congress cannot restrict the pardon power by statute. The rejection is entirely a polling result, which is why we hold the category rather than dismiss it.

## Framings we tested

Two framings clear the Strong bar among Republicans, and both describe the act rather than the rule: "inappropriate for a president to pardon themself," at 63% of Republicans and 88% of Democrats (YouGov, 2024),[^yougov-2024-norm] and "unacceptable for presidents to pardon themselves if charged with a crime," at about 75% of Republicans and more than 90% of Democrats (AP-NORC, 2018; party split from the AP write-up).[^apnorc-2018] Congress removing a president after a self-pardon reached 76% overall and 56% of Republicans in the same 2018 survey — Moderate, a consequence rule rather than a ban, and eight years old.[^apnorc-2018] The advocacy norm statement reached 59% of Republicans in battleground districts (YouGov for Protect Democracy, 2024) — Moderate, not a national sample, and a "should," not a "prohibit."[^protect-democracy-2024] A generic constitutional limit on the pardon power is weaker than the specific ban: 34% of Republicans, 58% of independents, 76% of Democrats (Economist/YouGov, 2025).[^yougov-dec-2025] The bundled ban has never been polled.[^hjres13] No framing rescues the category. The norm is bipartisan and durable; the failure is one of remedy and timing. The principle lives in the Pardon Reform target's clause voiding any pardon granted to shield the President from legal liability.

## The path back

We intend to commission an independent national poll asking the ban in forward-looking, both-parties wording — "No future president, of either party, may pardon himself or herself" — alongside the pardon target's other provisions, so the clause is measured as a rule for everyone, not a verdict on one person. We will reopen the category if Republican support reaches about 60% with Democrats above 60%. A Moderate result, with the "not sure" share moving toward support, would justify carrying the explicit ban inside the pardon package rather than as a standalone article. Two other triggers: an independent national replication of the 59% Republican norm figure, or a court ruling on an actual self-pardon in either direction.
