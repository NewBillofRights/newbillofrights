## The proposal

The idea is a constitutional rule that federal spending may not exceed federal revenue in a fiscal year. Every serious draft adds machinery, and there the drafts diverge. Most let a supermajority of each chamber — three-fifths or two-thirds — authorize a deficit, usually with an exception for war.[^hjres17-119] Some also require two-thirds of each chamber to raise taxes or the debt limit.[^hjres2-119] Advocates also pursue an Article V convention limited to the subject; by their own counts, 27 or 28 of the required 34 states have live applications.[^article-v-count]

## The evidence

The abstract question clears the bar in every independent poll we found. Asked about amending the Constitution to "require the federal budget to be balanced each year," 72% of Americans were in favor and 7% opposed — 84% of Republicans, 68% of Democrats, and 65% of independents (Economist/YouGov, 2026).[^yougov-2026]

Other abstract readings agree. "Require a balanced federal budget" drew 69% — 77% of Republicans, 69% of Democrats, 62% of independents (Cato Institute/Morning Consult, 2026; Cato holds a position on the amendment, though the fieldwork is Morning Consult's).[^cato-2026] A forced-choice question on "a balanced budget within 10 years" drew 80% — 83% of Republicans, 79% of Democrats, 77% of independents (Harvard CAPS/Harris, 2023).[^harris-2023] An advocacy-commissioned poll found 70% — 74% of Republicans, 67% of Democrats (McLaughlin & Associates for America's New Majority Project, 2022).[^anmp-polls]

Only one poll on record has named a trade-off (Fox News, 2011). In the abstract, 72% favored — 82% of Republicans, 62% of Democrats. Told that balancing the budget would mean "major spending cuts" to "entitlement programs like Medicare and Social Security," 31% favored and 63% opposed; 79% of Democrats opposed, and Republicans split 47% to 46%. Told it would mean "major tax increases," 62% opposed (no party breakdown published).[^fox-2011]

The programs a binding rule would reach have been polled repeatedly since, and both parties reject cutting them: 79% say Social Security benefits "should not be reduced in any way" — 77% of Republicans, 83% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2024).[^pew-ss-2024] Reducing benefits drew 6% in favor, 79% opposed (AP-NORC, 2023).[^apnorc-2023] Majorities of Republicans oppose "major cuts" to Social Security (73%), Medicare (64%), and Medicaid (55%) (KFF, 2025).[^kff-2025]

The amendment has never carried both chambers: the Senate fell two votes short in 1995 (65–35; Democrats 14–33) and one in 1997 (66–34; Democrats 11–34),[^senate-votes] and House Democratic support fell from 72 votes in 1995 to 25 in 2011 and 7 in 2018.[^house-votes]

## The test it failed

The bipartisan floor on the abstract question is 68% (Democrats, Economist/YouGov 2026): Strong. That is not the number a drafted amendment would face. On the conditioned question the floor is Weak: 31% overall, 79% of Democrats opposed, Republicans 47% to 46% (Fox News, 2011).[^fox-2011] Two failure modes apply.

**Depth.** Support fell roughly 40 points, from 72% to 31%, once the trade-off was named.[^fox-2011] That is one poll, fifteen years old, but every modern program-level poll above points the same way.[^pew-ss-2024][^apnorc-2023][^kff-2025] The abstract number rests on a belief that no trade-off exists: 64% of a 2025 advocacy sample agreed the budget can be balanced without raising taxes or cutting benefits.[^anmp-2025-arithmetic]

**Asymmetric coalition.** The two halves of any binding rule each lose a party. Benefit cuts lose Democrats (79% opposed) and half of Republicans (Fox News, 2011).[^fox-2011] Tax increases lose Republicans: 69% of Republicans want the deficit cut only or mostly through spending cuts, while 57% of Democrats want an equal mix of tax increases and cuts (Gallup, 2012),[^gallup-2012] and a Medicare tax on households earning over $400,000 drew 42% of Republicans against 75% of Democrats (AP-NORC, 2023).[^apnorc-2023]

The why-an-amendment test is arguably met — Congress cannot bind future Congresses by statute, and the 1985 statutory attempt did not survive the courts intact[^crs-r41907] — and is not why the category failed. But a working balanced-budget rule is thresholds, exemptions, and enforcement, which our mission assigns to legislation.

## Framings we tested

- Unconditioned "require a balanced budget": 68% of Democrats (Economist/YouGov 2026), 69% (Cato/Morning Consult 2026), 79% (Harvard/Harris 2023).[^yougov-2026][^cato-2026][^harris-2023] Clears the bar; measures approval of the outcome, not the rule.
- "Balanced within 10 years": 83% of Republicans, 79% of Democrats (Harvard/Harris 2023, forced choice).[^harris-2023] Same limitation.
- Conditioned on entitlement cuts: 31% overall, 79% of Democrats opposed (Fox News 2011).[^fox-2011] Fails.
- Conditioned on tax increases: 62% opposed, no party split published (Fox News 2011); the method data above make the Republican floor the binding one.[^fox-2011][^gallup-2012][^apnorc-2023] Fails.
- Balance "without raising taxes or cutting benefits" by reducing waste: 64% agree it is possible (America's New Majority Project, advocacy poll, 2025).[^anmp-2025-arithmetic] A claim about arithmetic, not a rule; it cannot be drafted.

No framing both clears the bar and describes an enforceable constitutional rule.

## The path back

The category reopens on a modern, independent, split-sample poll that names the mechanism — a balanced-budget amendment even if it meant a specified mix of cuts to major programs and tax increases, with a supermajority waiver for war or recession — and clears roughly 60% in both parties, the Democratic number surviving the benefit-cut condition and the Republican number the tax condition. A weaker signal would be a rerun of the 2011 experiment with a drop under 15 points in each party. The poll is on our commissioned-polling list.
