## The proposal

As an amendment, "election technology and modernization" resolves into two ideas. The first is a right to cast a ballot over the internet or from a mobile phone, the version that has been polled. We identified no congressional joint resolution proposing it as constitutional text; the vehicles are state bills, including a 2026 Minnesota bill with chief authors from both parties.[^mn-sf4559] The second is a general mandate to modernize machines, registration systems, ballot design and audits — not one proposal but a name for dozens of statutory choices, all already governed by federal law. Article I, Section 4 lets Congress "make or alter" the manner of congressional elections (Article II lets it set the time of choosing presidential electors),[^art-i-4] and the Help America Vote Act of 2002 created the Election Assistance Commission to set voting-system guidelines and certify systems.[^hava]

## The evidence

The best independent measurement is AP-NORC's September 2020 survey: "allowing people to vote entirely online by submitting their ballot through a website" drew 28% favor and 51% oppose (AP-NORC, 2020).[^apnorc-sept-2020] AP-NORC published no party table for that item; we computed the split from its public-use file: 39% of Democrats, 15% of Republicans and 30% of independents in favor.[^apnorc-puf-sept-2020] At the height of the pandemic in April 2020 the same wording peaked at 40% overall — 48% of Democrats and 26% of Republicans by the same computation — the high-water mark.[^apnorc-april-2020]

MIT's Survey of the Performance of American Elections corroborates the level. In 2024, "allow voting over the Internet" drew about 33% overall, about 45% of Democrats and about 19% of Republicans (MIT Election Lab, 2024; read from the published chart).[^spae-2024] The 2022 edition recorded the only majority in the record — about 52% of Democrats, against about 18% of Republicans (MIT Election Lab, 2022) — and it was gone by 2024.[^spae-2022]

The security bodies treat the proposal as the risk: the National Academies wrote in 2018 that marked ballots "should not be returned over the Internet or any network connected to it,"[^nasem-2018] and CISA, the EAC, the FBI and NIST in 2020 called electronic ballot return "high-risk even with controls in place."[^cisa-2020] The one advocacy poll — Hart Research for Tusk Philanthropies, December 2021, D.C. voters only, reporting 70% support for "digital voting tools" — has no published wording, sample or party breakdown.[^hart-tusk-2021]

## The test it failed

The bipartisan floor is 15% (Republicans, AP-NORC 2020, computed from the public-use file), corroborated at about 19% (MIT Election Lab, 2024). Verdict: Weak. Four failure modes apply.

**Not popular on the topline.** Outside the pandemic spike, overall support has sat between 28% and 33%, and no independent poll since 2022 shows a majority in either party.[^apnorc-sept-2020][^spae-2024]

**Partisan-coded, with a stable gap.** Across four readings the gap has run 25 to 34 points; Democratic support has ranged from 39% to about 52%, Republican support has never exceeded 26%, and the split held across 2018–2024 regardless of which party held the presidency.[^apnorc-puf-sept-2020][^apnorc-april-2020][^spae-2022][^spae-2024] The mirror-image proposal is party-sorted too: "prohibit the use of computers to mark or count ballots" drew about 50% of Republicans and about 33% of Democrats (MIT Election Lab, 2024).[^spae-2024]

**The existential-problem test.** The mission admits only problems that threaten the country's future, and the security consensus is that this proposal would create a risk to elections rather than remove one.[^nasem-2018][^cisa-2020]

**The why-an-amendment test.** Article I, Section 4 already gives Congress the power over the manner of congressional elections,[^art-i-4] Congress used it in HAVA — passed 357–48 in the House and 92–2 in the Senate[^hava-house][^hava-senate] — and the political branches are still revising the standards without an amendment: a March 2025 executive order directs the EAC to amend its guidelines toward voter-verifiable paper records.[^eo-14248] Voting technology is machinery, and the mission leaves machinery to legislation.

## Framings we tested

- **"Allow voting over the internet":** 28–33% overall; about 15–19% of Republicans, 39–45% of Democrats (AP-NORC 2020; MIT Election Lab 2024). Weak.[^apnorc-puf-sept-2020][^spae-2024]
- **"Voting using cell phones"** — closest to the idea as filed: about 23% overall, about 14% of Republicans, about 30% of Democrats (MIT Election Lab, 2024). Fails by a wider margin.[^spae-2024]
- **"Secure" or "quantum-cryptography" internet voting:** never polled by an independent pollster; the security assessments say no current technology meets the bar, so the qualifier is a contested claim, not a framing.[^nasem-2018][^cisa-2020]
- **"Require electronic voting machines to print a paper backup":** 84% overall — 87% of Republicans and 82% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2025). Strong.[^pew-2025] It clears the bar, but it is the opposite instinct to the filed idea, it is where federal standards are already moving,[^eo-14248] and it is a statutory standard, not a constitutional principle.
- **"Select election officials on a nonpartisan basis":** about 70% of Republicans and about 80% of Democrats (MIT Election Lab, 2024). Clears the bar, but it is election administration, never polled as constitutional text.[^spae-2024]

None of the framings that clear the bar is what we would draft under this heading.

## The path back

None identified as an amendment; the category belongs on the statutory track. Reopening it would require independent national polling showing internet or cell-phone voting at 60% or better in both parties across two consecutive federal cycles, together with a reversal of the National Academies and CISA/EAC/FBI/NIST findings.[^nasem-2018][^cisa-2020] The why-an-amendment test would remain unmet even then, unless a court held that Congress lacks power under Article I, Section 4 to set voting-technology standards.
