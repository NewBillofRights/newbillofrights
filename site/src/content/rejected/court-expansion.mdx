## The proposal

The idea is to add seats to the Supreme Court — usually four, from nine to thirteen — for the sitting president and Senate to fill. Democratic-sponsored bills to that effect were introduced in 2021, 2023, and May 2026; none has received a committee vote.[^expansion-bills] The Constitution does not fix the Court's size: Congress set it at six in 1789 and has left it at nine since 1869.[^crs-size] The only amendment on the subject in Congress runs the other way — a Republican-sponsored proposal to fix the Court at nine.[^keep-nine-bills] The category holds two mirror-image proposals, each carried by one party.

## The evidence

Every independent poll since 2019 finds the same shape: a majority of Democrats in favor, a majority of Republicans opposed. The most favorable reading is the Marquette Law School Poll of May 2026, which offers no "not sure" option: 50% favor, 50% oppose — 39% of Republicans, 52% of independents, and 62% of Democrats in favor.[^marquette-2026] Support has ranged from 48% to 54% across nine Marquette polls since 2021.[^marquette-trend]

The other readings sit lower:

- Marquette, October 2024, same wording: 54% favor — 35% of Republicans, 56% of independents, 71% of Democrats — the month before a presidential election.[^marquette-2024]
- Pew Research Center, 2023: 46% favor — 27% of Republicans, 66% of Democrats.[^pew-2023]
- YouGov, 2024, with "not sure" offered: 37% support, 42% oppose — 18% of Republicans, 33% of independents, 59% of Democrats.[^yougov-2024]
- New York Times/Siena College, 2020, wording naming a party: 57% of Democrats in favor; 89% of Republicans and 65% of independents opposed.[^siena-2020]
- Navigator Research, May 2026, advocacy-commissioned by a Democratic-aligned firm: 40% support — net +27 among Democrats, net −27 and net −18 among the two Republican subgroups it reports (net figures only by party).[^navigator-2026]

The legislative record matches: no expansion bill has been marked up in any Congress; the fixed-at-nine amendment cleared the House Judiciary Committee 15–8 in June 2026 with no Democratic votes or cosponsors,[^hjres1-report] and the Democratic majority blocked floor consideration of an earlier version in 2022.[^keep-nine-2022]

## The test it failed

The bipartisan floor is 39% (Republicans, Marquette 2026), 27% in the Pew reading and 18% in YouGov's: Weak under every independent measurement since 2019. Four failure modes apply.

**High topline, low floor.** The 50–54% Marquette toplines rest on 62–71% Democratic support against 35–39% Republican; Pew's 46% rests on 66% against 27%.[^marquette-2026][^pew-2023]

**Support tracks who holds power.** Republican-appointed justices have been a majority continuously since the 1970–71 term, apart from fourteen months in 2016–17 when an eight-member Court was evenly divided by appointing party, so every number above was measured under one regime.[^marquette-2026] Within it, Democratic support fell from 71% to 62% and Republican support rose from 35% to 39% between October 2024 and May 2026 (Marquette), after Republicans regained the presidency.[^marquette-2024][^marquette-2026] Ratification takes 38 states over a decade or more — long enough for the sponsoring coalition to find itself on the other side of the rule.

**Why an amendment.** Expansion needs none. Congress can change the number by statute; the 2021 Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court recorded "widespread agreement among legal scholars that Congress has the constitutional authority to expand the Court's size."[^commission-2021] Only a rule that fixes the number requires an amendment, and that is a different proposal.

**Symmetry.** An amendment authorizing more seats cannot bind both parties equally; the benefit goes to whoever holds the presidency and Senate when it takes effect. A fixed-number rule is symmetric on paper, but its only votes are single-party and, taken alone, it entrenches the majority sitting when it is ratified.

## Framings we tested

No wording tested by an independent pollster brings the lower party above 39% (Marquette 2026); "expanding the size of the Supreme Court" reaches 18% of Republicans (YouGov 2024), and wording that attributes the move to one party 6% (57% of Democrats — NYT/Siena 2020).[^marquette-2026][^yougov-2024][^siena-2020] "Thirteen justices for thirteen circuits" has not been polled.[^markey-2021] For the fixed-at-nine amendment we found no independent poll with a party breakdown; opposition to expansion (61–72% of Republicans, 20–38% of Democrats) is the closest proxy.[^marquette-2026][^pew-2023]

Two framings clear the bar, and neither is expansion under another name. Fixed terms instead of life tenure draw 73% of Republicans and 87% of Democrats (Marquette, 2026) — "unlike expansion of the court," in Marquette's words, "term limits for justices enjoy strong bipartisan support."[^marquette-2026] An enforced ethics code draws 88% of Republicans and 93% of Democrats (Marquette, 2024).[^marquette-2024] The demand behind expansion — 55% saw the Court as motivated by politics in October 2024, up from 35% in 2019[^marquette-2024] — is real, and we have routed it to where both parties already agree: eighteen-year fixed terms with regular appointments in the Term Limits amendment, and an enforceable code in the Ethics amendment.

## The path back

We would reopen this category only if all three occur: an independent pollster shows at least 45% support in each party for a size rule written to bind both parties — a supermajority requirement to change the number, or a bare fixed-at-nine rule; that reading is replicated after the Court's appointing-party majority has changed hands; and a version is introduced with cosponsors from both parties. None has; no path back for expansion itself is identified.
