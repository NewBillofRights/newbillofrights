## The proposal

The idea is that the Constitution should say, in terms a court can enforce, that a president may not act outside the law: by withholding or spending money in defiance of an appropriation, by shielding evidence from Congress, or by claiming immunity from criminal law. Half of it already lives in our [Executive Compliance amendment](/amendments/executive-compliance), which carries the duty to obey the courts on the strongest floor in our record after term limits — 84% say a president must obey the Supreme Court: 78% of Republicans, 76% of independents, 92% of Democrats (Marquette Law School Poll, 2025).[^marquette-nov-2025] This page covers the remainder: three sub-items that do not share a result and are not treated as one proposal. **(a) Appropriations fidelity** — funds appropriated by law must be spent as provided by law, and no appropriation may be withheld, delayed, or terminated except by law; variants deny any foreign-affairs, emergency, transfer, or commander-in-chief authority to override a funding decision, or require a supermajority for large reallocations during a declared emergency. **(b) Executive privilege** — no privilege against congressional investigation of impoundment, election interference, or the conditioning of foreign aid. **(c) Immunity** — presidential immunity does not reach acts performed as a candidate: altering election returns, pressuring state election boards, disrupting the transfer of power. The third exists because of a constitutional holding: in July 2024 the Supreme Court held, 6–3, that former presidents have absolute immunity for conduct within the President's exclusive constitutional authority, at least presumptive immunity for other official acts, and none for unofficial acts.[^immunity-holding]

## The evidence

The only independent national reading of the spending question with a party split: asked in May 2025 whether "agencies and spending that have been authorized by Congress are beyond the president's authority to close or freeze" or whether "the president has the authority to take these actions without congressional approval," 62% said beyond the president's authority and 38% said the president has the authority — 37% of Republicans, 64% of independents, and 88% of Democrats chose "beyond" (Marquette Law School Poll, 2025).[^marquette-may-2025]

Other independent readings:

- On the principle: "when decisions by Congress or the Supreme Court hold our country back, the president should be able to ignore them" drew 16% agreement and 78% disagreement — 24% of Republicans, 14% of independents, and 10% of Democrats agreed, so roughly 76% of Republicans did not (PRRI, 2025).[^prri-2025]
- On immunity: 39% favored and 61% opposed the July 2024 decision — 68% of Republicans, 31% of independents, and 13% of Democrats in favor (Marquette Law School Poll, 2025).[^marquette-feb-2025] "Enacting a Constitutional amendment to make clear that no person is above the law, even the President of the United States" drew 54% of Republicans, with majorities of Democrats and independents whose figures were not published (USA Today/Ipsos, 2024).[^ipsos-2024]
- The one forward-looking wording: asked whether "the next president" should have more, less, or about as much control over federal departments and agencies, Republicans split 25% more, 20% less, 55% about the same; independents 21 / 46 / 33; Democrats 9 / 72 / 18 (Marquette Law School Poll, 2026).[^marquette-jul-2026]
- Without party breakdowns: "if a president wants to withhold funds that Congress has passed a law to spend," 46% said the funds should be spent, 24% withheld, and 30% were not sure (YouGov, 2025; no party breakdown published)[^yougov-feb-2025] — the highest don't-know share of any item here. A CBS News/YouGov survey (2025) reported majorities of all three parties saying Congress, not the president, should have the most authority over federal spending, but published no percentages.[^cbs-2025]
- Advocacy-commissioned: a States United Democracy Center/YouGov survey (2025) that named the sitting president found 47% of Republicans agreeing and 27% disagreeing that the president should be able to withhold money from federal agencies without congressional approval; 84% of Democrats and 54% of independents disagreed.[^states-united-2025] An Issue One/YouGov battery (2025) found at least 67% saying the congressional power of the purse strengthens government, with no party figures printed.[^issue-one-2025]
- Executive privilege against congressional oversight: we found no national poll from any pollster, independent or advocacy.

## Where it stands

**Appropriations fidelity (a).** The bipartisan floor is 37% of Republicans (Marquette, 2025): Weak. High topline, low floor: 62% overall conceals a 51-point party gap. Abuse-vs-remedy gap, the category's signature: roughly 76% of Republicans reject the principle that a president may ignore Congress (PRRI, 2025); 37% accept its application to spending (Marquette, 2025). Framing dependency: within one advocacy survey the Republican number runs from 27% disagreeing with withholding disaster relief to 47% agreeing the president should be able to withhold funds (States United/YouGov, 2025).[^states-united-2025] Regime coding: every reading is a verdict on a sitting president, and the one "next president" wording found 20% of Republicans wanting less control (Marquette, 2026) — the standard remedy, partly tried, did not lift the floor. Depth: no poll has named the rule's cost, that it would equally bar a future president from spending or forgiving beyond an appropriation. Staleness is cleared; fieldwork runs 2024–2026. The existential test passes: the statutory settlement is failing in practice — in September 2025 GAO found that the Impoundment Control Act does not permit withholding funds past their expiration, and the funds lapsed anyway;[^gao] two weeks later the Supreme Court stayed an order to obligate roughly $4 billion in expiring foreign aid, reading the Act to leave enforcement with one officer.[^avac] The symmetry test is open and decisive. A rule that a president spend neither less nor more than Congress appropriated has receipts pointing both ways — the Antideficiency Act bars spending beyond appropriations, and in June 2023 the Court held, 6–3, that a statute did not authorize canceling $430 billion in student-loan principal[^biden-v-nebraska] — but no pollster has asked the symmetric version. The why-an-amendment test is weak: the Impoundment Control Act already requires the President to spend what Congress appropriates, a pending bill would ban pocket rescissions by ordinary law,[^hr5220] and bills from the other party would repeal the Act;[^s515] no cross-party vote exists either way.

**Executive privilege (b).** Unpolled. The closest proxies are the abstract PRRI item and an Issue One battery in which 78% said congressional oversight strengthens the system, no party figures published[^issue-one-2025] — proxies, because neither names privilege. On the current record the existential test is not met, and the why-an-amendment answer is weak: privilege is common law, and Congress has never enacted the subpoena-enforcement mechanism it could enact by statute.

**Immunity (c).** Read as opposition to the 2024 holding, the floor is 32% of Republicans (Marquette, 2025): Weak. Read as an amendment, 54% of Republicans (USA Today/Ipsos, 2024): Moderate. Regime coding is the binding constraint: 68% of Republicans favoring the holding and 54% backing "no person is above the law, even the President" come from the same eighteen-month window and point opposite directions. Two tests pass — existential, because whether criminal law reaches a president is a rule-of-law question, and why-an-amendment, the strongest in the category, because a constitutional holding about Article II is one no statute can narrow. Symmetry is harder: a candidate-capacity carve-out is drafted against one fact pattern, with no matched pairing identified.

## Framings we tested

Two framings clear or approach the bar. "A president may ignore Congress or the Supreme Court when their decisions hold the country back" is rejected by roughly 76% of Republicans (PRRI, 2025)[^prri-2025] — Strong, but abstract, and it is the principle the Executive Compliance amendment already carries. "A Constitutional amendment to make clear that no person is above the law, even the President" drew 54% of Republicans (USA Today/Ipsos, 2024)[^ipsos-2024] — Moderate, and it is the operative principle put as an amendment, not an abuse framing wearing a remedy's name. Two caveats keep it from carrying a verdict alone: fieldwork in August 2024, during a campaign in which the holding was a live issue, and only the Republican figure published. Nothing else clears: the spending item, 37% (Marquette, 2025); disaster relief, 27% (States United, advocacy, 2025); "next president," 20% (Marquette, 2026); the immunity holding, 32% (Marquette, 2025). Four items have no usable floor: YouGov's impoundment question and an Economist/YouGov item on immunity "for any actions" as president (20% agree, 63% disagree, split only by 2024 vote)[^economist-yougov-2024] published no party breakdown; CBS/YouGov and Issue One published no percentages. The symmetric appropriations wording has never been asked, and executive privilege has never been asked at all.

## What would move it

In. For (a): an independent pollster fielding a forward-looking, two-directional question — "any future president must spend the money Congress has appropriated, no more and no less, and may not withhold, delay, or cancel funds without Congress voting to change the law; this would equally bar a president from freezing approved funds and from spending or forgiving money Congress never appropriated" — clearing 60% of Republicans and holding within the Moderate band under a costs-named follow-up. At that result the clause would belong in the Executive Compliance amendment, where an Appropriations Fidelity Clause is already drafted and held back. A recorded vote in which majorities of both parties back a pocket-rescission ban would count for as much. For (c): a re-ask of the Ipsos amendment wording with full party breakdowns, fielded outside a presidential campaign, clearing 60% of Republicans. For (b): any national poll — the first task is establishing that a measurable opinion exists.

Out. For (a): a symmetric, forward-looking question that still returns fewer than 45% of Republicans would fail high topline, low floor — the test that rejected [the Electoral College](/evidence/rejected/electoral-college) — and confirm the symmetry failure, routing the sub-item to the statutory list. For (b): a first poll showing the remedy splitting by party as DOJ independence does would fail abuse-vs-remedy. For (c): a re-ask of the amendment framing below 45% of Republicans once the incumbent is out of view would show the 54% was regime-coded in the other direction.
