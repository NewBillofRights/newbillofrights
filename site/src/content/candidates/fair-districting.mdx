## The proposal

This category is the part of the [Redistricting amendment](/amendments/redistricting) that we held back. That amendment governs *when* maps are drawn — once a decade, with a court-remedy exception. This candidate governs what a map may contain and who draws it, in two layers. The anti-favoritism floor: no districting plan may be drawn with the intent, or the effect, of unduly favoring or disfavoring a political party — the rule Florida wrote into its constitution in 2010.[^florida] The neutral-authority principle: each state vests districting in a body whose members are not, and are not appointed by, the officeholders whose districts it draws. The "single-member" half of the name is already federal statute and is not the live question.[^2c] Federal bills carrying both layers exist and have not moved.[^fair-act][^s2885]

## The evidence

The best independent number measures the principle, not either mechanism. Asked whether states should be allowed to draw congressional districts in a way that intentionally favors one party, 71% said no — 69% of Republicans, 74% of Democrats, 70% of independents (Economist/YouGov, 2026).[^yougov]

That is a condemnation of the abuse, not a rule about who draws maps or what a map may contain. On the mechanisms, the record is thinner:

- 82% prefer congressional districts drawn by nonpartisan commissions rather than by the party in power (NBC News / SurveyMonkey, 2025; no national party breakdown published) — 71% of Republicans in Republican-controlled states, 88% of Democrats in Democratic-controlled states.[^nbc]
- Asked whom they trust most to draw fair lines, 38% of Republicans and 45% of Democrats chose independent commissions over legislatures and courts, with 27% overall unsure (Noble Predictive Insights for The Center Square, 2026). A forced choice; no option reaches a majority.[^noble-cs]
- Advocacy-commissioned: it is bad for the country when one party controls how districts are drawn — 57% of Republicans, 76% of Democrats, 72% of independents (Noble Predictive Insights for Common Cause, 2025); the same poll put independent citizen commissions at 77% with no party split.[^cc] Gerrymandering is a "major problem" for "just over half" of Republicans, no exact figure published (AP-NORC, 2023).[^apnorc]

Ballot results cut both ways. Florida's anti-favoritism text passed with 62.93% against a 60% threshold (2010).[^florida] Commissions passed in Colorado with 71.37%,[^colorado] Michigan with 61.28%,[^michigan] and Utah with 50.34% (all 2018),[^utah] and Missouri's demographer-and-formula model with about 62% (2018).[^mo18] Missouri voters then repealed that formula with about 51% (2020),[^mo20] and Ohio's citizens' commission failed 53.71–46.29 (2024) after ballot language approved on a 3–2 party-line vote and partly rewritten by court order.[^ohio][^ohio-lang] In 2026 an effort to repeal Utah's commission fell short of the ballot after roughly 9,000 voters withdrew their signatures.[^utah26]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 57% of Republicans on the advocacy-commissioned diagnosis (Common Cause / Noble, 2025): Moderate. On either operative mechanism the verdict is Unpolled — searches of 2024–2026 fieldwork from nine independent pollsters found no national poll asking either question.

- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap.** Both parties reject the abuse (69% of Republicans, 74% of Democrats; Economist/YouGov, 2026); neither remedy has an independent measurement.
- **Depth.** 70% of Democrats called gerrymandering "never acceptable," yet 18% would keep California's independent commission if it cost their party seats (Politico / UC Berkeley Citrin Center, 2025) — a 52-point drop when the cost is named.[^citrin] On the other side, 66% of Texas Republicans preferred commissions while their state drew a co-partisan map (NBC News / SurveyMonkey, 2025).[^nbc]
- **High topline, low floor — at risk.** 82% for commissions with no national party split is the shape the gate exists to catch.
- **Support tracks who holds the maps — symmetrically.** With both Texas and California named, Republican opposition to out-of-cycle partisan redraws rose from 62% to 70% and Democratic opposition fell from 80% to 73% between September and November 2025 (Marquette Law School Poll).[^marquette]
- **Existential-problem test — passes.** One 2026 projection counts 81% of House seats as effectively decided before the election.[^fairvote]
- **Why-an-amendment test — passes for the anti-favoritism floor.** *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) held partisan-gerrymandering claims beyond the reach of the federal courts; the majority observed that "there is no 'Fair Districts Amendment' to the Federal Constitution."[^rucho] For neutral authority the answer is durability rather than necessity: commissions are already permitted,[^airc] and Missouri's was repealed by simple-majority referendum.[^mo20]
- **Symmetry — passes on anti-favoritism; open on neutral authority**, which federalizes a state institutional-design choice.

## Framings we tested

One framing clears the bar honestly: "states should not be allowed to draw districts that intentionally favor one party" — 69% of Republicans, 74% of Democrats (Economist/YouGov, 2026), Strong.[^yougov] It does not resolve the category: it is a condemnation, not a rule. "Nonpartisan commissions rather than the party in power" probably clears — 71% of Republicans in Republican-controlled states (NBC News / SurveyMonkey, 2025) — but no national Republican figure exists to verify it.[^nbc] Neither operative wording — "no plan shall be drawn with intent to favor or disfavor a party," or "districts shall be drawn by a body not composed of, or appointed by, the officeholders whose seats are drawn" — has been polled. Every framing above 65% among Republicans describes what states may not do; every framing that federalizes what states must build either drops to 38% or has never been asked. Florida's supreme court enforced its anti-favoritism sentence in 2015, striking the 2012 congressional map for partisan intent.[^lwv]

## What would move it

In: two independent national polls, both-parties and forward-looking, each clearing 60% of Republicans — one on the federalized Florida wording ("no congressional district may be drawn with the intent to favor or disfavor a political party, applying to every state and every future legislature, whichever party controls it") and one on the negative neutral-authority wording ("should the officeholders whose own districts are being drawn be barred from drawing them?") — each surviving a costs-named follow-up ("even if it means your party wins fewer seats") without dropping below Moderate. A second route: a fairness-standard or commission measure passing after 2024, on clean ballot language, in a state whose legislature opposed it.

Out: a replicated independent finding that the Republican floor on either operative wording sits below 45%, or a re-run trade-off question showing both parties' support collapsing when seats are the price. The failing test would be depth, reinforced by the abuse-vs-remedy gap.
