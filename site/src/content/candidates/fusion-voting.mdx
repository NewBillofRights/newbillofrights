## The proposal

Fusion voting — cross-nomination — lets more than one party nominate the same candidate; the candidate appears on each party's ballot line, and the votes on those lines are added together. It was ordinary practice through the nineteenth century and was banned by a wave of state statutes around 1900.[^history] It is legal today in New York and Connecticut and in limited forms elsewhere; most states ban it, and South Carolina enacted a ban as recently as 2022.[^states][^sc2022] No fusion amendment has been introduced in Congress. As commonly framed, one would guarantee a qualified party's right to nominate any consenting candidate, and a voter's right to vote for that candidate on that party's line, with the votes aggregated; a narrower variant would bar state laws forbidding a candidate from accepting more than one nomination. Ballot access, party qualification, and counting would stay with state law. No target amendment overlaps it.

## The evidence

One independent poll asks the mechanism directly with a party split, and it is a single state. Asked whether the state should "allow third parties to nominate the same person for an office as one of the major parties," 56% of New Jersey adults were in favor — 55% of Republicans, 61% of Democrats, 62% of independents (Fairleigh Dickinson University Poll, 2023).[^fdu] The six-point party gap is among the narrowest in our research. But it is a state sample, fielded while a fusion lawsuit was pending there, and it cannot carry a national verdict.

No national pollster has been found asking the mechanism; we searched Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, and the University of Maryland Program for Public Consultation. The national numbers are proxies for the diagnosis, not the remedy. "A third major U.S. political party is needed" drew 62% — 43% of Republicans, 58% of Democrats, 74% of independents (Gallup, 2025).[^gallup2025] "Wish there were more parties to choose from" drew 37% — 29% of Republicans and 46% of Democrats, leaners included (Pew Research Center, 2025).[^pew2025] Neither transfers to a ballot mechanism.

The courts supply the rest. *Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party* (1997) upheld Minnesota's fusion ban 6–3: "the Constitution permits the Minnesota Legislature to decide that political stability is best served through a healthy two party system."[^timmons] State-constitutional challenges have so far failed — New Jersey's courts upheld that state's ban in 2025,[^nj] and the Kansas Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal on August 14, 2026, with an appeal announced.[^kansas] A Wisconsin suit, fully briefed as of August 7, 2026, has plaintiffs who include two former Republican legislators and amici who include the state's Libertarian and Working Families parties.[^wisconsin] Most non-court sources on fusion come from organizations litigating for it.

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is Unpolled. The best direct reading, 55% of Republicans in one state (Fairleigh Dickinson, New Jersey, 2023), would be Moderate if it were national; it is an indication, not a floor. The proxies' floors — 43% of Republicans (Gallup, 2025), 29% of Republicans (Pew, 2025) — are Weak, and measure the diagnosis, not the remedy.

- **High topline, low floor; support flips with who holds power** — both demonstrated on the proxy, unknown on the mechanism. Gallup's 62% conceals 43% of Republicans, and its Republican reading fell from 58% (2023) to 43% (2025) while its Democratic reading rose from 46% to 58%; Gallup notes partisans' support varies with which party controls the White House.[^gallup2023][^gallup2025] The one mechanism reading has a six-point gap.
- **Depth** — untested; no survey has named a cost. **Staleness** — the only direct reading is February 2023, never re-asked.
- **The symmetry test** — passes by construction. Fusion works identically for a party to the right of the Republican Party and one to the left of the Democratic Party; New York's ballots have carried both a Conservative Party line and a Working Families Party line.[^states] An argument, not a measurement.
- **The existential-problem test** — open. For: anti-fusion laws were enacted to suppress competition and sustain a two-party structure a majority tells Gallup is not adequate. Against: the country has run for a century with fusion banned in most states and legal in two, with no demonstrated threat-level difference; the leading research synthesis calls the depolarization claim "more speculative."[^newamerica]
- **The why-an-amendment test** — partial. *Timmons* forecloses the federal constitutional route, and the state route has failed in New Jersey and Kansas. But fusion is legal by ordinary statute in New York and Connecticut, and *Timmons* holds only that states need not permit it. The amendment case reduces to uniformity and durability — a right held at the sufferance of fifty legislatures.

## Framings we tested

The state ballot-mechanism wording — 55% of Republicans, 61% of Democrats (Fairleigh Dickinson, New Jersey, 2023) — would be Moderate if it held nationally.[^fdu] The diagnosis wordings do not clear: "a third major party is needed," 43% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats (Gallup, 2025);[^gallup2025] "wish there were more parties," 29% and 46% (Pew, 2025).[^pew2025] Fusion as a constitutional right, and fusion with trade-offs named — longer ballots, a name appearing more than once, nomination bargaining — have never been asked. None clears the bar honestly, and the closest does not poll the thing we would draft: New Jersey's wording asks whether a state should allow cross-nomination; an amendment would establish the right against every state, permanently. Legislatures of both parties have banned fusion, three since 1999 — behavior, not opinion.

## What would move it

In: a national poll from an independent pollster, with party crosstabs, asking the mechanism in both-parties, forward-looking wording — "Some states allow more than one party to nominate the same candidate, so that a candidate can appear on more than one party's line and the votes are added together. Would you favor or oppose a constitutional amendment guaranteeing that right in every state — including for a new party to the right of the Republican Party and a new party to the left of the Democratic Party?" — with a Republican floor of 60% or better, holding at 55% when the costs are named. Or a state-constitutional ruling striking an anti-fusion law, or a fusion-restoring statute passed with cross-party majorities; Wisconsin and the Kansas appeal are the live cases.

Out: a national poll of the mechanism with a Republican or Democratic floor under 45%, or the New Jersey majority collapsing when trade-offs are named — a rejection on the full-spectrum and depth tests. Or a state restoring fusion by statute and the practice taking hold without federal action, which would send the category to the statutory list. Or a finding that fusion's effects are as modest as the research synthesis suggests, closing the existential-problem test against it.
