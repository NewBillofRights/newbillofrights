## The proposal

The Appointments Clause makes Senate consent a precondition for appointing judges and officers of the United States and says nothing about when the Senate must answer, or whether it must answer at all.[^art2] An appointment-default rule fills that silence: after a fixed period, inaction produces a defined outcome instead of an indefinite vacancy. Three variants are in circulation. Under "deemed confirmed," a nomination not voted on within a window — 90 days in the version that reached us — is treated as confirmed. Under a forced floor vote, the nomination is discharged for an up-or-down vote; the Senate keeps the power to reject and loses only the power to never answer, a design proposed in the reform literature since 2000.[^brookings2000] Under a knock-out rule, consent stays intact but obstruction gains nothing permanent: the seat sits vacant with the court operating as constituted, or the appointment power passes to another body, as Germany's constitutional-court amendments of December 2024 provide after three months of deadlock.[^germany] The Supreme Court version of the knock-out rule is the confirmation-refusal question our [Term Limits amendment](/amendments/term-limits) must settle for its staggered seats — the leading Senate bill for biennial appointments has no provision for Senate inaction[^s3096] — and this page concerns the remainder: a default rule for every nomination, to every office.

## The evidence

The mechanism has never been polled. We searched for national polling on a deadline for Senate action, a deemed-confirmed rule, or a guaranteed up-or-down vote across Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, and the University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation, and found nothing. Every question in the record asks about a particular pending nomination during a live confirmation fight. Those readings are proxies, and they point in opposite directions depending on when they were taken.

During a vacancy, the closest proxy is a Monmouth question asked in identical words four years apart: whether the Senate should consider a sitting president's end-of-term Supreme Court nomination, or put the vacancy on hold for voters. In March 2016, 36% of Republicans, 60% of independents, and 74% of Democrats said consider (Monmouth University, 2016; registered-voter comparison base).[^monmouth2016] In September 2020, 83% of Republicans, 48% of independents, and 16% of Democrats said consider (Monmouth University, 2020).[^monmouth2020] Each party moved roughly 50 points, in opposite directions, when the presidency changed hands. Gallup's matched pair on whether the Senate should vote in favor of the pending nominee shows the same reversal: 33% of Republicans and 76% of Democrats in 2016; 89% of Republicans and 15% of Democrats in 2020 (Gallup, 2016 and 2020).[^gallup2016][^gallup2020]

Before a vacancy, the record holds one measurement. Marquette Law School's Supreme Court survey, fielded September 8–15, 2020, before the vacancy that opened on September 18, asked whether senators would be justified in voting against a qualified nominee "simply because the Senator is from a different political party from the president who made the nomination." 78% said not justified — 79% of Republicans, 81% of independents, and 74% of Democrats (Marquette Law School Poll, 2020).[^marquette] It is a proxy of a different kind: it asks whether a senator would be justified in voting no, not whether the Constitution should impose a consequence for never voting at all.

## Where it stands

Verdict: Unpolled. No independent national poll with a party breakdown asks about the mechanism, and the nearest measurements run from a 16% floor to a 78% consensus depending on whether a seat was contested when the question was asked. That gap is the finding.

- **Support flips with who holds power** — demonstrated, not suspected. On identical wording the floor was 36% (Republicans, 2016) and 16% (Democrats, 2020): Weak both years, held by a different party each time (Monmouth), and corroborated by Gallup on a second wording. A poll fielded during a live vacancy has historically measured which party holds the presidency, not what Americans think of the rule.
- **Framing dependency** — untested. Deemed-confirmed, forced vote, and knock-out are three different proposals; no survey has distinguished them.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap** — differently shaped here. Both parties reject blockade-by-party in the abstract (78%, floors of 74% and 79%, Marquette 2020), and each has endorsed the practice when its own side ran it (16–36% floors, Monmouth). The variable is timing, not remedy wording.
- **Depth and staleness** — no survey has put the counterargument (a president could seat a judge the Senate never approved) to respondents, and nothing has been asked since 2020.
- **Symmetry** — passes. The rule binds every future Senate and benefits every future president, and the record pairs cleanly: the 2016 vacancy was held open by a Senate of one party against a president of the other, the 2020 vacancy was filled at speed by the same Senate for its own president, and each party's voters approved the procedure that suited them in the year it suited them.
- **Why an amendment** — passes with a qualification. The clause imposes no duty to act and scholars divide on whether one exists,[^ncc-ramsey][^ncc-ulrich] so a rule that depends on which reading prevails is not a rule, and a Senate standing order adopted by simple majority can be undone by the next. The qualification: in September 2025 the Senate changed its rules by simple majority to confirm executive nominees in bundles, evidence that it can fix delay when it wants to; bundling does nothing for a nominee a majority prefers never to answer.[^brookings2025]
- **Existential problem** — open, and the test holding the category. One reading: a seat that can be held empty indefinitely converts an appointment power into one chamber's discretion. The other: this is a scheduling preference; vacancies have been absorbed throughout the system's history and elections remain the remedy. Both are defensible on the current record.

## Framings we tested

One framing clears the bar. Marquette's party-of-the-president item — 78% overall, 79% of Republicans, 81% of independents, 74% of Democrats (Marquette Law School Poll, 2020) — is a Strong reading from a probability panel with no live fight to code the answer.[^marquette] It does not resolve the category: it is a norm about how senators should vote, not a constitutional default for a Senate that never votes. In March 2016, 69% said the Senate should hold hearings on the sitting president's nominee — 56% of Republicans, 66% of independents, 85% of Democrats (Monmouth University, 2016) — a Moderate floor recorded against the respondents' own side's immediate interest; the parallel 2020 item drew 53%, with no party breakdown published (Monmouth University, 2020).[^monmouth2016][^monmouth2020] Every nominee-specific framing fails: Pew's March 2016 question on confirming the pending nominee drew 22% of Republicans and 70% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2016; no independent figure published),[^pew2016b] and the Gallup pair flips as above. Deemed-confirmed, forced vote, and knock-out have never been asked.

## What would move it

In: a forward-looking, mechanism-specific poll from an independent pollster, fielded when no seat is contested, testing the variants separately in both-parties wording — for example, "When any president nominates someone to a federal office or judgeship, should the Senate be required to hold an up-or-down vote within a set period, say 120 days, or should the Senate be able to decline to vote at all?" — with deemed-confirmed asked and reported separately, Marquette's party-of-the-president item carried in the same instrument as an anchor, and the counterargument stated to respondents. A bipartisan floor at or above 60% on the forced-vote variant clears the bar; 45–59% is Moderate; deemed-confirmed is held to the same 60%.

Out: a forward-looking poll in which either party's floor lands below 45% on every variant would fail the high-topline/low-floor and regime-coding tests. Independently, if answers still track which party holds the presidency even in wording that names no incumbent, the category fails the regime-coding test regardless of the level.
