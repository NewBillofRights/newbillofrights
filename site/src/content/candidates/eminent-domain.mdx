## The proposal

In *Kelo v. City of New London* (2005), a 5–4 Court held that taking fifteen unblighted homes and lots for a private redevelopment plan was a "public use" under the Fifth Amendment, because the Court had long read "public use" as "public purpose"; it added that nothing in the opinion precluded a state from restricting the takings power further.[^kelo] Because the Takings Clause binds the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, that reading is the federal floor for every level of government.[^cbq] The amendment as commonly framed would define "public use" in the Fifth Amendment itself: no government may take private property in order to transfer it to another private party for economic development. The state record supplies three variants — a flat ban on private-to-private transfer for development, as New Hampshire's 2006 amendment reads; a narrow public-use definition with an exception for blight defined as a danger to health and safety, as in South Carolina and Texas; and heightened compensation or process, such as Michigan's 125% of market value for a principal residence.[^ij-list] Most versions exempt common carriers and utilities and leave procedure, compensation, and blight findings to legislation.

## The evidence

The only national poll with a party breakdown is from the autumn after the decision. Asked about the *Kelo* decision in October–November 2005, 81% of Americans opposed it — 85% of Republicans, 79% of Democrats, and 83% of independents (Saint Index, University of Massachusetts Lowell Center for Economic and Civic Opinion, 2005). The Saint Index is a land-use survey series sponsored by a consulting firm and fielded by the UMass Lowell center; two House Judiciary Committee reports reproduce its figures.[^saint]

The rest of the record corroborates the direction without refreshing the party split:

- Twelve states amended their constitutions by popular vote between 2006 and 2012. The clean measures — limiting takings for private transfer and nothing else — passed by wide margins of 60% to 86%: South Carolina 86%, New Hampshire 85.7%, Georgia 82.7%, Texas 81.0%, Michigan 80.1%, Virginia 74.5%, Mississippi 73.1%, Florida 69.1%, North Dakota 67.5%, Nevada 63.1% (and 60.8% on the required second vote), California 62.0%, and Louisiana 54.8% on a September ballot.[^ballots] Every measure that failed was bundled with regulatory-takings compensation or rent-control repeal: California 47.6%, Washington 41.2%, and Idaho 23.9% in 2006, and California's Proposition 98 at 38.5% in June 2008 — the same electorate that passed the clean Proposition 99 at 62.0% that day.[^bundled] Nevada voters later refused, at 32.7%, a legislative attempt to revise their protections (2010).[^nevada2010]
- The House passed the Private Property Rights Protection Act 376–38 in November 2005 — Republicans 218–2, Democrats 157–36.[^roll568] It passed the House again in 2012 (voice vote), 2014 (353–65; Republicans 226–0, Democrats 127–65), and 2018 (voice vote). The Senate never passed it.[^laterbills]
- The newest independent party split is state-level and adjacent: 78% of Iowans opposed companies using eminent domain to build carbon-capture pipelines — 72% of Republicans, 82% of Democrats, and 79% of independents (Selzer & Co. for the Des Moines Register, 2023). Pipelines are common carriers, which most *Kelo* reforms exempt, so this is a proxy.[^iowa]
- Two advocacy-commissioned polls point the same way and carry no party split: 95% opposed *Kelo* (Zogby for the American Farm Bureau Federation, 2005), and 81% of voters in six Midwestern states opposed "corporations utilizing eminent domain for private projects" (Embold Research for Bold Alliance, 2024).[^advocacy] An automated survey found 66% opposed to the law allowing takings for public use "including … commercial development" (Rasmussen Reports, 2014; no party breakdown published).[^rasmussen]

We searched for and did not find any national poll since 2014 by Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, or Cato asking about takings for private development with a party breakdown.

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 79% of Democrats (Saint Index / UMass Lowell, 2005): Strong, with a six-point gap between the parties. The number is two decades old, and staleness is the first test at risk. Nothing since suggests it moved — the ballot record runs from Florida's 69.1% in 2006 to Virginia's 74.5% in 2012 without decay, and the 2023 Iowa reading sits in the same band — but nothing measures it either.

Depth is the second caution. At the height of the controversy, 40% of New Jersey adults had heard nothing about eminent domain. In the same survey, 47% agreed "there are times when it is O.K. to use eminent domain to rebuild an area," while taking "low value homes from people in order to build a shopping center" was acceptable to 4% (Monmouth University, 2005; New Jersey; no party breakdown published).[^monmouth] Which of those two sentences an amendment sounds like matters. Framing dependency is well measured and cuts the other way: the bundled measures lost by 15 to 60 points where the clean ones won.[^bundled]

The one partisan pattern is on the federal remedy, not the principle. House Democrats split 39–157 on a 2005 appropriations rider cutting off funds for takings for private development, then voted 157–36 for the standalone bill five months later and 127–65 in 2014;[^roll350] roughly a third of House Democrats have voted against the federal funding-cutoff mechanism, and the 2018 committee dissent objected on federalism and local-budget grounds.[^hrept115]

The symmetry test is met by construction: a public-use definition binds every level of government and every future majority. Reforms passed under Republican-led and Democratic-led legislatures; a Democratic governor's veto in Iowa was overridden, and a Republican governor's opposition in Mississippi was answered by voters at 73.1%.[^symmetry] The why-an-amendment test has a precise answer: "public use" is constitutional text, only the Court or an amendment can change its meaning, and the statutory route — a spending condition — has passed the House four times and never the Senate.[^laterbills]

The open test is the existential-problem test. Economic-development takings are rare in practice; forty-three to forty-five states have reformed their laws, though a 2009 study found seventeen of the first twenty-eight reforms largely symbolic;[^states] the federal government binds itself by executive order and appropriations rider;[^eo] and two sitting justices have asked the Court to reconsider *Kelo* (2021).[^cert] Against that: the decision remains the federal floor, one state has never reformed, one rolled its reform back, and this would be the only proposal on the slate restoring a clause already in the Bill of Rights.[^states] Whether that is a threat to the country's future or a policy preference is the question the category is waiting on.

## Framings we tested

The clean private-transfer ban clears the bar in every form it has been asked. "Prohibit government from taking private property for transfer to another private individual or business for economic development or increasing tax revenue" won 80.1% in Michigan (2006); the public-use-only wording with a health-and-safety blight exception won 86% in South Carolina (2006) and 81.0% in Texas (2009); a version confined to owner-occupied homes won 62.0% in California (2008).[^ballots] Ballot results carry no party breakdown, but the states span red, blue, and purple. Two framings do not clear. Anything that attaches regulatory-takings compensation or rent-control repeal has been rejected by voters five times.[^bundled] And the redevelopment framing — "rebuild an area" — drew a 47%–39% plurality (Monmouth, 2005),[^monmouth] which is why the wording of the ban and of its blight exception decide what number an amendment earns.

## What would move it

In: a fresh national poll with party breakdowns on forward-looking, mechanism-specific wording — for example, "no government — federal, state, or local — may take a person's home, business, farm, or land in order to transfer it to another private owner for economic development, even if the owner is paid fair market value" — clearing 60% in both parties when the exceptions (health-and-safety blight; roads, utilities, and pipelines open to the public) and the costs (some redevelopment projects, stadiums, or shopping centers cannot be built) are named. That would settle staleness; the existential-problem test would remain to be decided.

Out: the same poll returning a floor under 60% with exceptions and costs named would fail the depth test; a national result that turned on "rebuild an area" versus "homes for a shopping center" would fail on framing dependency; or a decision that a problem this rare, this widely remedied by the states, and reversible by the Court does not meet the existential-problem test would close the category on that test alone.
