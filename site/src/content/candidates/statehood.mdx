## The proposal

Two jurisdictions share one grievance — residents taxed and drafted who send no voting member to either chamber of Congress — and differ in law. Puerto Rico's admission is an ordinary statute: Article IV, Section 3 provides that "New states may be admitted by the Congress into this union," and thirty-seven states have entered under it without an amendment.[^art4] For the District of Columbia, the enclave clause gives Congress "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" over the seat of government,[^art1] and the Twenty-third Amendment grants presidential electors to "the District constituting the seat of government."[^amend23] Admission bills shrink the federal district to a capital core and admit the remainder, leaving the Twenty-third Amendment attached to a residual district with a very small population. The amendment as commonly framed would repeal the Twenty-third Amendment and provide for admission of the District's residential territory — or, in the alternative, give the District's residents congressional representation directly, without statehood.

## The evidence

The two jurisdictions are gated separately. For the District, the floor of record: asked about "making Washington, D.C., a separate state" in March 2021, 13% of Republicans supported and 64% opposed, 64% of Democrats supported and 13% opposed, and independents split 32% to 37% (Yahoo News/YouGov, 2021). Since July 2020, Democratic support had risen from 49% to 64% and Republican support had fallen from 23% to 13%.[^yahoo]

For Puerto Rico, the newest independent reading is conditional: "If the residents of Puerto Rico voted in favor of it, would you support or oppose making Puerto Rico a U.S. state?" drew 59% support — 79% of Democrats, 56% of independents, and 41% of Republicans (YouGov, 2024).[^yougov2024]

Gallup, in one June 2019 survey, put Puerto Rico statehood at 66% favor — 45% of Republicans, 69% of independents, 83% of Democrats, the highest Republican reading on record — and "making Washington, D.C., a separate state" at 29% favor: 15% of Republicans, 30% of independents, 39% of Democrats.[^gallup-pr][^gallup-dc] Morning Consult/Politico (2021) found DC at 40% support, with 62% of Democrats in support and 59% of Republicans opposed; 74% of opponents said "D.C. was never meant to be a state," against 17% citing partisan concerns.[^mc] Ballot results measure local demand, not national consent: statehood took 58.61% of valid votes in Puerto Rico's 2024 plebiscite, but 14.34% of ballots were left blank, putting it closer to 49% of all ballots cast;[^pr2024] the 2020 vote was 52.52% yes;[^pr2020] the District's 2016 advisory referendum was 85.69% yes.[^dc2016] Roll calls point in opposite directions: the Puerto Rico Status Act passed the House 233–191 in 2022 with 16 Republican yeas;[^roll529] the DC admission bill passed 216–208 in 2021 with Republicans 0–208, its predecessor 232–180 in 2020 with Republicans 0–178,[^roll132][^roll122] and the reintroduced bill carries 208 cosponsors, all from one party.[^hr51] An advocacy-commissioned 2026 poll of Latino likely voters (McLaughlin & Associates for the Puerto Rico Statehood Council) published no party breakdown of the national electorate and cannot carry a verdict.[^mclaughlin]

## Where it stands

**District of Columbia.** The bipartisan floor is 13% of Republicans (Yahoo News/YouGov, 2021): Weak, the lowest floor in our corpus, replicated at 13% of Republicans and 71% of Democrats (Washington Examiner/YouGov, 2020)[^examiner] and 15% (Gallup, 2019). Gallup's 39% among Democrats means the proposal did not command a majority in the more supportive party in that survey. Staleness applies: no national reading since April 2021. Support that moves with who holds power shows in the trend — Democrats up 15 points and Republicans down 10 across eight months spanning a change in Senate control and a House vote. The symmetry test fails as the idea is currently understood: admission is a one-time act with a known partisan result, and prospective dates and grandfathering do not neutralize it. Two tests are open. The why-an-amendment test, because repealing the Twenty-third Amendment requires an amendment. And the existential-problem test, the strongest thing in the file: roughly 700,000 citizens taxed without voting representation is a democracy claim, not a policy preference.

**Puerto Rico.** The floor is 41% of Republicans (YouGov, 2024): Weak, four points below Moderate; the best independent Republican reading is 45% (Gallup, 2019), and six independent readings run from 21% to 45% of Republicans against 63% to 86% of Democrats (Economist/YouGov, 2021; Washington Examiner/YouGov, 2020).[^economist][^examiner] High topline, low floor applies: 59% and 66% overall conceal the split. The why-an-amendment test fails: Article IV, Section 3 supplies the mechanism, Congress can and does use it, and a statutory idea is parked rather than drafted. The existential-problem test is weaker here, because residents can resolve their own status among statehood, independence, and free association and have not converged.

## Framings we tested

No framing clears the bar for either jurisdiction. For DC, a "taxation without representation" argument frame reached 42% of Republicans and 73% of Democrats in an advocacy poll (Data for Progress, 2021), the highest Republican share that pollster found; its plain topline was 34% and 74%.[^dfp][^cbs] For Puerto Rico, the conditional wording produces the 41%; the unconditional Gallup ask reached 45%; and when the question named the federal-power consequence — Puerto Rico voting for President — Republican support fell from 43% to 38% within one survey (YouGov, 2019).[^yougov2019] Two framings have never been tested by an independent pollster: congressional representation for the District without statehood, and a binding federal process for Puerto Rico's status. Neither has a number.

## What would move it

In: for DC, an independent national poll with 2026-or-later fieldwork asking, in forward-looking both-parties wording, whether the roughly 700,000 U.S. citizens who live in the District should have voting representation in the House and Senate, followed by the mechanism — statehood, representation by amendment without statehood, or retrocession to Maryland — with the remedy, not just the grievance, at 60% or more among Republicans; or a cross-party roll call in either chamber on any DC representation measure. For Puerto Rico, no poll moves it in as an amendment; a Strong reading would move it onto the statutory list.

Out: for DC, a 2026 independent reading below 45% of Republicans would close it on the bipartisan-floor test, on top of three pollsters at 13–15% and two House passages with zero Republican yeas. Puerto Rico is out as an amendment on the why-an-amendment test regardless of polling, and out on the floor as well if a 2026 reading holds below 45% of Republicans.
