## The proposal

The idea is that the government's account of itself — what it takes in, what it spends, and what its programs achieve — should rest on a constitutional footing rather than on statute and appropriation. Nobody has drafted it. Two shapes are under evaluation: a publication duty, requiring accurate and complete accounts of revenues, spending, population, and program results on a regular schedule; and an integrity guarantee, requiring that official statistics be produced by independent agencies free from political direction as to their substance, conclusions, or timing.[^hr4907] A third shape, a personal right of access to government records, belongs to the [rejected civic-information category](/evidence/rejected/civic-information) and is not evaluated here.

## The evidence

No independent national poll asks whether the Constitution should require accurate, nonpartisan official statistics, or whether the heads of statistical agencies should be protected from political removal. We searched for both questions and found neither, so this page carries no chart. What exists measures whether Americans believe the government's numbers and whether they think facts should be accurate.

The trust readings are fresh and split by party. In February–March 2026, 40% of Americans trusted "data reported about the economy by the federal government" — 67% of Republicans and 22% of Democrats (Economist/YouGov, 2026); the Democratic figure had been 54% in March 2025.[^yougov-2026] The same pollster's April 2022 wave, under the previous administration, ran the other way: Republicans were more likely to distrust than to trust each of twelve government statistics polled, and Democrats more likely to trust each (Economist/YouGov, 2022).[^yougov-2022] Each party has held each position.

The remaining readings are broad, old, or both. At least 8 in 10 Republicans and 8 in 10 Democrats agreed that facts must be "accurate, complete, and transparent" (AP-NORC/USAFacts, 2019).[^apnorc-2019] The one data-specific item with a party split: 53% said open-government data could make officials more accountable — 45% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2014 fieldwork).[^pew-2014] 49% said federal information is influenced by the president's political beliefs (AP-NORC/USAFacts, 2024; no party breakdown published).[^apnorc-2024] An advocacy-commissioned poll found 91% of Republicans and 90% of Democrats favor evidence-based policymaking (Echelon Insights for Results for America, 2026).[^echelon]

Congress has voted on the statutory version. The Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act, which requires each federal statistical agency to conduct "credible and accurate" and "objective" statistical activities, passed the House 356–17 in 2018 and the Senate by unanimous consent.[^evidence-act][^roll484] A 2025 bill that would allow removal of the heads of four statistical agencies only for cause, and never for "the substance, conclusions, or timing" of a release, has a sponsor and cosponsors from one party and no floor action.[^hr4907]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is Unpolled. The nearest data-specific proxy is Weak — 45% of Republicans (Pew, 2014) — and twelve years old; the supermajority readings are statements about facts in general, not a mandate. The tests that apply:

- **Support flips with who holds power.** Measured, not feared: 67% of Republicans and 22% of Democrats trust federal economic data now; in 2022 Republicans distrusted each statistic polled and Democrats trusted each (Economist/YouGov, 2022, 2026).[^yougov-2026][^yougov-2022] A floor measured in any single year would be measured against one administration.
- **High topline, low floor — at risk.** The 90% readings sit on wording no constitutional text would change; the one data-specific split lands at 45% of Republicans.[^pew-2014]
- **Depth — untested.** No survey has named a cost: enforcement against the executive, insulation of agency heads from elected accountability, or the expense of collection.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — likely.** About half say federal information is politically influenced;[^apnorc-2024] the remedy is unmeasured, and the only remedy bill has one party's cosponsors.[^hr4907]
- **The existential-problem test — open, and deciding.** The record since 2025 supplies a new argument: the head of a federal statistical agency was dismissed in August 2025 after a jobs report the administration disputed, and one estimate puts the output lost to the resulting uncertainty at roughly $20 billion;[^cepr] in 2019 the Supreme Court found the stated reason for a census question "contrived."[^commerce] The country has nonetheless run on statute here since 1790 without the counting itself failing.
- **The why-an-amendment test — fails on the present record.** The text is not silent: the Enumeration Clause commands a decennial count, and the Statement and Account Clause requires publication of "receipts and expenditures of all public money."[^const] Congress can bind itself and did, 356–17.[^roll484] The one gap is enforcement: in 1974 the Supreme Court held that a taxpayer lacks standing to enforce the Statement and Account Clause,[^richardson] so the existing duty has no plaintiff. That argues for a standing clause — and enforcement machinery has historically been the unpolled or party-coded part of any proposal.

## Framings we tested

No framing clears the bar honestly. Two post supermajorities — facts must be accurate, complete, and transparent (at least 80% of each party, AP-NORC/USAFacts, 2019),[^apnorc-2019] and evidence-based policymaking should guide government (91% of Republicans, 90% of Democrats; Echelon for Results for America, 2026, advocacy-commissioned)[^echelon] — but each is a statement no one opposes and no constitutional text would change: a different proposal wearing the name. The only framing measured on the subject itself with a party split — open data making officials more accountable — lands at 45% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats (Pew, 2014).[^pew-2014] The two framings that are the actual proposal, a constitutional duty to publish revenues, spending, and population, and for-cause-only removal of statistical-agency heads, have never been asked.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll with published party crosstabs asking both mechanisms, forward-looking — "The Constitution should require the federal government to publish accurate and complete accounts of what it takes in, what it spends, and the size and condition of the population, on a regular schedule set by law," and "The head of a federal statistical agency should be removable only for cause, and never because of the substance, conclusions, or timing of a statistical release — this rule would apply to every future president of either party" — each clearing a 60% floor in the less-supportive party, with the second re-asked after naming the cost and staying out of the Weak band. A cross-party floor vote on the 2025 bill or a successor, or a state constitutional amendment on official-statistics integrity passing by a wide margin in a state of the opposite partisan lean, would corroborate.

Out: if those questions come back Weak, or the floor swaps parties between waves under different administrations, the category fails on regime coding. If we resolve the existential-problem test as we did for civic information — a good-government improvement rather than a threat to the country's future — it fails that test, and the why-an-amendment answer is already weak.
