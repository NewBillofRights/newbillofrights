## The proposal

The idea is that the government's account of itself — what it takes in, what it spends, and what its programs achieve — should rest on the Constitution rather than on statute. Nobody has drafted it. Two shapes are under evaluation: a publication duty — accurate, complete accounts of revenues, spending, population, and program results, on a regular schedule — and an integrity guarantee — official statistics produced by independent agencies, free from political direction as to their substance, conclusions, or timing.[^hr4907] A third shape, a right of access to records, belongs to the [rejected civic-information category](/rejected/civic-information).

## The evidence

No independent national poll asks whether the Constitution should require accurate, nonpartisan official statistics, or whether statistical-agency heads should be shielded from political removal. We searched for both and found neither, so there is no chart.

The trust readings are fresh and split by party: 40% trust "data reported about the economy by the federal government" — 67% of Republicans and 22% of Democrats, the latter down from 54% in March 2025 (Economist/YouGov, 2026).[^yougov-2026] The same pollster measured 53% trust overall in March 2025 and 42% trust against 45% distrust by early August 2025 (Economist/YouGov, 2025; no party breakdown published in either wave).[^yougov-2025] In April 2022, under the previous administration, Republicans were more likely to distrust than to trust each of twelve government statistics polled, Democrats more likely to trust each (Economist/YouGov, 2022).[^yougov-2022] Each party has held each position.

Dissatisfaction with official information, unlike trust in the numbers, is bipartisan: 76% said information about government expenditures and outcomes is biased — 81% of Republicans, 72% of Democrats, 76% of independents (USAFacts/Harris, 2017; sponsor-commissioned).[^usafacts-2017] The rest is broad, old, or both: at least 8 in 10 of each party agreed that facts must be "accurate, complete, and transparent" (exact party figures not published) (AP-NORC/USAFacts, 2019);[^apnorc-2019] 53% said open-government data could make officials more accountable — 45% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2014);[^pew-2014] and 91% of Republicans and 90% of Democrats favor evidence-based policymaking (Echelon Insights for Results for America, 2026; advocacy-commissioned).[^echelon]

Congress voted on the statutory version: the Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act, requiring "credible and accurate" and "objective" official statistics, passed the House 356–17 in 2018 — Republicans 182–17, Democrats 174–0 — and the Senate by unanimous consent.[^evidence-act][^roll484] A 2025 bill making four statistical-agency heads removable only for cause has a sponsor and five cosponsors, all from one party, and no floor action.[^hr4907]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is Unpolled. The nearest data-specific proxy, 45% of Republicans (Pew, 2014), sits at the bottom of the Moderate band and is twelve years old. The tests that apply:

- **Support flips with who holds power.** Measured, not feared: 67% of Republicans and 22% of Democrats trust federal economic data now, and the positions were reversed in 2022 (Economist/YouGov, 2022, 2026).[^yougov-2026][^yougov-2022] Any floor measured in one year would be measured against one administration.
- **Depth — untested.** No survey has named a cost — enforcement against the executive, agency heads insulated from elected accountability, the expense of collection.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — likely.** The abuse half is measured and bipartisan, 81% of Republicans and 72% of Democrats (USAFacts/Harris, 2017);[^usafacts-2017] 49% say federal information is influenced by the president's political beliefs (AP-NORC/USAFacts, 2024; no party breakdown published).[^apnorc-2024] The remedy half is unmeasured, and the only remedy bill has single-party cosponsors.[^hr4907]
- **The existential-problem test — open, and deciding.** The record since 2025 supplies a new argument: a statistical-agency head was dismissed in August 2025 after a disputed jobs report, at an estimated $20 billion in output lost to the resulting uncertainty;[^cepr] in 2019 the Supreme Court found the stated reason for a census question "contrived."[^commerce] Against that: the country has run on statute since 1790 without the counting itself failing.
- **The why-an-amendment test — fails on the present record.** The text is not silent: Article I already commands a decennial count and publication of "receipts and expenditures of all public money."[^const] Congress can bind itself and did, 356–17, with no member of one party's caucus voting no and 91% of the other's voting yes.[^roll484] The gap is enforcement: in 1974 the Supreme Court held a taxpayer lacks standing to enforce the publication clause,[^richardson] so the duty has no plaintiff — which argues for a standing clause, historically the unpolled or party-coded part of any proposal.

## Framings we tested

No framing clears the bar. The two supermajorities — facts must be accurate, complete, and transparent (AP-NORC/USAFacts, 2019);[^apnorc-2019] evidence-based policymaking should guide government (Echelon for Results for America, 2026, advocacy-commissioned)[^echelon] — are statements no one opposes and no constitutional text would change: a different proposal wearing the name. The one framing with a genuinely bipartisan party split, that official information is biased,[^usafacts-2017] measures the complaint, not the cure. The only mandate-adjacent framing with a party split, open data making officials more accountable, lands at the bottom of Moderate, 45% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats (Pew, 2014).[^pew-2014] The two framings that are the proposal itself have never been asked.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll with party crosstabs asking both mechanisms, forward-looking — a constitutional duty to "publish accurate and complete accounts of what it takes in, what it spends, and the size and condition of the population, on a regular schedule set by law," and a statistical-agency head "removable only for cause, and never because of the substance, conclusions, or timing of a statistical release," binding "every future president of either party" — each clearing a 60% floor in the less-supportive party and staying out of the Weak band once the cost is named. A cross-party floor vote on the 2025 bill, or a wide-margin state statistical-integrity amendment in a state of the opposite lean, would corroborate.

Out: those questions returning Weak, or a floor that swaps parties between waves under different administrations, fails regime coding; resolving the existential-problem test as we did for civic information — an improvement in government, not a threat to the country's future — would fail that test.
