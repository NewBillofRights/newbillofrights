## The proposal

The filibuster is not in the Constitution. It is a Senate rule: since 1975, Rule XXII has required three-fifths of senators — 60 votes — to end debate on most matters; the first cloture rule, in 1917, set the bar at two-thirds of those voting.[^history] Amending the rule takes two-thirds present and voting, so in practice the threshold has been changed by a majority overruling the chair.[^crs] Four distinct proposals travel under the category name. Abolition — legislation passes by simple majority — is the version pollsters ask about, and it needs no amendment. Reform short of abolition would restore the "talking" filibuster, require 41 senators to sustain one, or carve out categories of bills. A third would constitutionalize simple-majority passage. A fourth inverts the rule: a supermajority to block a bill rather than to advance one — symmetric on its face, and never polled. We found no bill or resolution to constitutionalize any version.

## The evidence

The strongest evidence here is a pair of roll calls, not a poll. On November 21, 2013, the Senate established simple-majority cloture for executive and most judicial nominations by declining to sustain the chair, 48–52: the 48 were all Republicans, the 52 were 50 Democrats and 2 independents.[^rc2013] On April 6, 2017, the same procedure reached Supreme Court nominations, 48–52 again — the 48 now Democrats and independents, the 52 Republicans.[^rc2017] On January 19, 2022, a talking-filibuster exception for one bill failed 52–48, the 52 being 50 Republicans and 2 Democrats.[^rc2022] No appeal has drawn a cross-party majority. The polling runs the same way. Asked whether the Senate should "get rid of the filibuster entirely, keep it but reform how it can be used, or keep it as is with no changes," 17% chose elimination — 8% of Republicans, 16% of independents, 30% of Democrats (Monmouth University, 2022).[^monmouth2022]

Nine months earlier the same question read 10% of Republicans, 16% of independents, 30% of Democrats (Monmouth University, 2021); both waves ran under a Democratic Senate majority.[^monmouth2021]

- Asked whether the filibuster is a good or bad thing, Republicans said good 68–32 and Democrats said bad 68–32 (Economist/YouGov, 2021) — a mirror image the pollster attributed to which party controls the chamber.[^yougov-may2021]
- "Changing the rules to eliminate the filibuster" drew 66% of Democrats in favor and 74% of Republicans opposed (Rasmussen Reports, 2022).[^rasmussen2022] Under a Republican majority, opposition fell from 48% to 44% (Rasmussen Reports, 2025; party breakdown not verifiable), which the pollster attributed to the change in control.[^rasmussen2025]
- Cloture motions filed rose from 39 in the 94th Congress (1975–76) to 286 in the 119th (2025–26).[^cloture]
- Advocacy polling, flagged: getting rid of "the loophole that allows a small minority of U.S. senators to block legislation that a majority of senators support" would have a positive impact, said 51% of Republicans, 49% of independents, 71% of Democrats (Navigator Research, Democratic-aligned, 2024) — impact, not support, with the case argued inside the question.[^navigator2024]

The major reform batteries — Pew (2023), Cato/Morning Consult (2026), Maryland — contain no filibuster item.[^batteries]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 8% of Republicans (Monmouth University, 2022): Weak, the lowest we have measured. The opposite proposal fails too: keeping the filibuster "as is with no changes" drew 65% of Republicans and 19% of Democrats in the same survey.[^monmouth2022] Both directions fail on the same data; each is opposed by roughly nine in ten of one party.

- **Support flips with who holds power.** Same pollster, same question: 48% opposed under a Democratic majority, 44% under a Republican one (Rasmussen Reports, 2022, 2025).[^rasmussen2022][^rasmussen2025] The roll calls are cleaner: 48–52 in 2013 with Republicans as the 48, 48–52 in 2017 with Democrats as the 48.[^rc2013][^rc2017]
- **Symmetry fails as ordinarily framed.** Abolition and entrenchment each advantage the majority of the moment, and every recorded vote and reading rotates with control. The one design that does not favor the majority by construction — the inversion — has never been drafted or polled.
- **Staleness.** Every independent party breakdown dates from 2021–2022, under the opposite Senate configuration from today's.
- **No shared abuse.** In the same 2022 sample, 57% of Republicans supported using the filibuster to stop a specific pending bill and 72% of Democrats opposed that use.[^monmouth2022]
- **The existential-problem test is open.** One argument: a chamber that legislates only on 60 votes pushes lawmaking toward executive orders and budget procedures, and the cloture counts measure it.[^cloture] The other: the rule is the Senate's own under Article I, section 5, has been changed by simple majority twice in thirteen years, and governs which laws pass, not whether the structure functions. The polling cannot decide between them.
- **The why-an-amendment test cuts both ways.** A rule change stands only until the next majority reverses it, so an amendment is the only permanent instrument — for abolishing the filibuster and equally for entrenching it. But Article I, section 5 commits the question to each chamber; the text is not silent.

## Framings we tested

One framing clears. Restoring the "talking" filibuster drew 57% of Republicans and 54% of Democrats (Economist/YouGov, 2021): Moderate.[^yougov-mar2021] It does not resolve the category. It is not a threshold change; it puts a procedural cost on the objecting senator and leaves 60 votes intact. The Senate can adopt it by majority — and declined to in January 2022.[^rc2022] In the Constitution it would be floor mechanics, which our drafting rule sends to Congress.

Nothing else comes close. Making it harder to block by threat alone drew 35% of Republicans in support and 34% opposed, against 60% of Democrats in favor; a voting-rights exception drew 53% of Republicans opposed (Economist/YouGov, 2021).[^yougov-mar2021] Navigator's "loophole" item reached 51% of Republicans on impact, not support (2024).[^navigator2024] The inversion is unpolled.

## What would move it

In: a forward-looking question naming neither party and no pending bill, from an independent pollster with published crosstabs — for example, "Should the Constitution require that a bill can pass the Senate with a simple majority, no matter which party holds the majority — or should it allow the Senate to require more than a majority?" — with a parallel form testing the inversion. The bar: at least 45% in both parties on the same design, across two waves fielded under different Senate majorities; two waves is the only way to tell a preference from a regime effect. A rules change drawing majorities of both caucuses, which none of the three recorded appeals has done, would also count.

Out: a re-ask under the current Republican majority returning Democratic support for elimination above 60% and Republican support in the teens — 2021–22 with the parties exchanged. That would make regime-coding a measured fact and this a rejection on the symmetry test, with the polling floor as corroboration.
