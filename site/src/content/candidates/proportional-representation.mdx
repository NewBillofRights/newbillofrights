## The proposal

Proportional representation means electing several representatives from each district so that a party's share of seats tracks its share of votes, replacing winner-take-all single-member districts. The variants are multi-member districts with the single transferable vote, mixed-member systems that add list seats to district seats, and party-list systems. All differ from ranked-choice voting in single-member districts, which changes how one winner is chosen and produces no proportionality; nearly all American evidence concerns the second, and this page keeps the two apart. Multi-member congressional districts were lawful for most of American history; a 1967 statute, 2 U.S.C. § 2c, ended them.[^2c] The standing bill, the Fair Representation Act, would repeal that rule; its sponsors have all been Democrats, and no Republican has cosponsored it since 2017.[^fra] Our [redistricting amendment](/amendments/redistricting) covers the mid-decade re-draw ban only; no part of this idea lives there.

## The evidence

No independent national poll with a party breakdown asks about multi-member districts or proportional seat allocation. We searched Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, and the University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation and found none.

The only direct measurement is advocacy-commissioned. Verasight, for Fix Our House and New America (2026), found 47% support and 21% opposition for "proportional representation for U.S. Congressional elections," and 50% to 22% for seats "in proportion to their share of the overall vote"; roughly three in ten could not answer (no party breakdown published).[^verasight] The one partisan figure released is a share of a filtered subgroup — one presidential candidate's voters who expressed an opinion — not a floor.[^npr] Citizen Data for Protect Democracy, also advocacy-commissioned, put support at 24–27% cold and 52% or 61.5% after an explanation; 59% had never heard of the idea (no party breakdown published).[^pd]

The closest real-world evidence concerns ranked-choice voting in single-member districts, not proportional representation. On November 5, 2024, statewide adoption measures lost by wide margins in Idaho (30.4% yes), Oregon (42.3%), Colorado (46.5%), and Nevada (45.7%, reversing 53% approval in 2022);[^id-or-co][^nv] Missouri approved a constitutional prohibition (68.4%), the District of Columbia adopted it locally (72.9%),[^mo-dc] and Alaska kept its system by 743 votes.[^ak] Opposition across states tracked Republican presidential vote share (r=0.66).[^igpa] Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting, seventeen under single-party Republican control; in Ohio's 2026 roll calls, 63 of 65 House votes and 23 of 24 Senate votes in favor were Republican.[^bans] The one proportional result is local: Portland, Oregon, elected its 2024 council from four three-seat districts.[^portland]

Two independent proxies measure appetite for more parties, not the mechanism. Gallup (2023): 63% said a third major party is needed — 58% of Republicans, 75% of independents, 46% of Democrats.[^gallup] Pew Research Center (2023): "I often wish there were more parties to choose from" described 37% extremely or very well — 25% of Republicans and Republican leaners, 35% of Democrats and Democratic leaners.[^pew] The closest mechanism-adjacent item, a deliberative University of Maryland survey (2021), found 61% approving ranked-choice voting for federal elections — 49% of Republicans, 55% of independents, 73% of Democrats.[^umd]

## Where it stands

Verdict: Unpolled. No bipartisan floor can be computed. The nearest proxy floor is 46% of Democrats (Gallup, 2023); the nearest mechanism-adjacent floor is 49% of Republicans on ranked choice (Maryland, 2021). Which party would be the floor party on proportional representation itself is unknown.

- **Staleness: cleared.** Direct evidence is weeks old; the ballot record is 2024; legislation runs through March 2026.
- **Framing dependency: failing.** The same idea draws 24% cold, 47% by name, 50% by description, 61.5% after a metaphor, and 63% as a call for a third party.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap: wide.** 76% were concerned that a party winning 48% of a state's vote could hold 65% of its seats (Verasight, 2026, advocacy-commissioned, no party breakdown published);[^verasight] the remedy has cleared nothing.
- **Depth: untested.** No survey has named the costs — larger districts, weaker constituent ties, coalition government.
- **Regime coding: at risk.** Proportionality reduces the seat premium of whichever party the current map favors; Nevada's 53%-to-45.7% swing on one measure is what this test watches for, though the mechanism there was ranked choice.[^nv]
- **Symmetry: open.** Neutral by construction — each party gains where it is now shut out — but untested with voters, and the legislative record is one-sided in both directions.
- **Existential problem: arguably passes.** Single-member plurality districts are the precondition for gerrymandering, and 45% of adults identified as independents in 2025, a record (Gallup).[^gallup-ind]
- **Why-an-amendment: fails as things stand.** Congress imposed single-member districts by statute and can lift them the same way, which is what the standing bill proposes.[^2c] The argument for constitutional status is entrenchment — nineteen states have repealed ranked choice — and the counter is that district magnitude, thresholds, and transfer formulas are machinery of the kind our drafts send to Congress by law.

## Framings we tested

None clears the bar. By name, 47%; by description, 50%; multiple representatives for a larger area over one local representative, 61.1% (Verasight, 2026, advocacy-commissioned, no party breakdown published).[^verasight] After an explanation, 52% or 61.5%, from 24–27% cold (Protect Democracy, advocacy-commissioned, no party breakdown published) — the swing is the finding.[^pd] "A third major party is needed": 63%, floor 46% of Democrats (Gallup, 2023) — a diagnosis, not a remedy.[^gallup] "I often wish there were more parties": 37%, floor 25% of Republicans (Pew, 2023).[^pew] Ranked choice after briefing: 61%, floor 49% of Republicans (Maryland, 2021) — and ranked choice is not proportional representation.[^umd] Ranked choice at the ballot box: 30.4%–46.5% yes across four states, all defeated (2024).[^id-or-co][^nv] The framings with the best numbers are not what would be drafted.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll asking the mechanism in forward-looking, both-parties wording with published crosstabs — for example, "Would you favor or oppose electing House members from larger districts that each choose three to five members, so that a party winning 40% of the vote in an area wins about 40% of that area's seats?" — with at least 60% support in both parties, holding through a follow-up that names larger districts, more parties in Congress, and coalition bargaining. Statewide adoption of multi-member legislative districts by ballot measure, or a cross-party roll call on the Fair Representation Act, would also count. None of that answers the why-an-amendment test, which needs its own answer: why a rule Congress can enact by repealing one 1967 sentence belongs in the Constitution.

Out: an independent poll finding either party below 45% on the mechanism, or a costs-named version collapsing, would make this a rejection on the floor and depth tests; further statewide defeats would do the same on the ballot record. Independently of polling, a settled judgment that the why-an-amendment test has no answer would move the category to the statutory list.
