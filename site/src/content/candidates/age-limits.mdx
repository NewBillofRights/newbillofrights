## The proposal

The Constitution sets minimum ages for Congress — twenty-five for the House, thirty for the Senate — and no maximum; federal judges "hold their offices during good behaviour," with no terminal date.[^articles] An age-limit amendment would add the ceiling. Three variants circulate and are not interchangeable: a candidacy bar for Congress, under which no person who has reached a stated age may be elected to the House or Senate; a judicial retirement age, under which a federal judge leaves regular active service at a stated age, the common design in the states and abroad; and a presidential age cap, polled often but not part of this category as filed. Part of the judicial idea already lives in our [Term Limits amendment](/amendments/term-limits), which sets eighteen-year fixed terms for the Supreme Court and contemplates a retirement age for all Article III judges as an add-on. This page treats what remains: a retirement age beyond what a fixed term accomplishes, and the congressional half. A term limit is a count and can be made prospective at no cost. An age cap is a date, and any cap low enough to matter is, on its effective date, a list of sitting officeholders — so the drafting must decide whether to bar election or compel departure, whether incumbents are grandfathered, and whether the age is written into the text or delegated to Congress.

## The evidence

The two halves rest on different evidence, and the parties trade places between them. On Congress, the newest and best-documented reading told respondents there is a minimum age but no maximum and asked how much they would support "setting a maximum age limit for candidates to be eligible to run for the United States House of Representatives and Senate": 80% supported it — 83% of Republicans, 78% of Democrats, and 79% of independents. The question named no age (NPR/PBS News/Marist, 2026).[^marist]

On the judiciary, the best reading with a party split is older. A maximum age for Supreme Court justices drew 74% — 68% of Republicans and 82% of Democrats — in the same survey that put maximum age limits for federal elected officials at 79%, 82% of Republicans and 76% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2023).[^pew] Inside one sample, Republicans ran six points above Democrats on elected officials and fourteen below on justices.

<FullSpectrumChart proposal="Maximum age for Supreme Court justices — Pew Research Center, 2023" support={{ dem: 82, rep: 68 }} />

Other independent readings agree on the concept:

- Maximum age limits for elected officials, "not permitted to hold office any longer": 77% — 79% of Republicans, 76% of Democrats, 77% of independents. Asked what the limit should be, supporters split 45% for age 70, 22% for 60, and 18% for 80 (CBS News/YouGov, 2023).[^cbs]
- Upper age limits for president, senators, and House members: 67% — 72% of Republicans, 69% of Democrats; mandatory retirement for justices: 61% — 62% of Republicans, 67% of Democrats. The same sample found 61% agreeing older leaders "often have valuable experience" (Reuters/Ipsos, 2022).[^ipsos]
- With a prominent "not sure" option: 58% — 64% of Republicans, 57% of Democrats, 60% of independents (YouGov, 2022).[^yougov2022]
- A mandatory retirement age for justices: 81%, up from 71% in 2022 (Fox News, 2024; no party breakdown published).[^fox]

Voters have decided judicial age caps directly. Measures to raise or repeal a judicial retirement age failed in Hawaii in 2014 (22.03% yes, 72.78% no), Louisiana in 2014 (41.81% to 58.19%), Oregon in 2016 (36.95% to 63.05%), and Wyoming in 2022 (39.19% to 60.81%), each after a lopsided legislative referral; Pennsylvania's 2016 measure raising the age from 70 to 75 passed 51.09% to 48.91%.[^ballots] Thirty-one states and the District of Columbia set mandatory judicial retirement ages, most commonly 70.[^states]

## Where it stands

Both halves are Strong on the concept, and neither number is "the" floor for the category. The congressional floor is 78% of Democrats (Marist, 2026), corroborated at 76% (Pew, 2023; CBS News/YouGov, 2023) and 69% (Reuters/Ipsos, 2022). The judicial floor is 68% of Republicans (Pew, 2023), corroborated at 62% (Reuters/Ipsos, 2022).

Cleared: high topline, low floor — the widest party gap in the record is fourteen points, and the congressional gap is five to six across three pollsters. Abuse-vs-remedy — 78% agree officials over 75 raise concerns about their ability to perform the job, and 77% back the remedy in the same sample (CBS News/YouGov, 2023).[^cbs] Symmetry — a date applies identically to every officeholder of both parties. Why-an-amendment, decisively — the Supreme Court has held that each House may judge only the qualifications "expressly prescribed in the Constitution,"[^powell] and that neither Congress nor a state may add congressional qualifications by legislation; the change must come through Article V.[^thornton] For judges, Article III's tenure guarantee is the obstacle: state caps have survived federal age-discrimination and equal-protection challenges,[^gregory] but a federal cap on federal judges requires an amendment.

Open or at risk: depth. Every party breakdown in the record is on the concept of a cap; no poll has asked favor or oppose on a named age with a party split, and the two surveys that asked supporters for a number found no majority for any age — 45% for 70 (CBS News/YouGov, 2023), 39% for 70 (YouGov, 2022).[^cbs][^yougov2022] Regime coding is live in the category: on a maximum age to serve as president, Republicans were the most supportive group in 2022–23 and the least in 2025 — 64% of Republicans, 88% of Democrats — and on a cutoff of 79 or younger, 34% of Republicans and 75% of Democrats (YouGov, 2025).[^yougov2025] The congressional item has been the most stable — 79% of Republicans and 76% of Democrats in 2023, 83% and 78% in 2026 — but a cap's bite depends on who is currently old. Staleness is at risk on the judicial half, whose party splits date from 2022–2023. The existential-problem test is open, and binding for the congressional half: life tenure removes the electoral remedy for judges, while voters hold the remedy for Congress every two or six years, and an age is a proxy for capacity rather than a measure of it.

## Framings we tested

Every framing of the concept clears the bar, including the formats designed to depress it: candidates to run for House and Senate, 78% of Democrats and 83% of Republicans (Marist, 2026); federal elected officials, 76% and 82% (Pew, 2023); officials "not permitted to hold office any longer," 76% and 79% (CBS News/YouGov, 2023); asked alongside the experience counter-argument, 69% and 72% (Reuters/Ipsos, 2022); with a "not sure" option, 57% and 64% (YouGov, 2022), the one reading that is Moderate rather than Strong. On the judiciary: Supreme Court justices, 68% of Republicans and 82% of Democrats (Pew, 2023); mandatory retirement for justices, 62% and 67% (Reuters/Ipsos, 2022).

No framing with a number in it clears, because none has been measured as a proposal. "The maximum age should be 70" is the choice of 45% of supporters — 52% of Republicans, 45% of Democrats (CBS News/YouGov, 2023) — roughly 35% of all adults. A presidential cutoff of 79 or younger draws 34% of Republicans and 75% of Democrats (YouGov, 2025), the widest gap in the file. The concept is not the amendment: an amendment must state an age or delegate one, and the consensus stops being measurable at that point.

## What would move it

In: an independent poll with published party crosstabs asking a forward-looking favor-or-oppose on the operative clause with the age in it — for example, "no person who has reached the age of 75 may be elected to the House or Senate; the rule applies to every future candidate of both parties, and no one now in office would be removed before the end of their term" — clearing 60% in both parties, repeated at 70 and 80, with the counter-argument ("voters should be free to elect anyone they judge capable, regardless of age") tested in the same sample. A cross-party roll call on an age-limit joint resolution, or a state measure imposing rather than loosening a cap, would also count.

Out: if the named-age wording drops either party below 45%, the congressional half fails the depth test — Strong in the abstract, collapsing when the cost is specified. If leader age is judged a preference about who governs rather than a threat to the country's future, the congressional half fails the existential-problem test regardless of its polling. The judicial half is measured on its own evidence, and its overlap with the [Term Limits amendment](/amendments/term-limits) stands either way.
