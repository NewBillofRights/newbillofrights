## The proposal

The idea as commonly framed would insulate federal criminal law enforcement from presidential direction. It takes three forms: an independent constitutional status for the Department of Justice, so that no president may remove its top officers without cause or Senate concurrence; removal protection only, a for-cause standard for the Attorney General, the FBI Director, special counsels, and inspectors general, with dismissal reviewable by a panel of federal judges; or a conduct rule barring any president from directing an investigation or prosecution of a named person for a purpose unrelated to law enforcement. Nothing in our slate reaches prosecutorial direction: [executive compliance](/amendments/executive-compliance) binds the executive to court orders, and [pardon reform](/amendments/pardon-reform) governs a prosecution after the fact.

## The evidence

Only one structural mechanism has been polled with a party breakdown. Asked in January 2018 whether to require "the approval of a panel of federal judges before any special counsel could be fired by the president or attorney general," 62% of Americans supported it and 29% opposed — 44% of Republicans (47% opposed), 65% of independents, and 76% of Democrats (Monmouth University Poll, 2018).[^monmouth-jan-2018]

Two readings complete the national record on the remedy, both also from 2018. Letting a fired special counsel appeal to a panel of federal judges after the fact drew 57%: 39% of Republicans (43% opposed), 58% of independents, and 71% of Democrats (Monmouth University Poll, 2018).[^monmouth-apr-2018] Legislation to protect the special counsel from being fired drew 52%: 33% of Republicans and 75% of Democrats (CBS News/SSRS, 2018).[^cbs-2018] No independent national poll since 2018 has asked whether the Attorney General should be independent of the President, whether the FBI Director should be removable only for cause, or whether prosecutors should be protected from removal; we searched Pew, Gallup, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Fox, Monmouth, Quinnipiac, PRRI, and the University of Maryland's deliberative program.

The diagnosis is polled far more often. In May 2026, 55% of Americans said the Justice Department has filed unjustified criminal cases against the President's political opponents — 53% of Republicans, 55% of independents, and 58% of Democrats, "little difference in partisan perception" in the pollster's words (Marquette Law School Poll, 2026).[^marquette-2026] The same survey asked about the 2022–24 prosecutions of the sitting president: 77% of Republicans called them unjustified and 86% of Democrats called them justified.[^marquette-2026] Wording that named the sitting president as the actor, fielded the previous autumn, found 52% unjustified — 6% of Republicans, 58% of independents, and 92% of Democrats (Quinnipiac University, 2025).[^quinnipiac-2025] Advocacy-commissioned polling: 60% disagreed that the President "should be able to direct the Justice Department to pursue charges against his political opponents" — 36% of Republicans (30% agreed), 53% of independents, and 86% of Democrats (States United Democracy Center/YouGov, 2026).[^states-united-2026]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 39–44% of Republicans on judicial review of a special counsel's removal (Monmouth, 2018): Weak, on eight-year-old data. Stated plainly, the remedy is unpolled since 2018. The polling tests it is at risk on:

- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap.** 53% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats agree unjustified cases have been filed (Marquette, 2026); the three polls that named a preventive mechanism found 44%, 39%, and 33% of Republicans (2018).
- **Support flips with who holds power.** Favorable views of the Department rose 18 points in a year among Republicans and Republican leaners, to 51%, and fell 27 among Democrats and Democratic leaners, to 28%, with no change to its structure (Pew Research Center, 2025);[^pew-2025] inside one Marquette instrument, the parties split 77/86 on the prior prosecutions and sit within five points on the current ones.
- **Framing dependency.** Two independent pollsters fielded the same diagnosis in the same season and differed by roughly 47 points among Republicans (Marquette, 53%; Quinnipiac, 6%).
- **Staleness.** All remedy data is from 2018.
- **High topline, low floor.** 60% overall conceals 36% of Republicans (States United, 2026).
- **Depth.** No poll has asked whether an insulated Department is acceptable when a cost is named.

The non-polling tests read differently. The existential-problem test is met: the record contains prosecutions that each party's identifiers call unjustified when aimed at their own side (Marquette, 2025 and 2026).[^marquette-2026] The symmetry test is met by construction — a for-cause standard binds every future president identically. The why-an-amendment test is met decisively. In 2024 the Supreme Court held that investigative and prosecutorial decisionmaking is "the special province of the Executive Branch," within an authority that "Congress cannot act on, and courts cannot examine."[^immunity-2024] In June 2026 it held a statutory for-cause removal provision "contrary to the separation of powers" and overruled the 1935 precedent that had sustained such protections.[^slaughter-2026] A 1926 holding bars Congress from conditioning removal on Senate consent, the mechanism in the first variant.[^myers-1926] The contrary authority, a 1988 decision upholding for-cause removal of an independent counsel 7–1, rests on a statute that lapsed in 1999.[^morrison-1988] The constitutional case is the strongest part of the file; the evidence case is the thinnest.

## Framings we tested

Two framings clear the bar, and neither is a remedy: the diagnosis (53% R / 58% D, Marquette, 2026), and an adjacent question on whether statutes may set removal requirements at multimember agencies — 73%, with 54% of Republicans, 72% of independents, and 93% of Democrats (Marquette, 2026) — which concerns regulatory commissioners, not prosecutors.[^marquette-2026] The three remedy framings — judicial approval before firing (44% R), appeal after firing (39% R), and legislation (33% R), all 2018 — fall short, and softening the mechanism lowered the Republican floor rather than raising it. The advocacy questions measure rejection of a stated power, not support for a rule; a second one found 37% of Republicans and under 10% of Democrats agreeing the President should have authority to direct the Department to investigate political opponents (Yale Youth Poll, 2026).[^yale-2026] Two findings follow. The abuse framing and the remedy framing are not one proposal in different words: when the mechanism is named, Republican support sits at 39–44%. And the diagnosis is not stable enough to build on: naming the President rather than the Department as the actor moves Republicans from 53% to 6%. The one question a drafting decision would need — whether the Attorney General and FBI Director should be removable only for cause, asked about any future president — has never been asked.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll with 2026-or-later fieldwork asking, in forward-looking wording, "Should any future president be able to remove the Attorney General or FBI Director only for cause — such as misconduct or neglect of duty — rather than at will?" and, separately, "Should any future president be prohibited from directing the Justice Department to investigate or prosecute a specific person for reasons unrelated to law enforcement?" — each with an actor-named control arm and a costs-named follow-up. The bar: at least 60% of Republicans on the conduct rule and 55% on removal, holding through the follow-up. A state constitutional amendment insulating a state attorney general adopted by a cross-party ballot majority, or a federal roll call carrying majorities of both parties, would corroborate.

Out: if that poll reproduces the 2018 pattern — Republican support for the removal remedy in the 30s or low 40s while the diagnosis polls in the 50s — the category fails the abuse-vs-remedy and framing-dependency tests on fresh data. If forward-looking wording drops both parties below the incumbent-named readings, it fails as regime-coded.
