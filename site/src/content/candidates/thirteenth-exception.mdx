## The proposal

The Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." The proposal strikes that clause, leaving an unqualified prohibition — the narrowest edit in our pipeline, creating no new power and no new institution. Two variants exist. The bare deletion, the approach of the federal Abolition Amendment, removes the exception and stops.[^abolition-text] The second adds a savings clause for prison work, on Tennessee's ratified model: "Nothing in this section shall prohibit an inmate from working when the inmate has been duly convicted of a crime."[^tn-text] No target amendment touches this ground.

## The evidence

One independent national poll carries a party breakdown, and it asks a proxy question. Asked how they would vote on a measure in their own state that "ensures the state constitution doesn't legalize slavery and involuntary servitude as punishments for crime," 73% of Americans were in support — 66% of Republicans, 69% of independents, and 85% of Democrats — with 10% opposed and 17% not sure (YouGov, 2024). Republican opposition was 11%; the shortfall against Democrats is uncertainty (23% not sure), not opposition.[^yougov] No independent national poll has asked about amending the federal Thirteenth Amendment.

Eight states have removed their own version at the ballot, most by wide margins: Vermont 88.71% (2022),[^vt] Utah 80.48% (2020),[^ut] Tennessee 79.53% (2022, with the work savings sentence),[^tn] Nebraska 68.23% (2020),[^ne] Colorado 66.21% (2018),[^co] Nevada 60.60% (2024),[^nv] and Oregon 55.59% (2022).[^or] Alabama's 76.49% (2022) was a vote on a wholesale recompiled constitution — bundled evidence, not a single-issue result.[^al] Utah, Tennessee, Nebraska, Nevada, and Alabama voted Republican for president in 2024; Vermont, Colorado, and Oregon voted Democratic.[^fec] Every measure was legislatively referred; in Utah, Tennessee, Nebraska, and Alabama that required Republican legislative majorities.

Three measures failed. California Proposition 6 (2024), whose ballot label said "involuntary servitude" without the word "slavery," lost 46.66% to 53.34%.[^ca] Louisiana Amendment 7 (2022) lost 39.15% to 60.85% on text that kept the exception for "the otherwise lawful administration of criminal justice"; its own sponsor urged a no vote.[^la] Colorado Amendment T (2016), identical in substance to the measure that passed two years later, lost 49.68% to 50.32%.[^co]

The federal record runs the other way. The Abolition Amendment has never received a committee or floor vote. Its House resolution drew 11 Republican cosponsors of 193 in the 117th Congress[^hjres53] and 5 of 129 in the 118th;[^hjres72] the Senate companions drew 14 cosponsors each — 13 Democrats and 1 independent, no Republicans.[^sjres21][^sjres33]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 66% of Republicans (YouGov, 2024): Strong — on a state-measure proxy. The 19-point party gap is among the narrowest we have measured, so high-topline / low-floor is cleared. Regime coding is cleared: the clause binds no officeholder and confers no partisan advantage. The abuse-vs-remedy gap is cleared: the remedy is a deletion, with no machinery to poll separately. Symmetry passes by construction. The why-an-amendment test is the strongest in the pipeline: the exception is constitutional text, and every state that removed its version had to amend its constitution.

One test is at risk and two are open. Framing dependency is at risk, and the record documents it: identical substance swung sixteen points in Colorado between 2016 and 2018,[^co] and on the same night in 2024, Nevada's "slavery"-worded question passed at 60.60% while California's "involuntary servitude" label lost at 46.66%.[^nv][^ca] Depth is untested: no pollster has asked a version naming corrections costs, ended work programs, or wages for incarcerated workers, and only California faced sustained public argument. The existential-problem test is open, and decisive. On one reading, an explicit constitutional authorization for slavery as punishment is a live carve-out in the founding charter. On the other, the change is substantially declaratory: work programs continue under both variants, and the operative fights — wages, conditions, discipline — are statutory and would remain so. A February 2026 Colorado ruling that the state's corrections department violates the amended state constitution by compelling work under threat of punishment cuts both ways: the deletion has operational bite, delivered through ordinary litigation; the order barred discipline for work refusal and left work programs standing.[^cbs] Staleness is mild: October 2024 fieldwork, before that ruling.

## Framings we tested

- **"Slavery" in the question.** Nevada, 60.60% (2024);[^nv] the YouGov national item, which also uses the word, 66% of Republicans (2024).[^yougov] This is the framing that travels.
- **"Involuntary servitude" without "slavery."** California, 46.66%, defeated (2024),[^ca] after pre-election readings of 41% (Public Policy Institute of California, 2024; no party breakdown published).[^kqed] Same substance, same day, roughly fourteen points apart.
- **Deletion plus a work-programs savings clause.** Tennessee, 79.53% (2022) — the highest single-issue result in a Republican-voting state. The clause did not depress the number.[^tn]
- **Carve-out-preserving text.** Louisiana, 39.15%, defeated (2022).[^la]
- **Bundled into a broader cleanup.** Alabama, 76.49% (2022) — not attributable to this clause.[^al]
- **The federal ask.** Never polled; the only signal is the cosponsor pattern above.

One framing clears the bar honestly: the word "slavery" plus a work-programs savings clause, at a 66% Republican floor and 79.53% in Tennessee. It is not a different proposal wearing the name; it is the second variant above. What it does not clear is the existential-problem test.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll with party crosstabs on the federal mechanism — approximately, "The U.S. Constitution's Thirteenth Amendment bans slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for a crime. Would you support or oppose amending the Constitution to remove that exception, while allowing prisons to continue operating voluntary or paid work programs?" — clearing 60% of Republicans, split-sampled against a version without the savings clause and a version naming costs. Also: a state passing a "slavery"-worded measure with a savings clause after a contested campaign with organized, funded opposition, which no passing measure has yet faced.

Out: a costs-named or contested-campaign version below 45% of Republicans, failing the depth test; or a settled judgment that the change is substantially declaratory, with the operative fights remaining statutory, failing the existential-problem test. A ballot defeat alone would not be decisive: California's is already in the record and is attributable to wording.
