## The proposal

The idea is to put in the Constitution what the Pendleton Act of 1883 and the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 put in statute: federal jobs below the political-appointee layer are obtained on merit and cannot be taken away for political reasons.[^pendleton] Three variants travel under the name and poll differently enough that treating them as one is the category's central measurement problem: the merit-entry principle (hiring and promotion on merit, not political belief); tenure protection (career employees may not be dismissed for political reasons); and for-cause protection for agency heads, evaluated under the [independent-agency candidate](/evidence/candidates/fcc-independence). The live dispute is a 2026 rule that moves roughly 50,000 policy-influencing career positions to at-will status while stating they "will remain career jobs filled on a nonpartisan basis."[^rule] The rule claims the first variant while changing the second, so a poll asking only about merit hiring cannot tell its supporters from its opponents.

## The evidence

One series carries nearly the whole record: the Partnership for Public Service's annual surveys, fielded by Impact Research on nationally representative samples. In spring 2024, "civil servants should be hired and promoted based on merit rather than their political beliefs" drew 95% of Republicans, 96% of Democrats, and 94% of independents, and "presidents should be able to fire any civil servants that they choose for any reason" drew agreement from 37% of Republicans and 11% of Democrats (Partnership for Public Service / Impact Research, 2024).[^pps2024]

The chart shows the merit-entry principle, which the disputed rule itself claims to honor. The contested item moved. "A nonpartisan civil service is important for having a strong American democracy" stood at 87% in 2024, with Republicans and Democrats at approximately the same rate; in 2025 it fell to 66% of Republicans and 81% of Democrats; in 2026 Republicans recovered to 78% (Partnership for Public Service / Impact Research, 2024–2026; the 2026 Democratic figure is not published).[^pps2025][^pps2026] The mirror items moved with it: Republican agreement that presidents may fire any civil servant for any reason rose from 37% to 60% between 2024 and 2025, and Republican opposition to presidents filling federal jobs with policy allies fell from 65% to 26%.[^pps2025]

The rest is proxy evidence. Extending the congressional stock-trading ban to all federal employees drew 40% — 42% of Republicans, 37% of Democrats, 42% of independents (University of Maryland Program for Public Consultation, 2023) — the only mechanism-level reading in the record, and a different mechanism.[^ppc] About one quarter of Americans favored eliminating a large number of federal jobs, Republicans likelier than Democrats or independents (AP-NORC, 2025; no party percentages published) — the cuts, not the protections.[^apnorc] The one recorded federal vote, restoring collective bargaining, passed the House 231–195 in December 2025, Republicans 20–195 and Democrats 211–0 — adjacent, not a vote on merit protection.[^roll332] A March 2025 Partnership survey found 79% of Republicans for that year's workforce changes and 87% of Democrats against, but on a quota sample the Partnership itself calls not nationally representative.[^pps-quota] Nothing in the record asks whether merit or tenure protections should be constitutional.

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor depends on which sentence is measured. On merit hiring it is 94% (Partnership / Impact Research, 2024): Strong, but not the contested question. On the nonpartisan-civil-service item it is 66% of Republicans (2025): Strong by the letter, having been about 87% a year earlier and 78% a year later. On the operative remedy — a constitutional bar on politically motivated removal — the category is Unpolled.

- **Support moves with who holds power.** The Republican line went 87 → 66 → 78 in 24 months while the presidency did not change hands. The Democratic line moved the same direction (88 → 81), evidence of a swing in attention rather than a durable partisan divide.[^pps2025][^pps2026] A floor measured in 2025 or 2026 reads the current alignment.
- **Framing dependency.** The framing that clears the bar is not what would be drafted; what would be drafted is unmeasured.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap.** The parties disagree about whether an abuse occurred (79% of Republicans for the 2025 changes, 87% of Democrats against, in the flagged sample).[^pps-quota]
- **Depth.** Untested: no survey names the cost that a constitutional protection makes a poor performer harder to remove, and the 40% reading suggests the public is receptive to that argument.[^ppc]
- **Existential problem.** Open. For: tenure protection is what lets an official refuse an unlawful order. Against: the dispute covers roughly 50,000 of about two million positions, and the rule preserves merit hiring by its terms.[^rule]
- **Symmetry.** A merit clause binds every future president identically; no matched pairing of both sides' abuses has been identified.
- **Why an amendment.** At risk. The Court's removal cases, from 1926 through a 2025 stay order, concern officers, not career employees, and the Court has said in terms that nothing in them casts doubt on the civil service system.[^fef] Congress bound the executive by statute in 1883 and 1978, and the current rule rests on a statutory exception Congress wrote. The First Amendment already bars dismissing non-policymaking employees for party affiliation,[^elrod] which sharpens what an amendment would add: the policy-influencing tier those cases leave exposed.

## Framings we tested

Two framings clear the bar honestly: merit hiring (94% floor) and "civil servants should serve the people more than any individual president" (90% of Republicans, 91% of Democrats, 83% of independents; Partnership / Impact Research, 2024).[^pps2024] The first is not the contested rule; the second is a duty statement, not an enforceable protection. Neither resolves the category. The nonpartisan-civil-service item clears nominally, and the swing is the finding. The mirror item reached 60% Republican agreement in 2025, and the patronage item is where the collapse was deepest (Republican opposition 65% → 26%).[^pps2025] The stock-trading extension (40%) and the collective-bargaining roll call (Republicans 20–195) do not clear.[^ppc][^roll332] A constitutional merit-and-tenure clause has never been asked.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll with 2027-or-later fieldwork asking the operative clause in forward-looking, both-parties wording — career federal employees may be hired, promoted, and dismissed only on merit and conduct, never for political belief or party, binding every future president of either party — clearing 60% in both parties on the base question and on a costs-named follow-up ("even if this makes it harder for a president to remove career officials who are performing poorly or resisting lawful policy"), with less than a 10-point swing on a symmetry probe, and holding across a change of administration. A second route: majorities of both caucuses for a statutory merit-protection bill, which the December 2025 roll call is far short of.[^roll332]

Out: a repeat collapse in the next wave — Republicans back under 60% on the nonpartisan-civil-service item, or the fire-for-any-reason item above 55% in either party — would fail the test of support that moves with who holds power; a costs-named poll near the 40% band would fail the depth test; and the why-an-amendment test applied as written — the Court's civil-service carve-out plus the 1883 and 1978 statutes as a complete statutory answer — would move it to the statutory list.
