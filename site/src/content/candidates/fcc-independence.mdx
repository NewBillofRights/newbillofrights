## The proposal

The category carries the FCC's name, but the Federal Communications Commission is the example, not the proposal. The idea as commonly framed is general: an amendment to Article II stating that Congress may create offices — members of multimember regulatory commissions, inspectors general — whose holders the President cannot remove without cause. Variants differ in reach: authorization only (Congress *may* set removal conditions), entrenchment (the amendment itself fixes terms and a for-cause standard), and a process check (Senate concurrence or a supermajority before removal), which no national poll found has asked. Pulling or threatening broadcast licenses over coverage is a First Amendment question, not a removal question; no variant would touch a licensing action. Our [executive-compliance amendment](/amendments/executive-compliance) already binds the president to obey a court order reinstating a removed officer, but says nothing about whether the removal was lawful. Whether Congress may insulate an office at all is the remainder this page measures.

## The evidence

The newest mechanism-specific reading follows the Supreme Court's June 2026 decision. Told that the Court had "overruled a 1935 precedent and held that federal laws barring the president from removing members of federal regulatory agencies, like the Federal Trade Commission, except for cause, violate the constitutional separation of powers," 49% of adults favored the decision and 51% opposed it — favor 58% of Republicans, 50% of independents, 38% of Democrats. Support for insulation, read as opposing the decision, was therefore 42% of Republicans, 50% of independents, and 61% of Democrats (Marquette Law School Poll, July 2026).[^mlsp-july]

Nine weeks earlier, before the ruling, the same pollster asked prospectively whether statutes may limit removal from independent, multimember regulatory agencies to "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office": 73% said they may — 54% of Republicans, 72% of independents, 93% of Democrats (Marquette, May 2026).[^mlsp-may] The identical wording in November 2025 drew 71% (Marquette; no party breakdown published in the release).[^mlsp-nov] Only 20% had heard "a lot" about the June decision and 37% nothing at all.[^mlsp-july]

- **The Federal Reserve companion.** In the same July 2026 survey, 70% favored the Court's refusal to lift a lower-court order preventing removal of a Federal Reserve governor — 51% of Republicans, 67% of independents, 91% of Democrats (Marquette, 2026).[^mlsp-fed] In May, 60% of Republicans said the president may remove Fed governors (Marquette, 2026).[^mlsp-may]
- **The speech abuse, a proxy.** 68% called it unacceptable "for the government to pressure broadcasters to remove shows that include speech it disagrees with" — 42% of Republicans, 74% of independents, 88% of Democrats (The Economist/YouGov, 2025).[^yougov] This measures the abuse, not the remedy.
- **The legal record.** On June 29, 2026, the Court held 6–3 that the FTC's for-cause removal provision is "contrary to the separation of powers" and overruled *Humphrey's Executor* (1935), while reserving entities that exercise no executive power and naming the Federal Reserve as one example.[^slaughter][^humphreys] The same day, a different five-justice majority declined to lift the order protecting the Fed governor on statutory and due-process grounds — a stay ruling, not a holding that the Constitution protects the Fed.[^cook]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 42% of Republicans (Marquette, July 2026): Weak. The prior reading of the same subject, in prospective wording, was 54% (Marquette, May 2026): Moderate. Within one quarter the floor has crossed the Weak/Moderate line; neither reading approaches Strong. The Fed-only variant reads 51% of Republicans: Moderate.

- **Support flips with who holds power — the controlling open test.** Inside one pollster's series, Republican support moved from 54% to 42% and Democratic support from 93% to 61% in nine weeks, in opposite directions, as the case resolved. Some of that is wording — endorsing a decision already made is not the same act as predicting a ruling — but each party's direction tracks who won.
- **High topline, low floor — applies.** May's 73% concealed a 39-point party gap; July's 49/51 conceals a 20-point gap the other way.
- **Framing dependency — applies.** Congress's authority to set removal requirements draws 54–55% of Republicans; approval of the decision draws 42%.
- **Abuse-vs-remedy gap — at risk.** 68% find pressuring broadcasters unacceptable; the structural remedy sits roughly 25 points lower among Republicans, and no clause bridges the two.
- **Depth — untested.** No survey has named the costs of insulation or of at-will removal. **Staleness — cleared:** the controlling data are eight weeks old.
- **The existential-problem test — open.** For: agencies administering elections, broadcast licensing, securities, and monetary policy now serve at will. Against: peer democracies solve this design question many ways, and Congress keeps appropriations, oversight, and statute — 55% of Republicans and 63% of Democrats say Congress can take back delegated power (Marquette, July 2026).[^mlsp-general]
- **The symmetry test — passes by construction.** A rule about who may be removed binds every future president, and both parties' removals are in the record: the 2026 removals at the FTC and the Fed,[^slaughter][^cook] and, in the case that founded the doctrine, a Democratic president's 1935 removal of a Republican-appointed FTC commissioner.[^humphreys]
- **The why-an-amendment test — passes, as of June 29, 2026.** Before the decision a statute could do this job; after it, re-enacting the FTC standard would re-enact a provision the Court has held unconstitutional.

## Framings we tested

None clears the bar honestly. All are Marquette Law School Poll, July 2026, unless noted.[^mlsp-general]

- The president may not remove a Federal Reserve governor: 51% of Republicans, 91% of Democrats — Moderate, the highest Republican reading of any insulation item, and it describes the one institution the Court has already carved out.
- The next president should be less able to make policy without congressional authorization: 55% of Republicans, 91% of Democrats — Moderate, and a different proposal, about executive action without statutory authority rather than protection of an officer.
- The next president should have less control over federal departments and agencies: 20% of Republicans, 72% of Democrats — Weak; 55% of Republicans choose "about as much as today."
- Congress has given up too many powers to the president: 52% of Republicans, 85% of Democrats — Moderate, a diagnosis rather than a remedy.
- Government pressure on broadcasters is unacceptable: 42% of Republicans, 88% of Democrats (The Economist/YouGov, 2025) — Weak on the party split despite a 68% topline.

The category's most popular fact — broad discomfort with using a regulator against critics — is not evidence for the category's actual proposal.

## What would move it

In: one independent national poll, fielded no earlier than 2027 so it is not read in the immediate wake of the ruling, asking the remedy in forward-looking, both-parties, mechanism-specific wording — for example, "Should the Constitution be amended to say that Congress may set fixed terms for members of federal regulatory commissions and may require a stated cause before any president removes them — a rule applying to every future president of either party?" — with a Republican floor at or above 60%, holding in at least the Moderate band under a costs-named follow-up. A cross-party roll call would also count: a bill restoring for-cause protection, or an amendment authorizing it, drawing majorities of both House caucuses.

Out: a second independent reading with the Republican floor at or below 42% in prospective wording — low support even when the question concerns future presidents rather than a decision one side just won — would fail the category on regime coding and high-topline/low-floor. If a re-poll lifts the floor only on the Federal Reserve question, the finding is a consensus about central banking, not about agency independence, and the category would close with a note pointing to that narrower result.
