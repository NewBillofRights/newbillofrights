## The proposal

The Constitution caps district size at one representative per 30,000 people and guarantees each state one seat; the rest is statute. The House grew after nearly every census until 1911, failed to reapportion after the 1920 census, and was fixed at 435 by the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929.[^cap] Each representative now stands for 761,169 people.[^census] The amendment as commonly framed would entrench a formula: a fixed ratio (roughly one per 300,000 in one version); the Wyoming Rule, sizing the average district to the least-populous state (about 574 seats on 2020 data); or the cube-root rule (about 692 seats).[^amacad5] A fourth variant is a pedigree argument — "Article the First," the unratified first of the twelve articles sent to the states in 1789, still pending, whose formula read literally today would require more than 6,000 representatives.[^article-first] The one bill now before Congress on it would set a December 31, 2026 ratification deadline, extinguishing rather than reviving it.[^hjres186] The leading institutional proposal — 150 more seats, a House of 585 — is statutory: "a vote of Congress is all that is needed."[^amacad-init] No target amendment covers chamber size.

## The evidence

One national poll in the modern record asks about House size with a party breakdown. Asked in July 2023 whether the House should be increased, decreased, or kept the same, 29% of Americans chose increase, 53% keep the same, and 15% decrease — 23% of Republicans and Republican leaners and 35% of Democrats and Democratic leaners favored an increase, and "larger shares in both parties favor keeping the House at its current size" (Pew Research Center, 2023).[^pew2023] The same survey found 87% for congressional term limits; the House-size number is not general reluctance to change institutions.[^pew2023]

Five years earlier the same question drew 28% — 21% of Republicans and 34% of Democrats (Pew Research Center, 2018). Half the sample was told the first Congress had 65 members representing roughly 60,000 people each, against 435 representing roughly 700,000 now. Support rose to 34%, one-sidedly: Democrats rose to 44%; Pew reported no differences among Republicans.[^pew2018] The strongest argument for expansion, delivered directly, moved one party and not the other.

The newest number is advocacy-commissioned: 54% support and 27% opposition for "expanding the House of Representatives" with 150 new members (Verasight for Fix Our House and New America, both advocates of the reform, 2026; no party breakdown published).[^verasight] It is a two-way question with no "keep it the same" option, so it is not comparable with Pew's three-way form; part of the gap between 29% and 54% is likely format rather than movement. There is no roll-call vote and no ballot measure; the last statutory enlargement bill, for a House of 585, died in committee without a vote in the 118th Congress.[^real-house] No independent-tier pollster has asked since 2023, and a June 2026 battery of eight amendment ideas omitted it.[^cato-battery]

## Where it stands

The bipartisan floor is 23% of Republicans (Pew, 2023): Weak. The Democratic figure — 35% in 2023, 34% to 44% in 2018 — is itself below the Moderate band. Neither party has recorded majority support in an independent poll, and a majority of both chose "keep it the same"; every category we have rejected on polling had one party above 60%.

- **High topline, low floor — does not apply.** There is no high topline hiding a split; the 29% overall is the finding.
- **Staleness — at risk.** The only party-split data are from 2023 and 2018; the one later reading (54%, 2026) is advocacy-commissioned and cannot show whether opinion has moved.
- **Framing dependency — unresolved.** The gap between Pew's three-way form and Verasight's two-way form is confounded with sponsorship, format, and question content.
- **Depth — never tested.** No survey has named the costs: more members, salaries, and offices.
- **Support flips with who holds power — cleared.** Nothing ties either party's position to who controls the chamber.
- **Symmetry — passes.** The American Academy of Arts and Sciences, which advocates enlargement and publishes its method, ran 10,000 simulations at each House size from 435 to 700 seats: Republicans gain roughly 2–3 points in probability of control between 436 and 524 seats, Democrats' odds rise above 525 seats but "never by more than roughly 2.7 percent," and the Electoral College shows "no partisan impact."[^amacad6] A rule whose partisan effect is small and points both ways depending on where the number lands is close to one written behind the veil.
- **Existential problem — open.** The ratio change is documented — roughly 60,000 constituents per member in the first Congress, 761,169 today[^pew2018][^census] — and the United States has one of the widest gaps between chamber size and the cube root of population among democracies.[^amacad5] Whether that threatens the country's future is unsettled; 53% "keep it the same" (Pew, 2023) is not the answer of a public that experiences it as a crisis.
- **Why an amendment — weak.** House size has been statutory since 1911 and 1929, Congress can enlarge the House by ordinary legislation, and the leading institutional proposal asks it to. No court has struck an enlargement statute; Article I fixes the ceiling and the one-per-state floor and leaves the rest to Congress. Amendments lock in agreement the country has already reached, and here the statute has never been passed.

## Framings we tested

Only two framings have been measured by an independent pollster, and they are the same question five years apart: 23% of Republicans and 35% of Democrats (Pew, 2023); 21% and 34% (Pew, 2018). Founding-era context moved Democrats from 34% to 44% and Republicans not at all (Pew, 2018). The one majority result — 54% for adding 150 members (Verasight for Fix Our House and New America, 2026; advocacy-commissioned; no party breakdown) — publishes no floor. The Wyoming Rule, the cube-root rule, a constitutional floor or formula, and "Article the First" have never been polled by name or description. None clears the bar; the named ones have not been asked. One distinction matters for any poll: a constitutional ratio, Article the First's approach, produces a chamber of thousands and is a different proposal from a floor or formula. The formula, not the slogan, is what would need testing.

## What would move it

In: an independent national poll with published party crosstabs (Pew, AP-NORC, Marquette, Economist/YouGov, Monmouth, or the University of Maryland's deliberative format) that tests the mechanism, not the direction — for example: "The number of House seats has been fixed at 435 by law since 1929, while the average district has grown to about 760,000 people. Would you favor or oppose setting the size of the House by a fixed formula tied to population — for instance, so that the average district is about the size of the smallest state — which would add roughly 140 seats?" — with a follow-up naming the added members, salaries, and offices. A Republican floor of 45% would move the category from Weak to Moderate; 60% in both parties would clear the bar. Passage of a statutory enlargement, or a serious cross-party vote on one, would supply the roll-call evidence the record lacks and answer the why-an-amendment test at once.

Out: a well-formed independent poll of the Wyoming-Rule or cube-root mechanism returning a Republican floor still below 45% would make this a rejection on the bipartisan floor — the test that rejected the Electoral College and the self-pardon ban. Separately, if we conclude that a number Congress can change by majority vote does not belong in the Constitution before the statute has been tried, the category moves to the statutory list, not to a target.
